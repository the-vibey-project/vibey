## PHASE 1 — PROBLEM IDENTIFICATION

### What Phase 1 Actually Is

Phase 1 is not the beginning of writing. It is the beginning of
thinking — the systematic process of converting a frustration, a
question, an observation, or a gap into an intellectual claim precise
enough to be argued against, significant enough for someone to care
about, and surprising enough to earn a stranger's sustained attention.

Most books fail before the first word of prose is written because the
author started with a topic rather than a thesis. A topic is a
territory. A thesis is a position staked inside that territory — a
specific claim that could be wrong, that challenges what the reader
already believes, and that changes how they operate in the world if
it turns out to be true.

**The foundational test:** Can you write one sentence that someone
intelligent could argue against? If yes, you have a thesis. If the
sentence merely describes what you plan to cover, what you find
interesting, or what most people already believe, you have a topic.
No amount of subsequent prose rescues a book whose claim was never
sharp enough to cut.

### The Limits on Means Applied to Phase 1

The limits on means directly prohibit starting with a predetermined
conclusion and working backward through the evidence. Research that
begins with a predetermined answer is advocacy masquerading as inquiry
— a violation of the limits on means regardless of whether its
conclusion happens to be correct.

Phase 1 demands what the investigative journalism tradition calls
**hypothesis discipline**: the working claim must be genuinely
testable, which means it must be genuinely possible for the research
to disprove it. An author who cannot describe what evidence would
change their mind has not yet formed a thesis — they have formed a
preference.

### Topic vs. Thesis: The Core Distinction

- **Topic** = static territory; names what you are interested in;
  makes no claims. Example: "Spiritual formation." A book built
  around a topic produces a "report" — information without a
  governing claim.
- **Thesis** = position staked inside the territory; a specific
  claim that could be wrong; changes how the reader operates if
  proven true.

The failure mode: mistaking the topic for the thesis produces the
most common and most damaging sentence in book proposals — "This book
is about [topic]." A finished Phase 1 never produces that sentence.
It produces: "This book argues that [claim]."

### The Fundamental Progression: Topic → Question → Problem → Claim

**Stage One: Topic.** A static territory. Necessary starting point,
not an argument.

**Stage Two: Question.** Moving from topic to question gives research
a direction and purpose. A question names what you do not know. The
critical distinction: a question whose answer is settled fact, purely
speculative, or a dead end — where a perfect answer connects to
nothing larger — is not a research question. A genuine research
question is answerable through evidence and consequential in its
implications.

**Stage Three: Problem.** The most important and most frequently
skipped transition. A question names what you want to find out. A
problem names what is at stake if the question remains unanswered.
A question becomes a research problem only when not answering it
costs a community of readers something they value. Every problem
has two essential components: a **condition** (the state of not-
knowing) and a **cost** (what that gap prevents).

Apply the cascading "So what?" sequence: state your question, then
ask "So what? Why would anyone care if this question remains
unanswered?" Continue until the reader cannot reasonably ask "So
what?" one more time — until they are asking "What do we do about
this?" That is the problem.

**Stage Four: The Claim.** The answer to the problem. Not a preview
of topics to be covered, but a specific, falsifiable position the
book will defend through accumulated evidence, argument, and earned
personal testimony.

Robert McKee's "controlling idea" provides the most useful
formulation: a sentence containing a value and its cause. "Strategic
competence without character formation produces cultural failure,
because strategies are adopted by institutions but formation changes
people" is a controlling idea. Every chapter, finding, and piece of
testimony in the finished book either proves, develops, or applies
the controlling idea. If a chapter cannot be connected to the
controlling idea, it does not belong in the book.

### What Makes a Claim Load-Bearing vs. Ornamental

A **load-bearing claim**:
- Is falsifiable — a specific type of evidence could, in principle,
  disprove it
- Is significant — asks the reader to change something they actually
  believe, not merely add information
- Is surprising — a knowledgeable reader finds it genuinely
  unexpected; the reaction is "That can't be right — or can it?"
- Is implicative — if true, changes how the reader operates in the
  world on a specific Tuesday afternoon
- Is vantage-dependent — could not be claimed with equal authority
  by any writer with access to the same research

An **ornamental claim** confirms what a knowledgeable person already
suspects, summarizes what the book covers, or requires no proof
because no reasonable person would dispute it.

### The Thick Thesis Formula

A thin thesis states a position without reasons. A thick thesis states
a position, the reasons that support it, the qualifications that
limit it, and an acknowledgment of the strongest counterposition.

**Formula: Claim + because + Reasons + although + Acknowledgment**

To develop thin to thick: (1) State the thin version. (2) Ask "Why
is this true?" — write the two or three primary reasons. (3) Ask
"Under what conditions is this not true?" — write the most important
qualifying conditions. (4) Ask "What would someone who genuinely
disagrees say?" — write the strongest version of the counterposition,
not a strawman.

### The Five-Part Problem Statement

Phase 1 is complete only when all five components are answered in
writing. Incomplete answers are symptoms that the thinking is not
yet finished.

**Component 1: The Problem.** One sentence naming the gap, failure,
contradiction, or confusion that currently exists — the condition the
book addresses.

**Component 2: Who Has It.** A portrait of the specific person in
the specific situation who experiences this problem most acutely. Name
their current condition: what they already know, what they have tried,
what is not working. Name the condition they will be in when the book
is finished: what they will understand and do differently. Specific
enough to recognize on the street.

**Component 3: Why Existing Answers Fail.** One sentence per existing
answer — what each gets right and where it falls short. The most
interesting gaps are in books that are largely correct but missing
one specific, consequential thing. That missing thing is the
author's claim.

**Component 4: The Author's Vantage Point.** An argument — not a
credential list — for why this author's specific combination of
experience, formation, limitation, and perspective enables them to
see something that a full insider and a full outsider would each
miss. When the author's specific history is the only thing that
makes the claim credible, the vantage point is essential.

**Component 5: The Claim.** If this book succeeds, the reader will
believe this specific thing they did not believe before — in thick
thesis format.

### Social Currency Audit (Four Tests, Applied in Writing)

Social Currency — the quality that makes a reader say "I did not
know that and I cannot stop thinking about it" — is a property of
the underlying claim, not of polished prose.

- **Surprise Test:** Would a knowledgeable person find the claim
  genuinely unexpected? The reaction must shift from "Obviously" to
  "That can't be right — can it?"
- **Specificity Test:** Can the claim be stated in one or two
  sentences that a stranger could repeat to a friend at dinner?
- **Implication Test:** If true, does it change how the reader
  acts on a specific Tuesday afternoon in a specific role?
- **Verification Test:** Is the claim grounded in something beyond
  personal conviction — a study, a dataset, a documented case?

### The Awe Diagnostic

Awe — the genuine cognitive response to discovering that reality is
more coherently designed than previously assumed — is not a stylistic
goal but an argumentative one. Three primary sources of awe:

1. **Independent traditions converging on the same truth** —
   empirical research arriving at what a philosophical, theological,
   or cultural tradition already mapped, producing the awe of double
   confirmation. The convergence is most powerful when the two
   traditions developed independently.
2. **Overturning of assumed hierarchies** — the thing the reader
   assumed was powerful is actually weak; what they dismissed as
   secondary is actually central.
3. **The specific containing the universal** — a single concrete
   observation illuminates a structural principle governing a much
   larger domain.

### The Phase 1 Quality Gate

Phase 1 is complete only when all of the following are checked in
writing. Any unchecked item means the project must not proceed to
Phase 2.

- [ ] Falsifiable — revision conditions are written out
- [ ] Significant — the "So what?" cascade reaches genuine stakes
- [ ] Surprising — a knowledgeable reader's reaction is not
      "Obviously"
- [ ] Implicative — changes reader behavior on a specific Tuesday
- [ ] Vantage-dependent — could not be claimed by any writer
- [ ] Held provisionally — revision conditions are explicit
- [ ] Five-part problem statement complete in writing
- [ ] All four Social Currency tests pass in writing
