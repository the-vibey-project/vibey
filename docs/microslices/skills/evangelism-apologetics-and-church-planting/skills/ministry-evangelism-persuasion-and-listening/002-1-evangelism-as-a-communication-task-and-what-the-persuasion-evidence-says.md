## §1 Evangelism as a communication task, and what the persuasion evidence says

### What the evidence supports

> **WHAT THE PERSUASION EVIDENCE SAYS**
>
> Listening first — eliciting the other person's view before advancing yours — produces durable
> change. Conversation beats broadcast, substantially. Moral reframing — arguing from their values
> rather than yours — is difficult, effective, and rarely done, because it requires understanding
> values you may not share. Relevant facts and evidence are repeatedly the strongest identified
> lever. More forceful assertion persuades less than assumed. Facts alone against identity-linked
> beliefs persuade less than assumed. Attitude change and behaviour change are different things and
> may not correlate. **Winning the argument is not the same as changing what someone does.**

Two of those lines are easy to misread in opposite directions, and the source states both:

- **Evidence is the strongest identified lever** — so preparing an honest answer is worth the
  work. The apologetic material in §6 → `ministry-apologetics-arguments-for-god` exists for this
  reason.
- **Facts alone against identity-linked beliefs persuade less than assumed** — so the same evidence
  aimed at a belief someone's identity is built on will underperform your expectation. These are
  not in tension: evidence is the best lever available *and* the lever is weaker than people think
  when identity is load-bearing.

Read the list as a set of tendencies, not as measured effects. The source supplies no studies, no
sample sizes and no numbers, and this skill does not invent any. What the list is good for is
**ruling techniques in and out** — it is a reason to stop preparing speeches and start preparing
questions, not a formula with a known conversion rate.

### Relational evangelism

**Relational evangelism** is sharing the gospel through ongoing authentic relationships rather than
through strangers-at-a-distance approaches. Its grounding claim is an observation, not a statistic:
**most people who come to faith do so through a trusted relationship** — a friend, family member or
coworker who earned the right to be heard over time. The source gives no figure; do not supply one.

The approach has real costs, and the source names them where it tabulates the six methods in
§3–§4 → `ministry-evangelism-methods-and-presentations`: relational evangelism is **slow, requires
genuine investment, and cannot be scaled through programs**. It is the highest-trust method and the
one that resists being turned into a campaign.

### Trust decomposes into three things

The research on trust is directly relevant, because the relationship is the mechanism:

| Component | The question the other person is actually asking | Where it is won or lost |
|---|---|---|
| **Competence** | Can you do it? | Knowing what you are talking about; not bluffing an answer you do not have |
| **Reliability** | Do you do what you said? | Small kept commitments, repeated over time |
| **Care** | Are my interests in your calculation? | Whether you are a friend or a project manager with a conversion target |

> **MOST TRUST FAILURES ARE CARE, MISTAKEN FOR COMPETENCE**
>
> When someone stops trusting you, the diagnosis you reach for is usually competence — *I did not
> have a good enough answer*. The source says the third component is the usual culprit: the person
> concluded their interests were not in your calculation. Studying harder does not fix that, and it
> is the failure most likely to be invisible to the person who caused it.

Care is also where evangelism is most easily corrupted, because a relationship maintained *in order
to* produce a decision is a relationship with a hidden purpose. That is precisely what the
disclosure test in §5 → `ministry-evangelism-ethics` is built to catch.
