## §5 The Ethics of Evangelistic Influence

### The disclosure test

> **THE DISCLOSURE TEST APPLIED TO EVANGELISM**
>
> If you told the person exactly what you were doing and why, would this still work? A genuine
> gospel invitation survives disclosure: "I believe Jesus is who he said he is, and I want you to
> know him." Manufactured pressure does not: engineered guilt, false urgency, exploiting grief or
> fear or loneliness, or using relationship as leverage. The test is simple: are you giving them
> what they need to decide, or what makes them decide your way?

The test is a single question, and its power is that it is **cheap to run and hard to fool**. You do
not need to adjudicate your own motives, which is the part nobody does well. You need only ask what
the move would sound like said out loud.

| The move | Roughly what disclosing it would sound like | Survives? |
|---|---|---|
| A genuine gospel invitation | "I believe Jesus is who he said he is, and I want you to know him." | **Yes** |
| Engineered guilt | "I am working on how bad you feel about yourself, because that is what moves you tonight." | No |
| False urgency | "I am implying a deadline that does not exist, because it shortens your deliberation." | No |
| Exploiting grief, fear or loneliness | "I raised this now because you are in no state to weigh it." | No |
| Relationship as leverage | "Where the friendship goes depends on where you land on this." | No |

*Only the first row is the source's own wording. The rest are the disclosure test applied to the
four categories the source names — they are what the test produces, not additional claims about how
common any of these are.*

### Persuasion, manipulation, coercion — the structural line

The source draws the line **structurally**, not by tone or intensity. The question is what the move
does to the other person's reasoning.

| | What it does to their reasoning | Why it works, or stops working | Survives disclosure? | Available to the evangelist? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Persuasion** | Engages it | Works *because* they evaluated it | Yes | **Yes — this one only** |
| **Manipulation** | Bypasses it, by exploiting bias, emotion, time pressure, social cost, or incomplete information | Stops working if the target sees the mechanism | No | No |
| **Coercion** | Removes the option | Does not need their reasoning at all | No | No |

Persuasion and manipulation are separated by a property you can actually check, and it is the same
property the disclosure test probes: **manipulation depends on not being seen; persuasion does not
care whether it is seen.** That is why a test made of one question does real work here.

The source's positive statement of what evangelism is allowed to be has four clauses, and the fourth
carries the same weight as the first three:

1. The gospel is **presented**.
2. The evidence is **given**.
3. The invitation is **extended**.
4. The person is **free to say no**.

### The five supporting questions

| Question | What it is probing | Fails when |
|---|---|---|
| Am I giving them what they need to decide, or what makes them decide my way? | The disclosure test, restated at the level of a single move | The information is selected for effect rather than for adequacy |
| Would I be comfortable if the roles were reversed? | Reciprocity — whether you would accept this being done to you | You would call it pressure coming the other way |
| Am I exploiting a state — grief, fear, exhaustion, loneliness — rather than making an argument? | Whether the state is doing the work the argument should be doing | The approach only works while the state lasts |
| Is there a power asymmetry? | Whether "yes" and "no" cost the same | A pastor, a parent or an employer is asking |
| Do they have a real exit? | Whether declining is actually available | Declining costs the relationship, the standing, or the job |

### Power asymmetry

The source states it flatly: **a pastor, a parent, an employer has power that changes the ethical
calculus of influence.** It names the asymmetry as a factor and does not tell you what to do about
it.

What the asymmetry changes is not whether the conversation may happen but what counts as a free
answer inside it. Where declining is expensive, a yes carries less information — about conviction,
and about anything else — because the yes was cheaper than the no.

> **WHAT THE SOURCE DOES NOT SETTLE**
>
> The reference identifies power asymmetry as a factor in the ethical calculus. It does not supply a
> remedy, a threshold, or a rule for when the asymmetry is disqualifying. Do not read a remedy into
> it, and do not treat "I noticed the asymmetry" as having discharged it. The structural end of the
> same problem — who holds power in a community and whether that power is accountable — is
> §19–§22 → `ministry-church-planting`, which covers norms, the invisible power structure a group
> without formal structure acquires, and safeguarding as a legal obligation rather than a matter of
> judgement.

### Exploited states, and what the objection is not

Grief, fear, exhaustion and loneliness are the four the source names. The objection is **not** that a
grieving or frightened person may not come to faith, and the source does not say that. The objection
is to the substitution: using the state *rather than making an argument*.

The tell is the one in the table above — an approach that works only while the state lasts was
working on the state. The pastoral counterpart to this sits in the problem-of-evil material: people
asking about suffering are often suffering, and a purely intellectual answer is pastorally
inadequate (§9–§11 → `ministry-apologetics-evil-objections-and-worldview`). Both halves are needed:
do not answer a wound with a syllogism, and do not use the wound as the argument.

### A real exit

"Do they have a real exit?" is the question that catches what the four named failures have in common.
**Using relationship as leverage is precisely the removal of the exit** — if the friendship is
contingent on the answer, declining is not actually available, and what remains is not persuasion
even if every word spoken was true.

### The hard cases

The source names three and holds all three to the same test — **engage reasoning, do not manufacture
pressure**.

| Hard case | Why it is hard | What the test still asks |
|---|---|---|
| Persuading someone toward **their own stated spiritual goals** | The goal is theirs, so the move looks exempt from the whole question | Are you engaging their reasoning, or using a goal they cannot argue with as a lever? |
| **Altar calls** | A decision is asked for publicly, inside a bounded moment, in a setting the asker designed | Is the moment giving them what they need to decide, or making the decision cheaper to say yes to than to defer? |
| **Public invitations** | Same structure, with the social cost of the response visible to the room | Is declining as available as responding? |

> **THE TEST APPLIES TO DESIGNED PRACTICES, NOT ONLY TO CONVERSATIONS**
>
> Two of the three hard cases are not conversations at all — they are practices somebody designed,
> scheduled and repeated. That is the point of naming them: the disclosure test is meant to be run
> over a service, a campaign, a curriculum or a follow-up sequence, where the pressure is built into
> the structure rather than chosen in the moment by the person speaking.

> **THE SOURCE DOES NOT RULE FOR OR AGAINST ALTAR CALLS**
>
> Altar calls and public invitations are contested between and within Christian traditions, and the
> reference does not adjudicate the practice. It names them as hard cases and applies the same test
> it applies to everything else. Read it that way: the test judges a particular instance, not the
> category.
