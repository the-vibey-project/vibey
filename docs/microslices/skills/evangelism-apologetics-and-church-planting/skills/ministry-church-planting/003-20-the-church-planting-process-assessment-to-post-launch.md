## §20 The church planting process, assessment to post-launch

The source lays the process out in five phases with month ranges. Treat the months as the **shape of
the sequence and the relative weight of each phase**, not as a schedule with authority — a planning
frame rather than a measured median, and a context with no sending church or no existing
relationships will run longer.

### Phase 1 — Assessment and calling (months 1–6)

Before anything else, assess: is the planter called, gifted and ready? Church planting assessment
centres evaluate **character, competency and chemistry**.

> **NOT EVERYONE WHO WANTS TO PLANT A CHURCH SHOULD**
>
> The source states this plainly. Assessment exists to prevent failures that are painful and
> expensive for everyone involved — the planter, the family, the core team, the sending church, and
> the people who joined something that then collapsed. A negative assessment is the process working.

The key questions:

- Is there a genuine calling — **not just frustration with a current church**?
- Is there apostolic gifting: the ability to start something from nothing?
- Is there a team?
- Is there a target context?
- Is there a sending church or network providing accountability and support?

### Phase 2 — Team building and vision casting (months 6–12)

Gather a core team, typically **10–30 people** who share the vision and commit to the plant. The team
should include complementary gifts: someone who teaches, someone who pastors, someone who
evangelizes, someone who organizes, someone who welcomes.

Start with a real purpose and real people, not a platform. The founding problem here is **critical
mass** — an empty room is unattractive, so early effort goes into a small core who show up reliably.
**Consistency beats scale**: a small group present every week is a church; a larger number each
attending occasionally is not yet one.

### Phase 3 — Pre-launch and community engagement (months 12–18)

Before launching public worship services, build relationships in the target community: serve it, meet
needs, develop a presence. The goal is not to fill a room with strangers on launch Sunday but to
build enough relational trust that the first service is full of people **who already know the
planters**.

This is where relational evangelism and service-based approaches bear fruit — trust mechanics in
§1–§2 → `ministry-evangelism-persuasion-and-listening`, the service, missional and relational
approaches compared in §3 → `ministry-evangelism-methods-and-presentations`. **Rhythm matters more
than content**: a predictable, sustainable cadence builds community faster than occasional excellent
events.

### Phase 4 — Launch (month 18)

The first public worship service. Market it appropriately — invitations, social media, word of mouth.
Make it **excellent but not overproduced**; the goal is authenticity, not performance. Preach the
gospel clearly (§12–§15 → `ministry-preaching`). Collect contact information. **Follow up with every
visitor within 48 hours.**

The first three months after launch are critical. The source's observation is that people who come in
the first month and are followed up well often stay, and people who are not followed up almost never
return — practitioner observation about the direction of the effect, not a measured retention rate,
and uncontrolled for the obvious confounder that the people who get followed up well are often the
ones who already knew somebody. Follow up regardless: the cost is low and the failure mode is
invisible.

### Phase 5 — Post-launch and sustainability (months 18–36+)

**This is where most plants fail.** The adrenaline of launch fades, the core team is tired, visitors
stop coming, and support from the sending church may be ending.

The most common failure mode named here is the **founder bottleneck**: a church where one person does
everything cannot grow, cannot survive that person's absence, and quietly discourages others from
taking initiative. The third clause is the one people miss — it is not only a capacity limit, it
teaches everyone else that initiative is unwanted.

Two rules follow, both counterintuitive enough to state flatly:

- **Delegating badly early beats delegating well later.** A task done imperfectly by someone learning
  it is worth more than the same task done perfectly by the one person who must not stop.
- **Succession should be planned before it is needed.** Planning it after it is needed is not
  succession, it is a crisis.
