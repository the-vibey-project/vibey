## §14 Delivery and Speech Anxiety

**Substance matters more than delivery** — that is the persuasion evidence in §1 →
`ministry-evangelism-persuasion-and-listening` — **but delivery still matters, and poor delivery can
obscure good substance.** The goal is delivery that serves the message rather than drawing attention
to itself.

| Element | What to do | The failure it prevents |
|---|---|---|
| **Voice** | Vary pitch, pace and volume. Slow down for important points. **Pause after significant statements** — silence is the most underused tool in preaching, and it gives the hearer time to process | **Monotone is the most common delivery failure** |
| **Pace** | Aim for **130–150 words per minute**. Slowing down feels excruciatingly slow to you but sounds normal to the listener | Most preachers speak too fast, especially when nervous |
| **Movement** | Purposeful movement, not pacing. **Stand still for important points.** Move during transitions | Repetitive gestures — the congregation starts counting them instead of listening |
| **Eye contact** | Look at people, not at notes. Sustained contact with different sections of the room | Notes as script. Notes should be a reference — ideally you know the sermon well enough that they carry structure, not content |

The 130–150 wpm figure is a practitioner's working range, not a measured optimum for comprehension.
Its value is as a calibration check: time a rehearsal, divide, and see whether you are inside it.

### Speech anxiety

The source lists the interventions that **genuinely help**, and is specific about the mechanism in
each case — which is what makes the list usable rather than reassuring.

| Intervention | Mechanism the source names |
|---|---|
| **Exposure** — repeated preaching | **Habituation, not tuition.** You do not think your way out of it; the response attenuates with repetition |
| **Preparation and rehearsal** | Reduces *genuine* uncertainty — this is the part of the fear that is well-founded |
| **Reappraisal** | Interpreting arousal as readiness rather than fear; **the physiology is nearly identical** |
| **Slow exhalation** | Steadies the breath |
| **Attention shift** | From self-monitoring to the audience and the message. Much of the distress is self-focus, and **having a task outside yourself displaces it** |

**The audience cannot see most of what you feel.** The internal experience of anxiety is far louder
to the speaker than its outward signs are to anyone watching.

These come from the public-speaking-anxiety and emotion-regulation literature. The source presents
them as the interventions that genuinely help and names a mechanism for each, but supplies no
citation and no effect size for any of them, and neither does this pack — read the short list as the
source's selection, not as a graded evidence review. This is craft guidance, not clinical guidance:
anxiety that is disabling rather than uncomfortable is a matter for a clinician, and the source
offers nothing on it.
