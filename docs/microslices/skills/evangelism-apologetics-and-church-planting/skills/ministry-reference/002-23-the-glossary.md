## §23 The glossary

Sixteen terms, alphabetical as the reference lists them. Each definition below is the reference's own.
The third column sends you to the section where the term does work.

| Term | The reference's definition | Explained in |
|---|---|---|
| **Apologetics** | The discipline of giving reasons for Christian faith and responding to objections. From the Greek *apologia* — a defense. Not about winning arguments but about demonstrating that faith is rationally warranted and removing obstacles to belief. | §6 → `ministry-apologetics-arguments-for-god` |
| **Cosmological Argument** | An argument for God's existence from the existence of the universe. The Kalam version: whatever begins to exist has a cause; the universe began to exist; therefore the universe has a cause. The Leibnizian version: everything contingent has an explanation; the universe is contingent; therefore its explanation is a necessary being (God). | §6 → `ministry-apologetics-arguments-for-god` |
| **Covenant** | A binding agreement between God and humans that structures the biblical narrative. The major covenants: Abrahamic, Mosaic, Davidic, and the New Covenant inaugurated by Christ. | Defined but never argued — see *Two terms defined but never argued* below. The word appears in the body once, as an example of jargon a congregation may not know (§13 → `ministry-preaching`); the nearest treatment of the substance is the Creation–Fall–Redemption–Restoration arc at §4 → `ministry-evangelism-methods-and-presentations` |
| **Discipleship** | The lifelong process of growing in Christlikeness through teaching, modeling, practice, and community. More than information transfer — includes character formation, habit development, and relational attachment to Christ and his people. | §17 → `ministry-teaching-and-discipleship` |
| **Expository Preaching** | A preaching method that takes a specific biblical text as its starting point and expounds its meaning, with the main point of the sermon derived from the main point of the text. Lets Scripture set the agenda rather than the preacher. | §13 → `ministry-preaching` |
| **Fine-Tuning Argument** | A teleological argument: the physical constants of the universe are fine-tuned to a degree that permits life; this is not explainable by physical necessity or chance; therefore it is due to design. | §6 → `ministry-apologetics-arguments-for-god` |
| **Founder Bottleneck** | The most common failure mode in community building and church planting: a community where one person does everything cannot grow, cannot survive that person's absence, and discourages others from taking initiative. | §20 → `ministry-church-planting` (and again as the founder-to-team threshold at §22 → `ministry-church-planting`) |
| **Gospel** | The core Christian message: God created a good world; humanity fell into sin and separation from God; God acted in Christ (his incarnation, death, and resurrection) to redeem; and Christ will restore all things. The appropriate response is repentance and faith. | §4 → `ministry-evangelism-methods-and-presentations` (and as the thing being communicated from §1 → `ministry-evangelism-persuasion-and-listening` onward) |
| **Hermeneutics** | The theory and method of biblical interpretation. Includes attention to historical context, literary genre, grammar, theology, and the relationship between the Old and New Testaments. | Defined in the glossary only — see below. The practice appears without the label: exegesis and the author's main point at §13 → `ministry-preaching`, and context, genre and authorial intent as the answer to alleged contradictions at §10 → `ministry-apologetics-evil-objections-and-worldview` |
| **Moral Argument** | An argument for God's existence: if God does not exist, objective moral values do not exist; objective moral values do exist; therefore God exists. Rests on the intuition that some moral truths are objective, not culturally constructed. | §6 → `ministry-apologetics-arguments-for-god` |
| **Problem of Evil** | The objection that the existence of evil (especially gratuitous suffering) is incompatible with an omnipotent, omniscient, wholly good God. The logical problem (incompatible) and the evidential problem (improbable given the amount of suffering). | §9 → `ministry-apologetics-evil-objections-and-worldview` |
| **Relational Evangelism** | The practice of sharing the gospel through ongoing authentic relationships rather than strangers-at-a-distance approaches. Grounded in the observation that most conversions occur through trusted relationships. | §1 → `ministry-evangelism-persuasion-and-listening` (as a method among five others at §3 → `ministry-evangelism-methods-and-presentations`) |
| **Resurrection (apologetic argument)** | The argument that the established historical facts (Jesus's death, the empty tomb, the disciples' experiences of the risen Jesus, the conversions of Paul and James) are best explained by the actual resurrection of Jesus. Uses the minimal facts approach to establish common ground with skeptical scholars. | §7 → `ministry-apologetics-resurrection-and-scripture` |
| **Soul-Making Theodicy** | John Hick's response to the problem of evil: God permits suffering because character development (courage, compassion, patience) requires real challenges. The world is a soul-making environment, not a pleasure palace. | §9 → `ministry-apologetics-evil-objections-and-worldview` |
| **Teleological Argument** | An argument for God's existence from design or purpose in the universe. The contemporary version focuses on the fine-tuning of physical constants that permit life. | §6 → `ministry-apologetics-arguments-for-god` |
| **Worldview** | A comprehensive framework of beliefs about reality, knowledge, and ethics. Effective apologetics engages whole worldviews, not just individual arguments, showing that the Christian worldview provides the most coherent foundation for human knowledge, morality, and meaning. | §11 → `ministry-apologetics-evil-objections-and-worldview` |

**Nine of the sixteen are apologetics terms.** That is not an accident of drafting: Part II is where the
reference argues against a counterparty, and arguing against a counterparty is what generates technical
vocabulary. Parts III, IV and V contribute one term each because their content is craft and structure,
which the reference states in plain words rather than naming.

### How to read the argument entries

> **A GLOSSARY ENTRY STATES AN ARGUMENT, NOT A RESULT**
> The cosmological, fine-tuning and moral entries each end in a conclusion clause — "therefore the
> universe has a cause", "therefore it is due to design", "therefore God exists" — because that is the
> **form of the argument**, which is what a glossary entry is for. The teleological entry is the one
> exception, and deliberately so: it names the argument's subject and then defers its conclusion to the
> fine-tuning entry, which it identifies as the contemporary version. None of the four is a settled
> result. Each is contested philosophy with a literature of replies, and the reference itself attaches a
> stated limitation to every one: the cosmological argument establishes a first cause but not all of
> God's attributes, and fine-tuning meets the multiverse objection. The fourth argument §6 walks — the
> ontological — gets **no glossary entry at all**, which is consistent with how the reference treats
> it: its limitation is that the key premise, that a maximally great being is possible, *is precisely
> what the atheist denies*, and the reference concedes outright that most philosophers consider the
> argument inconclusive. Read an entry for the shape; read
> §6 → `ministry-apologetics-arguments-for-god` for what it costs.

The reference's own framing already says this: the classical arguments **do not compel belief — if they
did, every philosopher would be a theist**. What they are offered as doing is showing that theistic
belief is rationally warranted and that the burden of proof is shared, with the case built cumulatively
rather than resting on any single argument. A glossary user who quotes one entry as a proof has used the
glossary against the reference's own instruction.

The **Resurrection** entry carries the same caution in a different form. "The established historical
facts" is the minimal-facts framing — the claim that a specific short list is accepted across the
scholarly spectrum, so an argument can be built on it without first settling the reliability of the
Gospels. That framing is a **specific apologetic method with named critics in the scholarly literature**,
disputed both on which facts belong on the list and on how much agreement each really commands. The
reference does not overstate the standard of proof: its own conclusion is **historical probability, not
mathematical certainty**. The five counter-theories it tabulates — swoon, hallucination, legend,
theft/conspiracy, cognitive dissonance — are at §7 → `ministry-apologetics-resurrection-and-scripture`,
reproduced with the problems the source says each faces.

### Two terms defined but never argued

> **COVENANT AND HERMENEUTICS ARE DEFINED BUT NEVER ARGUED**
> Neither term is argued anywhere in §1–§22. They are the reference's two orientation entries: standard
> theological vocabulary a reader of a ministry reference will meet elsewhere, defined here so the rest
> reads, rather than terms the argument leans on. *Hermeneutics* as a label does not appear in the body
> at all, though the *practice* does — as exegesis in the preaching week (§13 → `ministry-preaching`) and
> as context, genre and authorial intent answering the contradictions objection (§10 →
> `ministry-apologetics-evil-objections-and-worldview`). *Covenant* does appear in the body, exactly
> once and not as a claim: it is one of the three words the curse-of-knowledge callout names as jargon a
> congregation may not know (§13 → `ministry-preaching`). The nearest thing to a treatment of its
> substance is the Creation–Fall–Redemption–Restoration arc at §4 →
> `ministry-evangelism-methods-and-presentations`.

This is worth knowing before you build on either one. A glossary entry with no section arguing behind it
is a definition, not a position the reference has argued for or qualified.
