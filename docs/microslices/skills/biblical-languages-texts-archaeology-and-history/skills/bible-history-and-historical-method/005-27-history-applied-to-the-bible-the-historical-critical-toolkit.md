## §27 History applied to the Bible: the historical-critical toolkit

### §27.1 The main methods

| Method | Question | Landmark figures |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Source criticism** | What earlier written sources did the author use? | Pentateuch: Documentary Hypothesis (J, E, D, P) — Julius Wellhausen (1878/1883); now heavily revised (many European scholars work with P / non-P models and later datings: Erhard Blum, Reinhard Kratz, Konrad Schmid). Gospels: the **Synoptic Problem** |
| **Form criticism** | What oral forms (genres) lie behind units, and what setting in life (*Sitz im Leben*)? | Hermann Gunkel (OT), Rudolf Bultmann and Martin Dibelius (NT) |
| **Tradition history** | How did traditions develop over time? | Gerhard von Rad, Martin Noth |
| **Redaction criticism** | How did editors/authors shape sources to their purposes? | Günther Bornkamm, Hans Conzelmann, Willi Marxsen |
| **Social-scientific criticism** | How do honor/shame, kinship, patronage, economy illuminate texts? | Bruce Malina, Wayne Meeks, the Context Group |
| **Literary / narrative criticism** | How do texts work as literature? | Robert Alter, Meir Sternberg, Adele Berlin |
| **Canonical criticism** | How do texts function in the final form of the canon? | Brevard Childs, James Sanders |
| **Reception history** | How have texts been read and used? | A major 21st-century growth area |
| **Memory studies** | How do communities remember, shape, and transmit the past? | Jan Assmann; in Jesus research: Chris Keith, Anthony Le Donne, Rafael Rodríguez |

### §27.2 The Synoptic Problem

Matthew, Mark, and Luke share extensive material, often verbatim. Leading solutions:

- **Two-Source Hypothesis:** Mark came first; Matthew and Luke used Mark plus a lost sayings source **Q**. **\[majority\]**
- **Farrer Hypothesis:** Markan priority, no Q — Luke used Matthew (Austin Farrer; Mark Goodacre, *The Case Against Q*, 2002). **\[significant minority, growing\]**
- **Matthean Posteriority** (Mark → Luke → Matthew) and the **Griesbach/Two-Gospel Hypothesis** (Matthew first, Mark last) have smaller followings.

### §27.3 The quests for the historical Jesus

| Phase | Characteristics |
| --- | --- |
| **First (Old) Quest** (Reimarus, published by Lessing 1774–78; D. F. Strauss 1835; liberal lives of Jesus) | Ended by **Albert Schweitzer's** *Quest of the Historical Jesus* (1906), which showed each "life of Jesus" looked like its author, and that Jesus was an apocalyptic prophet alien to modern liberalism |
| **"No Quest"** | Bultmann: the historical Jesus is largely inaccessible and theologically unnecessary |
| **New (Second) Quest** (Ernst Käsemann's 1953 lecture) | Renewed interest using form-critical **criteria of authenticity** |
| **Third Quest** (1980s–) | Jesus in his Jewish context: E. P. Sanders, John P. Meier, N. T. Wright, Dale Allison, Paula Fredriksen, Geza Vermes; also the **Jesus Seminar** (Robert Funk, John Dominic Crossan), known for voting with colored beads |
| **Memory/"post-criteria" turn** (2010s–) | Critique of the criteria and a shift to social memory, gist, and the Gospels as receptions of Jesus |

### §27.4 The criteria of authenticity — and their critique

The traditional criteria: **multiple attestation** (a tradition in independent sources), **dissimilarity** (unlike both Judaism and the early church — so not invented by either), **embarrassment** (awkward material the church would not invent — e.g., Jesus' baptism by John), **coherence** (fits the "authentic" core), and **Semitic/Palestinian features**.

Critique: **Morna Hooker** argued in 1970–72 that the criteria were the "wrong tool," imported from form criticism (designed to analyze literary units, not establish history), and that dissimilarity creates a Jesus artificially cut off from Judaism and his followers. The critique crested with ***Jesus, Criteria, and the Demise of Authenticity*** (Chris Keith and Anthony Le Donne, eds., 2012), whose contributors variously argued the criteria should be abandoned or significantly recast. Alternatives include **social memory** approaches (the Gospels as memories shaped by communities — the question becomes why Jesus was remembered this way), **gist memory** reasoning (Dale Allison, *Constructing Jesus*, 2010: trust recurrent patterns more than individual sayings), and **eyewitness testimony** arguments (Richard Bauckham, *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses*, 2006). **\[contested — the field is mid-transition\]**

### §27.5 What historians broadly agree on about Jesus (and why the list is short)

Across confessional lines, most critical historians accept that Jesus of Nazareth existed; was a Galilean Jewish teacher and healer/exorcist in reputation; was baptized by John; gathered disciples; spoke of the kingdom of God; caused a disturbance in the Temple; and was crucified under Pontius Pilate around 30 CE; and that his followers soon proclaimed he had been raised. **\[settled/majority for the core\]** The "Jesus myth" (non-existence) position is rejected by nearly all specialists. Beyond this core, interpretations diverge sharply.

The resurrection claim sits at the boundary of historical method: historians can study the **beliefs** and **reported experiences** of early Christians, and arguments are offered that the resurrection best explains them (the "minimal facts" approach). Whether a miracle can ever be the *best historical explanation* depends heavily on prior commitments (Ernst Troeltsch's principles of criticism, analogy, and correlation; methodological naturalism vs openness to the supernatural) — so agreement on the facts does not settle the conclusion. **\[contested at the level of method, not only evidence\]**
