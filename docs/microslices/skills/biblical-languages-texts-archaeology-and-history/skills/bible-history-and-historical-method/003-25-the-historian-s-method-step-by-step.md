## §25 The historian's method, step by step

### §25.1 Heuristics: finding sources

Archives, libraries, excavation reports, databases, collections of inscriptions and papyri, oral histories. For ancient history, the corpus is **small and largely fixed**, so progress often comes from new finds (papyri, inscriptions, scrolls) or new questions asked of old sources.

### §25.2 Types of sources

- **Primary** (produced at or near the time: documents, letters, inscriptions, artifacts) vs **secondary** (later analyses) vs **tertiary** (reference works).
- **Narrative sources** (histories, chronicles — written to tell a story) vs **documentary sources** (receipts, laws, letters — produced for practical purposes).
- **Intentional vs unintentional evidence.** Marc Bloch prized "witnesses in spite of themselves": what a source reveals unintentionally (assumptions, incidental details) is often more reliable than what it sets out to tell you.

### §25.3 External criticism (is it what it claims to be?)

The classic handbooks broke source criticism into questions: **when** was it produced (date), **where** (localization), **by whom** (authorship), **from what** earlier material (analysis of sources), **in what original form** (integrity — this is where *textual criticism* sits), and **with what evidential value** (credibility). The first group is external criticism; the last is internal criticism. Tools: palaeography, diplomatics, codicology (the physical book), materials science, radiocarbon, linguistic dating, provenance research.

### §25.4 Internal criticism (can we believe it?)

- **Competence:** Was the author in a position to know (eyewitness, informed, contemporary)?
- **Sincerity/interest:** What were the author's motives, biases, audience, and genre conventions?
- **Genre:** A royal victory inscription, a hymn, a legal text, and a biography make claims in different registers. Misreading genre is the most common error in using ancient sources — including biblical ones.
- **Corroboration:** Are there **independent** sources that agree? (Dependence must be established or ruled out — two sources copying a third are one witness, exactly as in textual criticism.)
- **Plausibility:** Does the claim fit what else we know?

### §25.5 Synthesis: arguing to conclusions

Historians generally reason by **inference to the best explanation** (abduction). C. Behan McCullagh's *Justifying Historical Descriptions* (1984) formalized criteria for the best hypothesis: explanatory **scope**, explanatory **power**, **plausibility**, being **less ad hoc**, fitting better with accepted beliefs, outstripping rivals on these criteria. Some philosophers of history (Aviezer Tucker) model historiography in explicitly **Bayesian** terms; Bayesian arguments are also used (and disputed) in biblical history debates.

### §25.6 Characteristic errors

- **Anachronism / presentism** — reading modern categories into the past.
- **Argument from silence** — "X isn't mentioned, so X didn't happen." Valid only when a source *would be expected* to mention X and survives fully enough to check.
- **Parallelomania** — Samuel Sandmel's 1962 term for overstating similarities between texts or traditions and inferring dependence too quickly.
- **Confirmation bias** and **cherry-picking**.
- **Single-source overreliance** and **counting sources without testing their independence**.
- **Consensus counting** — citing "most scholars" as evidence rather than engaging the arguments (and misstating the consensus).
