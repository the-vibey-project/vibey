## §3 Biblical Latin

### §3.1 The Vetus Latina ("Old Latin")

Christians in the Latin-speaking West (North Africa first, then Italy, Gaul, Spain) began translating the Greek Bible into Latin in the 2nd century. The result was not one translation but a **family of independent, uneven translations**, collectively called the **Vetus Latina**. Scholars distinguish an "African" type (seen in Cyprian's quotations) and "European" types. The Old Testament Vetus Latina was translated from the **Septuagint**, not the Hebrew. **\[settled\]** It survives in manuscripts and in the quotations of Latin church fathers; the Vetus Latina Institute at Beuron (Germany) has been producing a comprehensive edition since 1949.

### §3.2 Jerome and the "Vulgate"

In 382 Pope Damasus I commissioned **Jerome** (c. 347–420) to revise the Latin Gospels against the Greek. Jerome then, on his own initiative, went much further — eventually translating most of the Old Testament **directly from the Hebrew** (*iuxta Hebraeos*), learning Hebrew from Jewish teachers in Bethlehem. That was controversial: Augustine worried Jerome was undermining the church's inspired Septuagint.

**The "Vulgate" is a composite, not Jerome's single work** **\[settled\]**:

- The **Gospels** are Jerome's revision of the Old Latin.
- The **rest of the New Testament** was revised by someone else (candidates include Rufinus the Syrian or Pelagian circles); nobody knows for sure.
- Several books were never touched by Jerome and entered the Vulgate in Old Latin form (1–2 Maccabees, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch).
- The **Psalms** are the weirdest case: Jerome made three Psalters. The *Romanum* (light revision), the *Gallicanum* (revised from Origen's Hexaplaric Greek), and the *iuxta Hebraeos* (from Hebrew). The medieval church used the **Gallican** — i.e., a Latin translation of a Greek translation of the Hebrew — because liturgy had already made it familiar. Jerome's better Hebrew-based Psalter lost.

The word *Vulgata* ("common, commonly used") only became the standard label for this Bible late; Roger Bacon in the 13th century is the earliest known to apply it this way.

### §3.3 Medieval and early-modern Vulgate history

- **Codex Amiatinus** (early 8th century, Wearmouth-Jarrow, England) — the oldest surviving complete Latin Bible and a key witness.
- **Alcuin's** revision under Charlemagne (c. 800) and Theodulf's rival edition tried to clean up corrupted texts.
- The 13th-century **Paris Bible** standardized book order and adopted the **chapter divisions** attributed to **Stephen Langton** (c. 1200s) — the chapters we still use.
- The **Gutenberg Bible** (c. 1454–55) was a Vulgate.
- The **Council of Trent** (1546) declared the Vulgate "authentic" for public doctrine — meaning doctrinally reliable, not that the original languages were superseded (a nuance often misreported).
- The **Sixtine Vulgate** (1590) was such a mess — Sixtus V reportedly edited it personally — that after his death the cardinals halted sales and later ordered copies destroyed. Its replacement, the **Clementine Vulgate** (1592), became the Catholic standard for nearly four centuries.
- **Modern critical editions:** the Oxford Vulgate (Wordsworth-White, NT), the Benedictine San Girolamo edition (OT, 1926–1995), and the one-volume **Stuttgart Vulgate** (Weber-Gryson; 5th ed. 2007) — the scholars' Vulgate.
- **Nova Vulgata** (1979; 2nd ed. 1986): the Holy See's official Latin Bible, not a Jerome edition but a revision of Jerome's Latin brought into line with modern critical Hebrew and Greek texts. The Clementine Vulgate remains in use for the 1962 Missal.

### §3.4 What makes "biblical Latin" distinctive

**Christian Latin** as a register was created partly by Bible translation:

- **Greek loanwords:** *ecclesia*, *baptizare*, *apostolus*, *evangelium*, *angelus*, *episcopus*, *presbyter*.
- **Hebraisms via Greek:** *et factum est* ("and it came to pass"), *in saecula saeculorum* ("unto ages of ages").
- **New meanings for old words:** *fides* (from "reliability/contract good faith" to "faith"), *gratia*, *salus*, *sacramentum* (originally a soldier's oath).

### §3.5 Latin renderings that changed Western history

| Passage | Vulgate | What happened |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Genesis 3:15 | *ipsa conteret caput tuum* ("**she** shall crush your head") | Hebrew has a masculine/common pronoun ("he/it"); the Latin feminine fed Marian interpretation and art |
| Exodus 34:29 | *cornuta esset facies sua* ("his face was **horned**") | Hebrew *qaran* ("shone/sent out rays") shares a root with *qeren* ("horn"); hence Michelangelo's horned Moses |
| Matthew 4:17 | *paenitentiam agite* ("**do penance**") | Erasmus pointed out the Greek *metanoeite* means "repent/change your mind"; Luther's first of the 95 Theses (1517) turned on this distinction |
| Luke 1:28 | *gratia plena* ("full of grace") | Greek *kecharitōmenē* ("favored one"); a key text in Catholic/Protestant debate |
| 1 John 5:7–8 | The *Comma Johanneum* ("three that bear witness in heaven...") | A Latin addition absent from early Greek manuscripts; entered the Textus Receptus and the KJV (Part III — §11.7 → `bible-textual-criticism`) |

### §3.6 The Latin legacy in modern Catholic translation policy

The Latin church's relationship to translation is governed by documents worth knowing: *Divino Afflante Spiritu* (1943) encouraged translation from the original languages; Vatican II's *Dei Verbum* (1965) urged translations "especially from the original texts"; *Liturgiam Authenticam* (2001) demanded close, formal translation for liturgy (requiring conformity with the Nova Vulgata in certain respects); and *Magnum Principium* (2017) returned more authority over liturgical translation to bishops' conferences.
