## §5 Biblical English

### §5.1 Old English (c. 700–1100)

No complete Old English Bible existed, but a great deal of Scripture was rendered:

- **Cædmon's** biblical poetry (7th century, per Bede).
- **Bede** reportedly translating John's Gospel on his deathbed (735).
- **Interlinear glosses:** Aldred's 10th-century Old English gloss written between the lines of the Latin **Lindisfarne Gospels**.
- The **Wessex Gospels** (late 10th century): the first free-standing translation of the four Gospels into English.
- **Ælfric of Eynsham** (c. 1000): translated parts of the Old Testament (the "Old English Hexateuch").

### §5.2 Middle English: the Wycliffite Bibles

The first complete English Bible is associated with **John Wycliffe** (d. 1384) and his circle. Two versions exist: an **Early Version** (c. 1380s), extremely literal from the Vulgate, and a **Later Version** (c. 1388–1395), more idiomatic, often credited to John Purvey. Both were translated from **Latin**. The church's response — Archbishop Arundel's **Constitutions of Oxford** (1409) — forbade unlicensed English translations; the Wycliffite Bible nonetheless survives in about 250 manuscripts, an extraordinary number for its time.

### §5.3 The printed Reformation Bibles

| Year | Bible | Significance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1526** | **Tyndale New Testament** (Worms; a 1525 Cologne printing was interrupted) | First English NT translated from **Greek** (Erasmus' edition) and first printed. Tyndale also translated the Pentateuch (1530), Jonah, and historical books. Executed near Brussels in 1536. |
| 1535 | **Coverdale Bible** | First complete printed English Bible (from Latin and German sources plus Tyndale). Coverdale gave us "lovingkindness" and much of the Psalter still in the Book of Common Prayer. |
| 1537 | "Matthew" Bible | John Rogers' compilation of Tyndale's full work plus Coverdale |
| 1539 | **Great Bible** | First "authorized" Bible for reading in English churches, under Henry VIII / Cromwell |
| **1560** | **Geneva Bible** | Made by Protestant exiles in Geneva; roman type, **verse numbers throughout** (using Robert Estienne's NT verse divisions of 1551), extensive (often anti-monarchical) study notes. The Bible of Shakespeare, the Puritans, and the Mayflower. |
| 1568 | Bishops' Bible | The Church of England's answer to Geneva; widely judged weak |
| 1582/1609–10 | **Douay-Rheims** | Catholic, from the Vulgate; Latinate vocabulary ("supersubstantial bread") |
| **1611** | **King James Version** (Authorized Version) | See below |

### §5.4 The King James Version: how it was actually made

- **Origin:** the **Hampton Court Conference** (1604), where the Puritan John Rainolds proposed a new translation; James I agreed, partly because he disliked the Geneva notes.
- **Organization:** Richard Bancroft, Bishop of London, recruited the scholars and issued **15 rules**. About 50 translators (54 were named; perhaps 47 worked) were divided into **six companies** — two each at Westminster, Oxford, and Cambridge — three on the Old Testament, two on the New, one on the Apocrypha.
- **Key rules:** follow the **Bishops' Bible** and alter it as little as "the truth of the original will permit"; keep traditional ecclesiastical words ("church," not Tyndale's "congregation"); **no doctrinal marginal notes** (a direct strike at Geneva); consult Tyndale, Matthew, Coverdale, the Great Bible, and Geneva where they agree better with the original.
- **Process:** each translator drafted a section; the company reviewed; drafts circulated to other companies; a final general committee resolved disputes. A few working documents survive (e.g., an annotated Bishops' Bible and John Bois' notes of the final revision meetings).
- **Tyndale's ghost:** the 1611 preface says the aim was not to make a new translation but to "make a good one better." Jon Nielson and Royal Skousen's 1998 quantitative study estimated that about **84% of the KJV New Testament and 76% of the Old Testament portions Tyndale translated** retain Tyndale's wording. **\[majority\]** (Other estimates range from two-thirds to 90% depending on method.)
- **Texts used:** for the NT, Greek editions in the Erasmus–Stephanus–Beza tradition (later called the *Textus Receptus*, Part III — §11 → `bible-textual-criticism`); for the OT, the Masoretic Hebrew of the Bomberg Rabbinic Bibles.
- **The KJV you buy is not the 1611.** Nearly every KJV printed today follows Benjamin Blayney's **1769 Oxford** standardization of spelling, punctuation, and italics. The 1611 had printing errors (the 1631 "Wicked Bible" famously omitted "not" from "Thou shalt not commit adultery").
- **Reception:** slow at first; the Geneva Bible stayed more popular for decades. The KJV's cultural dominance came in the late 17th–19th centuries.

### §5.5 The modern English translations

**The revision line (formal, KJV → RSV tradition):**

- **English Revised Version** (NT 1881, OT 1885): first official KJV revision; NT based on the new critical text of Westcott and Hort. Its American counterpart: **American Standard Version** (1901), which printed "Jehovah."
- **Revised Standard Version** (NT 1946, OT 1952): mainline Protestant; the Isaiah 7:14 "young woman" controversy made it a culture-war object.
- **New Revised Standard Version** (1989) → **NRSV Updated Edition** (NRSVue, 2021): the academic standard in most university biblical studies.
- **English Standard Version** (2001): an evangelical revision of the 1971 RSV. Crossway released text updates in 2007, 2011, 2016, and announced a limited update rolling out from spring 2025, expected to reach nearly all editions by fall 2026 — including returning Genesis 3:16 from the 2016 "contrary to" back to the 2001 rendering "for." **\[as of 2026\]**
- **New King James Version** (1982): modernized KJV, retaining the Textus Receptus.

**Independent formal-equivalence:**

- **NASB** (1971; 1995; 2020 update) — often called the most literal major English translation.
- **Legacy Standard Bible** (2021): a revision of NASB95, rendering the divine name as "Yahweh."

**Balanced / "optimal equivalence":**

- **NIV** (NT 1973, full 1978, revised 1984, 2011) — the best-selling modern English Bible; an intermediate TNIV (2005) sparked the gender-language controversy.
- **HCSB → CSB** (Christian Standard Bible, 2017; minor update 2020), produced by a trans-denominational team of 21 conservative evangelical scholars, based on BHS and NA28/UBS5.
- **NET Bible** (2005; 2nd ed. 2019): famous for about 60,000 translators' notes explaining choices — probably the best free window into how translators think.

**Functional / dynamic:**

- **Good News Bible / Today's English Version** (1966–76), Eugene Nida's school in action.
- **New Living Translation** (1996; revised 2004, 2015): a true translation (not a paraphrase like the old *Living Bible*).
- **The Message** (Eugene Peterson, 1993–2002): an idiomatic paraphrase.

**Catholic:** Jerusalem Bible (1966) → New Jerusalem (1985) → Revised New Jerusalem (2019); **New American Bible** (1970) → NABRE (2011). The US bishops have announced a revised version to be published in 2027 under a new name, the **Catholic American Bible**, intended to serve prayer, catechesis, and liturgy in one text. **\[as of 2026\]**

**Jewish:** the Jewish Publication Society's 1917 version; the **NJPS Tanakh** (1985; revised 2023 as the *JPS Tanakh: Gender-Sensitive Edition*); Everett Fox's *Schocken Bible* (Buber-Rosenzweig-style foreignizing); **Robert Alter's** *The Hebrew Bible* (2018) — a single scholar's literary translation, influential on how people think about Hebrew style.

**British/ecumenical:** New English Bible (1961/1970, a fully fresh translation) → Revised English Bible (1989).

### §5.6 The spectrum, simplified

```
MORE FORMAL ←——————————————————————————————————————————→ MORE FUNCTIONAL
Interlinear  YLT  NASB/LSB  ESV/NKJV/KJV  NRSVue  CSB  NIV  NET  NABRE/REB  NLT  GNT  CEV  Message
```

Warning: this line hides real complexity. A "literal" translation can be less *accurate* when Greek or Hebrew idiom is rendered word for word (e.g., "bowels of mercies"), and a "dynamic" one can over-interpret. Every translation sits at different points on the spectrum in different books. The honest summary: **formal translations preserve more of the source's form and ambiguity; functional translations make more decisions for you.** Serious readers use one of each.
