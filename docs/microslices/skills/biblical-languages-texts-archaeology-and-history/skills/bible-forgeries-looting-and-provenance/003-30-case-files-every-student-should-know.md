## §30 Case files every student should know

| Case | What happened | Lesson |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Post-2002 "Dead Sea Scroll" fragments** and the **Museum of the Bible** | Around 70 purported Dead Sea Scroll fragments entered the market from 2002. The Museum of the Bible (Washington, D.C.) bought 16. In 2018 it announced at least five were not authentic; in March 2020 an independent Art Fraud Insights team led by Colette Loll reported, in a 200+ page study, that **all 16** were modern forgeries — mostly ancient leather rather than the parchment typical of genuine scrolls, inked in modern times and treated to mimic the real thing. Several leading scholars had published some of them as genuine. The genuine corpus (tens of thousands of fragments, mostly in the Israel Museum and IAA collections) is unaffected. | Scholarly publication does not authenticate an unprovenanced object; material science does. |
| **Hobby Lobby / Iraqi artifacts** | In 2017 Hobby Lobby agreed to forfeit thousands of smuggled cuneiform tablets and other objects and pay $3 million in a U.S. civil action. | Collecting by well-meaning buyers can fund looting. |
| **"First-century Mark" fragment** | Rumored for years as the earliest NT manuscript; when published by the Egypt Exploration Society in 2018 (P.Oxy. 5345) it was dated to the late 2nd–early 3rd century. The EES then reported that papyri from its Oxyrhynchus collection had been offered for sale without authorization, a scandal centered on the Oxford papyrologist Dirk Obbink, who denied wrongdoing. | Hype outruns evidence; institutional custody matters. |
| **Gospel of Jesus' Wife** | A Coptic papyrus scrap announced in 2012 by Harvard's Karen King, containing "Jesus said to them, 'My wife...'". Journalist Ariel Sabar's 2016 investigation traced its seller and showed a fabricated provenance; King accepted that it was probably a forgery. | Grammar and handwriting red flags were raised early by specialists; provenance research closed the case. |
| **James Ossuary** ("James, son of Joseph, brother of Jesus") | Announced 2002. Israel's antiquities authority charged the owner with forgery; after a years-long trial he was acquitted of forgery charges in 2012, with the court stressing it had not ruled the inscription authentic. **\[unresolved\]** | Legal verdicts are not scholarly verdicts. |
| **Codex Sinaiticus** | Tischendorf's removal of the codex from St. Catherine's Monastery in the 19th century remains disputed — loan, gift, or appropriation. The manuscript is now split among the British Library, Leipzig, St. Petersburg, and Sinai; the digital project reunites it virtually. | Colonial-era acquisitions carry unresolved ethical debts. |
