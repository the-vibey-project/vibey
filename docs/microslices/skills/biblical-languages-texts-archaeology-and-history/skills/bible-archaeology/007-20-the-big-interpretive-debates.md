## §20 The big interpretive debates

### §20.1 Minimalists and maximalists (1990s–2000s)

The "**Copenhagen school**" and allied scholars (Niels Peter Lemche, Thomas L. Thompson, Philip Davies, Keith Whitelam) argued that the Hebrew Bible is largely a Persian- or Hellenistic-era literary creation with little reliable history about the monarchy — "minimalism." Critics (William Dever, Kenneth Kitchen, Baruch Halpern, many Israeli archaeologists) argued for more historical kernel — "maximalism," though few accept the label. The Tel Dan inscription's discovery landed in the middle of this fight. Today the extreme positions have receded; debate focuses on specific periods and evidence. **\[field has moved to the middle\]**

### §20.2 The Iron Age chronology debate (David and Solomon)

In 1995–96, **Israel Finkelstein** proposed a **Low Chronology**, lowering the dates of key Iron I–IIA strata by about 60–100 years. The implication: monumental structures once attributed to Solomon (e.g., the Megiddo, Hazor, and Gezer gates and palaces) belong to the 9th-century Omride dynasty of the northern kingdom, and 10th-century Judah was a small highland chiefdom. **Amihai Mazar** proposed a **Modified Conventional Chronology** (Iron IIA roughly 980–840/830 BCE), which overlaps with both. The debate drove large-scale radiocarbon projects in Iron Age archaeology; it now turns largely on Bayesian models, sample selection (short-lived vs charcoal), and the stratigraphic security of samples. Finkelstein himself has written that the gap between his system and the "reasonable voice" of the traditional camp is narrowing, but that the dispute is far from resolved. **\[contested\]**

**Khirbet Qeiyafa** (excavated 2007–2013 by Yosef Garfinkel and Saar Ganor): a fortified early-10th-century BCE town overlooking the Elah Valley, with a casemate wall, two gates, and an ink-inscribed ostracon. Garfinkel argues it shows a Judahite state administration in David's time; others identify it with Philistine, Canaanite, or northern Israelite polities, or accept the date while disputing the interpretation. **\[contested\]**

### §20.3 Exodus, conquest, and the emergence of Israel

- **Jericho:** John Garstang (1930s) dated a destroyed wall to Joshua's era; Kathleen Kenyon's stratigraphic work redated it to the Early Bronze Age — nearly two thousand years earlier — and found little Late Bronze occupation. Bryant Wood's 1990 reassessment argued for a later destruction date; most archaeologists follow Kenyon's general picture. **\[majority\]**
- **Models of Israel's emergence:** (1) **conquest** (Albright), (2) **peaceful infiltration** of pastoralists (Alt, Noth), (3) **peasant revolt** (Mendenhall, Gottwald), (4) **gradual emergence** from within Canaanite society, supported by highland survey data showing hundreds of small new villages in Iron I (Finkelstein, Dever, and others). The majority of archaeologists hold some form of (4), often with (2), and with debate over how much memory of an Egyptian sojourn the Exodus traditions preserve. **\[majority/contested\]**
- A minority of evangelical archaeologists (e.g., the Associates for Biblical Research, excavating at Shiloh and Khirbet el-Maqatir) argue for a 15th-century BCE conquest ("early date") — a position not accepted in mainstream academic archaeology. **\[minority\]**

### §20.4 Jerusalem

Jerusalem is continuously occupied, holy to three faiths, and politically contested — archaeologically among the hardest sites on earth. The debate over the size and nature of 10th-century Jerusalem (Eilat Mazar's "Large Stone Structure," claimed as David's palace; skeptics date parts later or read it differently) continues. **\[contested\]** Excavations in the **City of David**, largely funded by the Elad foundation in the Palestinian neighborhood of Silwan, are simultaneously productive and politically fraught (Part VI — §31 → `bible-forgeries-looting-and-provenance`).
