## §17 Dating: how archaeologists know when

### §17.1 Relative dating

- **Stratigraphy** (above/below).
- **Typology and seriation:** artifacts (especially pottery) change style in datable sequences. Petrie's insight: a sherd can date a layer.
- **Ceramic assemblages** define periods: Late Bronze, Iron I, Iron IIA, etc.

### §17.2 Absolute dating

- **Radiocarbon (¹⁴C):** measures decay of carbon-14 in once-living material. Modern **AMS** dating needs milligrams. Raw ages must be **calibrated** against a curve (currently **IntCal20**) built from tree rings and other records.
- **Pitfalls:** the **old wood effect** (charcoal from a long-lived tree or reused beam gives dates older than the event — so short-lived samples like seeds and olive pits are preferred); **calibration plateaus**, above all the "**Hallstatt plateau**" (c. 800–400 BCE), where different real dates produce similar radiocarbon ages, blurring precisely the period of the late Judahite monarchy and exile.
- **Bayesian modeling** (e.g., OxCal) combines radiocarbon dates with stratigraphic order to narrow ranges. The Iron Age chronology debate (below) is largely a Bayesian-modeling debate.
- **Dendrochronology** (tree rings), **archaeomagnetism** (the Levant has a well-studied Iron Age geomagnetic "spike"), **OSL** (luminescence dating of sediments).
- **Historical synchronisms:** Egyptian and Assyrian chronologies (anchored by astronomical records such as the eclipse of 763 BCE in the Assyrian eponym list) provide fixed points; biblical events are tied to them via inscriptions (e.g., Shoshenq I's campaign list at Karnak; Assyrian annals naming Israelite and Judahite kings).
- **Coins** (from the Persian period on) and **inscriptions** date contexts directly.
