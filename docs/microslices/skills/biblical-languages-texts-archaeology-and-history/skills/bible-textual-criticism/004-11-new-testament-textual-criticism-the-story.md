## §11 New Testament textual criticism: the story

| Date | Milestone |
| --- | --- |
| 1516 | **Erasmus'** Greek NT (*Novum Instrumentum*), based on a handful of late manuscripts. For the last six verses of Revelation his manuscript was defective, so he back-translated from Latin. |
| 1550–51 | **Robert Estienne (Stephanus)** prints an edition with variant notes (1550), then introduces the **verse numbers** we still use (1551). |
| 1633 | The Elzevir edition's preface advertises a text "now received by all" — the origin of the label **Textus Receptus** (TR). |
| 1707 | **John Mill** lists roughly 30,000 variants — shocking contemporaries. |
| 18th c. | Bengel (weigh manuscripts by families), Wettstein (manuscript sigla), Griesbach (canons of criticism) |
| 1831 | **Karl Lachmann** publishes the first major edition that abandons the TR for a text built from the oldest witnesses. |
| 1844/1859 | **Constantin von Tischendorf** acquires leaves (1844) and then the main body (1859) of **Codex Sinaiticus** from St. Catherine's Monastery, Sinai — a controversial story still disputed between the monastery and the modern holders. |
| 1881 | **Westcott & Hort**, *The New Testament in the Original Greek*: the "text-type" theory (Neutral/Alexandrian, Western, Syrian/Byzantine), strong preference for Vaticanus and Sinaiticus; the basis of the Revised Version. |
| 1898 | **Eberhard Nestle** publishes the *Novum Testamentum Graece*, originally a majority text of the three leading 19th-century editions. |
| 20th c. | Papyri revolution: **Chester Beatty** papyri (P45, P46, P47) in the 1930s; **Bodmer** papyri (P66, P72, P75) in the 1950s. |
| 1959 | **Kurt Aland** founds the Institute for New Testament Textual Research (**INTF**) in Münster. |
| 1966 | United Bible Societies' *Greek New Testament* (UBS1) — designed for **translators**, with a letter-grade {A}–{D} confidence rating per variant. |
| 1979/1993 | NA26 / NA27 (same text as UBS3/UBS4). |
| 1997– | **Editio Critica Maior** (ECM) begins (Catholic Letters first), using the **CBGM**. |
| 2012 | **NA28**: revised apparatus; Catholic Letters text changed in just over 30 places to follow the ECM. |
| 2017 / 2021 / 2024 | ECM volumes for Acts (2017), Mark (2021), and Revelation (2024). |
| **In preparation** | **NA29** — see below. |

### §11.1 Where things stand in 2026 **\[as of 2026\]**

- **NA28 (2012)** remains the current standard critical text.
- **NA29** is being prepared by an editorial committee appointed by the United Bible Societies. The Deutsche Bibelgesellschaft says it will adopt all of the ECM's textual changes in Mark (ECM 2021), Acts (2017), and Revelation (2024), thoroughly revise the apparatus in those books, adjust the witnesses cited in Paul based on ECM preparatory work, and add newly accessible papyri. No release date has been announced in the sources checked.
- The INTF states its aim is to have the complete ECM published **by 2030**.

### §11.2 How many manuscripts? (The honest answer)

The official register is the **Kurzgefasste Liste** maintained by INTF. Each witness gets a **Gregory-Aland (GA)** number in four categories:

- **Papyri** (𝔓1, 𝔓2...) — written on papyrus; the earliest.
- **Majuscules** (01, 02...; also known by letters: 01 = א Sinaiticus, 02 = A Alexandrinus, 03 = B Vaticanus, 05 = D Bezae) — capital letters on parchment.
- **Minuscules** (1, 2, 3...) — lowercase cursive script, from the 9th century on; the bulk of manuscripts.
- **Lectionaries** (ℓ1, ℓ2...) — arranged by liturgical readings.

In a September 2023 INTF post, Katie Leggett and Greg Paulson gave the then-current count of Liste entries as **about 5,700**, while explaining why "how many manuscripts" is a trickier question than it seems: one GA number can be a scrap with a few verses or a codex of 300+ leaves; one physical book can carry several GA numbers; scattered leaves share one number; palimpsests put two witnesses on the same page; some manuscripts are destroyed, missing, or in untraceable private hands; and the Liste is regularly purged of duplicates. They recommended asking "how many witnesses have been catalogued" instead, and showed that removing destroyed and missing items leaves about **5,541**. **Rounded popular figures like "5,800+" come from older counts or different counting rules.**

Add to that roughly 10,000+ Latin manuscripts, thousands in Syriac, Coptic, Armenian, Georgian, Ethiopic, Slavonic, and others, plus a vast body of patristic quotations.

### §11.4 Text-types and their critics

The older model grouped witnesses into **text-types**: **Alexandrian** (Egyptian; generally prized), **Western** (paraphrastic tendencies), **Byzantine** (the majority of later manuscripts; smooth, full), and sometimes **Caesarean** (now largely abandoned). Modern specialists increasingly treat these as **clusters on a spectrum** rather than discrete recensions, and the CBGM (below) was designed to work without them.

### §11.5 The Coherence-Based Genealogical Method (CBGM) — the method reshaping the text

Developed by **Gerd Mink** at Münster, the CBGM tackles the core problem of NT transmission: **contamination**. Scribes corrected manuscripts against multiple exemplars, so a simple family tree (a stemma) of manuscripts is impossible — any witness can have readings both older and younger than another witness.

The CBGM's key moves:

1. **Treat texts, not manuscripts.** The unit is the *state of the text* a manuscript carries (a "witness"), which may be far older than the physical manuscript.
2. **Collate everything** in full transcriptions and record every variation unit.
3. **Pre-genealogical coherence.** Compute the percentage agreement between every pair of witnesses across all variation units. This is fairly objective: it's just counting agreement.
4. **Local stemmata.** At each variation unit, editors decide — using traditional internal and external criteria — which reading gave rise to which (A → B, A → C, etc.). This is where human judgment enters.
5. **Genealogical coherence.** Aggregate thousands of local decisions to find, for each witness, which others are its **potential ancestors** (witnesses that more often have the prior reading where the two differ).
6. **Textual flow diagrams** show how a given reading moved through the tradition. If a reading's supporters are incoherent (scattered across unrelated witnesses), it probably arose more than once independently — a clue that it's a natural scribal change.
7. **Iterate.** Where a local stemma implies implausible genealogy, editors revisit it. The process loops.
8. **Global stemma.** A simplified overall picture of relationships.

**What the CBGM is not:** a machine that spits out the text. Its developer and INTF describe it as a tool for testing editorial hypotheses; human editors still define readings, build local stemmata, weigh internal evidence, and decide what to print. **Critics** have raised methodological objections (e.g., Stephen Carlson on potential bias; debates over pre-genealogical coherence; the incomplete integration of versional and patristic evidence), and INTF scholars have published replies. **\[method majority-adopted; details contested\]**

**Two important consequences:**

- The ECM marks some places with a **split guiding line** (a diamond ◆ in the ECM/NA), where editors could not decide between two readings — an explicit admission of uncertainty. This increased the number of acknowledged "undecided" places.
- The goal was reframed from "the original text" to the **initial text** (*Ausgangstext*): the hypothetical text from which the extant tradition descends. Whether the initial text should be identified with the authorial text — and whether "original text" is even a coherent target, given ancient publication practices — is a live debate (Eldon Epp, Michael Holmes, David Parker's "living text"). **\[contested\]**

### §11.6 How much is actually uncertain?

- Estimates for the number of NT variants run around **400,000–500,000+** across all Greek manuscripts — more variants than words in the NT (\~138,000). The number is huge **because** the manuscript base is huge; more copies mean more copying differences.
- The overwhelming majority are spelling, word order, the Greek *movable nu*, the article with names, and obvious slips — meaningless in translation. A small minority are both meaningful and viable.
- Popular claims like "99% certainty" are advocacy summaries, not measured statistics; what can fairly be said is that the wording of the NT is known with **unusually high confidence by ancient standards**, and that the uncertain places are **mapped and footnoted**, not hidden. The public Ehrman–Wallace debates are the best-known clash over how to characterize this.

### §11.7 The famous variant passages

| Passage | Issue | Where it stands |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Mark 16:9–20** ("longer ending") | Absent from Sinaiticus and Vaticanus; other endings exist ("shorter ending"; Freer Logion) | Most scholars think Mark originally ended at 16:8 or that its ending is lost; the longer ending is ancient (2nd c.) but likely secondary **\[majority\]**. Translations print it with brackets or notes. |
| **John 7:53–8:11** (woman caught in adultery) | Absent from the earliest manuscripts; floats in others (after Luke 21:38, after John 21) | Almost universally judged not original to John, though possibly an authentic early tradition **\[settled that it's not Johannine\]** |
| **1 John 5:7–8** (Comma Johanneum) | Trinitarian phrase found in Latin tradition; appears in only a handful of late Greek manuscripts, some of them clearly back-translated | Omitted by virtually all critical texts **\[settled\]**; Erasmus added it in his 3rd edition (1522) under pressure, reportedly after a manuscript (Codex Montfortianus) surfaced containing it |
| **Luke 22:43–44** (sweat like drops of blood) | Present in some early witnesses, absent from others (including 𝔓75, B) | Genuinely disputed **\[contested\]** |
| **John 1:18** | "the only God" (μονογενὴς θεός, 𝔓66, 𝔓75, א, B) vs "the only Son" (μονογενὴς υἱός) | Most critical texts print *theos*; translations divide, often with a note |
| **Mark 1:1** | "Son of God" present or absent | Bracketed in NA28 |
| **Hebrews 2:9** | "by the grace of God" (χάριτι θεοῦ) vs "apart from God" (χωρὶς θεοῦ) | Classic *lectio difficilior* debate |
| **Acts (the "Western" text)** | Codex Bezae's significantly longer and different Acts | A textbook case of two text-forms of one book |

### §11.8 Positions in the field

- **Reasoned eclecticism** — weigh external and internal evidence case by case (the mainstream; NA/UBS/ECM). **\[majority\]**
- **Thoroughgoing eclecticism** — rely almost entirely on internal evidence (G. D. Kilpatrick, J. K. Elliott).
- **Byzantine priority** — the majority text form is closest to the original (Maurice Robinson & William Pierpont's edition). **\[minority\]**
- **Textus Receptus / Confessional Bibliology** — the TR (as received by the Reformation churches) is providentially preserved; associated with KJV-preference and "KJV-only" movements. **\[minority; theological rather than purely text-critical\]**
- **Conjectural emendation** — adopting a reading found in no manuscript. Rare in NT criticism (the Nestle-Aland text prints a conjecture at Acts 16:12), much more common in Hebrew Bible criticism. INTF hosts an online database of NT conjectures.
