## §10 The reasoning: external and internal evidence

Textual critics weigh two kinds of evidence at every **variation unit** (a place where manuscripts differ):

### §10.1 External evidence (the witnesses)

- **Date and quality** of the manuscripts (though an early manuscript can carry a bad text, and a late manuscript can preserve an early one).
- **Geographical distribution** — independent support from Egypt, the West, and the East is stronger than support from one region.
- **Genealogical relationships** — witnesses are **weighed, not counted**. A thousand copies of one exemplar are really one witness.
- In NT criticism: **Greek manuscripts**, **early versions** (Latin, Syriac, Coptic, and others), and **patristic quotations**.

### §10.2 Internal evidence (the reasoning about the text)

**Transcriptional probability** (what would a scribe likely have done?):

- *Lectio difficilior potior* — the **harder reading is preferable**, because scribes tended to smooth difficulties, not create them. (The rule has limits: a reading can be "hard" because it's nonsense produced by error.)
- *Lectio brevior potior* — the **shorter reading is preferable**, because scribes tended to add. This old canon (Griesbach) was significantly qualified by **James Royse's** study of early papyri (2008), which found early scribes more often **omitted** than added. **\[majority, post-Royse\]**
- **The reading that best explains the others** — the master principle. If reading A explains how B and C arose, but not vice versa, A is likely earlier.

**Intrinsic probability** (what would the *author* likely have written, given style, vocabulary, theology, and context?).
