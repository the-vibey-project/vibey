## §24. Wildlife Management

**⚠️ Modern wildlife management is applied population ecology, and the North American Model
of Wildlife Conservation is the institutional frame in the US** — **wildlife as a public
trust, prohibition on commercial market hunting, allocation by law rather than by wealth,
and ⚠️ hunter- and angler-funded conservation through licence fees and the Pittman-Robertson
excise tax on arms and ammunition.**
```
CARRYING CAPACITY  ⚠️ K. Biological vs CULTURAL carrying capacity (what
   people will tolerate — usually the binding constraint near towns)
COMPENSATORY vs ADDITIVE MORTALITY  ⚠️ THE key concept. Below K, harvest
   may substitute for natural mortality rather than adding to it. Above
   some level it becomes additive
RECRUITMENT   ⚠️ fawns/poults surviving to join the population. Habitat-driven
SEX/AGE STRUCTURE  ⚠️ harvesting antlerless animals controls population;
   harvesting bucks mostly does not
HABITAT       ⚠️ food, cover, water, space — and the LIMITING one governs (§4's
   Liebig logic applies to wildlife too)
```
> **⚠️ GOTCHA — "protect the deer by not shooting does" is the most persistent and most
> damaging piece of management folklore.** ⚠️ **Population growth is driven by
> reproductive females. A herd over carrying capacity degrades its own habitat, and
> browse damage cascades into forest regeneration failure and reduced fawn survival.**
> **⚠️ Antlerless harvest is the primary population control tool, and reluctance to use it
> is a well-documented management problem across the whitetail range.**

**⚠️ Habitat management is mostly deliberate disturbance** (§6 → `agri-botany-and-plant-science`): **prescribed fire (⚠️ the
most under-used and most effective tool in much of the eastern and southern US), timber
stand improvement, edge feathering, native warm-season grass establishment, and food
plots — ⚠️ which are a supplement and a hunting tool, not a substitute for habitat.**
**⚠️ Predator-prey dynamics rarely work the way people assume**: ⚠️ **predator removal
frequently produces smaller and shorter-lived effects on prey populations than expected,
because habitat and weather usually dominate recruitment.**
