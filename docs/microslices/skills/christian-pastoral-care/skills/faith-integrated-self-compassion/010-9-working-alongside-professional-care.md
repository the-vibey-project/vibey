## 9. Working alongside professional care

### 9.1 When to refer — the red flags
Refer to, or insist on, licensed clinical care when there is:

- **Any suicidal thinking, plan or self-harm** — urgently (see the scope note).
- **Trauma symptoms** that disrupt life — intrusive memories, nightmares, flashbacks, dissociation,
  constant alertness, avoidance that is shrinking the person's world.
- **Burnout or depression with loss of function** lasting more than a couple of weeks, or getting worse.
- **Substance misuse, disordered eating, psychosis, or mania.**
- **Ongoing abuse** — and if a child or vulnerable adult is involved, the legal reporting duties where
  you are.
- **Spiritual struggle that is intensifying** — feeling abandoned or punished by God (§7: a recognised
  risk marker).
- **Suspected undiagnosed autism or ADHD** — assessment by a qualified clinician, not a pastoral guess.

### 9.2 What professional treatment looks like
For trauma, **trauma-focused psychotherapies** — such as trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy,
EMDR, cognitive processing therapy and prolonged exposure — are **first-line in major clinical
guidelines** (**Standing: well-supported**). Depression and anxiety have their own effective
treatments, including medication where a physician judges it appropriate. Taking them is not a lack
of faith.

### 9.3 How the two fit together
- **The person decides** whether and how faith enters their therapy. Many clinicians will work with a
  client's faith respectfully; Christian clinicians exist, but **licensure and trauma competence come
  first**, and a neurodivergent-informed clinician matters for autistic and ADHD adults.
- **Keep roles separate.** A pastor is not the therapist and should not become one; pastoral care
  supplies what therapy does not — presence, prayer, Scripture, sacrament, community, practical help.
- **With consent, coordinate.** Practices in §5 can reinforce therapeutic work — for instance, an
  identity truth the person has chosen can be rehearsed in prayer — while §8 is honest that the
  faith-framed versions are not shown to work better.
- **Confidentiality and safety.** What is shared pastorally stays within the limits the person agreed
  to, except where law or immediate safety requires otherwise.

### 9.4 Pacing and the stabilisation question
Many trauma clinicians follow a **phase-based** approach after **Judith Herman** — safety and
stabilisation, then processing and grieving, then reconnection. **Diane Langberg**'s summary of what
healing takes — **"talking, tears, and time"** — is good pastoral counsel about speed. ⚠️ Whether a
long stabilisation phase must come before trauma processing is **debated among trauma researchers**
(*wider literature*, for example de Jongh et al., 2016); that sequencing is the **clinician's call**,
not the pastor's. The source, for instance, holds that lament should wait until stabilisation is
established; others find the structure of lament a safe container from the start. Follow the person
and their clinician.
