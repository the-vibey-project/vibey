## The evidence: strong on behavior, weak on investment math

### The snowball has modest behavioral support and a small interest penalty

The academic record supports a narrower claim than "snowball beats avalanche." Kettle, Trudel, Blanchard and Häubl (Journal of Consumer Research, 2016) found concentrating repayments on one account increases motivation to get out of debt, strongest when it is the smallest account, and people appear to judge progress by the largest proportional reduction in any single account ([JCR abstract](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/43/3/460/2630069); [St. Gallen record](https://www.alexandria.unisg.ch/entities/publication/fa49decd-c7bd-4c0c-813a-aa497db505fe)). Gal and McShane (2012) found closing out whole accounts predicts debt elimination ([JMR](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmr.11.0272)). But Amar and coauthors (2011) describe "debt account aversion" as potentially costly when it ignores interest rates ([JMR](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmkr.48.SPL.S38)), and Hamilton (2023) quantifies the snowball's extra interest ([Southern Economic Journal](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/soecon/v89y2023i3p830-859.html)). Ramsey's own FAQ concedes the avalanche costs a little less. Early Retirement Now argues avalanche is a "mathematical certainty" to be more efficient ([ERN](https://earlyretirementnow.com/2017/06/14/good-advice-vs-feel-good-advice/)); Kitces values the snowball's behavioral benefits but would target debts above roughly 8% earlier ([Kitces](https://www.kitces.com/blog/checklist-style-financial-planning-dave-ramsey-baby-steps-money-guy-show-order-of-operations/)).

A **self-computed** example from the research: four cards totaling $30,000 ($3,000 at 19.99%, $6,000 at 29.99%, $9,000 at 24.99%, $12,000 at 17.99%, minimums of $75, $180, $250 and $300) with a $1,500 monthly budget. Avalanche takes 25 months and $7,222 of interest; snowball takes 26 months and $7,571, a gap of about $349 and one month. The gap grows when the largest balance also carries the highest rate and the budget is tight. When the difference is a few hundred dollars, the method a person will actually sustain is the better one. Whatever one concludes, the Baby Steps' consolidation ban and snowball are bundled: nothing in the sources shows that rejecting a lower-rate consolidation loan beats using one with the same discipline.

### The "millionaire study" is real but not independent

Ramsey's National Study of Millionaires covers over **10,000** millionaires, fielded November 17, 2017 to January 31, 2018, on a page dated June 11, 2024; it reports 79% received no inheritance, 8 in 10 invested in a 401(k), and 94% live on less than they earn ([Ramsey study](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/retirement/the-national-study-of-millionaires-research); [PDF](https://cdn.ramseysolutions.net/media/company/pr/everyday-millionaires-research/national-study-of-millionaire-new.pdf)). A "2,000" figure circulating in summaries is not a separate study: book excerpts describe a random subsample of about 2,000 with no prior Ramsey connection, and one independent reader estimates the rest came from Ramsey's own audience, raising selection concerns ([Empathic Finance](https://empathicfinance.com/what-does-the-data-say-reading-the-national-study-of-millionaires/); the 2,000/8,000 split is that reader's inference, not Ramsey's document). The data are self-reported, nearly nine years old, and cannot show how many Baby Steps followers did not become millionaires (survivorship). No separate Baby Steps outcome study exists. The only academic work found on Financial Peace University is an Iowa State thesis of 32 couples, self-reported with no control group ([Iowa State](https://dr.lib.iastate.edu/handle/20.500.12876/25655)).

### The return assumptions are the weak leg

Ramsey told a caller in May 2025 that $100 per month at 12% for 40 years grows to about $1.176 million; at 8% it would be roughly $300,000 ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/dave-ramsey-calls-listeners-math-033116346.html)). Over 40-year S&P 500 periods from 1871 to 2012, the average was 9.27% nominal and 6.42% real, and 5.56% of periods exceeded 12% nominal ([DQYDJ](https://dqydj.com/dave-ramseys-investment-advice/)). US stocks from 1926 to 2014 averaged 12.14% arithmetically but 10.14% annualized ([White Coat Investor](https://whitecoatinvestor.com/dave-ramsey-doubles-down-on-his-bad-investment-advice)). The dispute is arithmetic versus compound and nominal versus real, not the raw data. The withdrawal claim is weaker: Ramsey defended an 8% safe withdrawal rate as 12% minus 4% inflation, and Early Retirement Now's 30-year simulations show roughly 56% to 61% failure at 8% ([ERN](https://earlyretirementnow.com/2023/11/12/dave-ramsey-8-percent-withdrawal-rate/)); Of Dollars and Data found about 42% of 30-year periods since 1935 ran out of money at 8% in all-equity portfolios ([Of Dollars and Data](https://ofdollarsanddata.com/how-safe-is-an-8-withdrawal-rate/)). Yahoo reported in April 2026 that Ramsey again suggested retirees withdraw around 8% ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/articles/dave-ramsey-says-withdraw-8-094500759.html)). These matter to anyone reading Step 4 as a retirement plan, but they are irrelevant to the debt-payoff sequence.

### Planner critiques cluster on a handful of rules

Pausing the employer match is the most-cited objection: Robert Johnson (CFA) calls forfeiting the match one of the worst mistakes, and CFPs quoted by Investopedia in November 2025 would contribute at least to the match while paying debt ([Nasdaq/Benzinga](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/dave-ramsey-urges-americans-pause-401k-contributions-should-you); [Investopedia](https://www.investopedia.com/why-financial-experts-say-dave-ramsey-is-wrong-about-pausing-401k-contributions-11852780)). Kitces' order of operations puts capturing the match second, ahead of non-high-interest debt. Ramsey's reply is the "power of focus" framing and the rate-versus-return comparison in the Step 2 FAQ; no direct reply to a named critic was found ([transcript](https://podcasts.happyscribe.com/the-dave-ramsey-show/my-dad-has-hoarded-500-000-in-cash)). Other objections: avoiding credit cards entirely (card-affiliate outlets making that case have an opposite conflict); front-end load funds, which Ramsey defends as a one-time cost ([White Coat Investor](https://whitecoatinvestor.com/dave-ramsey-doubles-down-on-his-bad-investment-advice)); 15-year-only mortgages; and student-loan advice for forgiveness-eligible borrowers ([White Coat Investor on PSLF](https://www.whitecoatinvestor.com/dave-ramseys-bad-advice-about-pslf/), a 2015 letter that predates RAP and SAVE's end).

### Where independent planners agree

Kitces praises the Baby Steps for clear, low-friction actions and behavioral momentum, and says it matters more that people act than that the assumptions be perfect ([Kitces](https://www.kitces.com/blog/checklist-style-financial-planning-dave-ramsey-baby-steps-money-guy-show-order-of-operations/)). Planners endorse 3-to-6-month emergency funds and minimizing debt, and even math critics accept that paying off a 14%-plus card is correct ([Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/2020/09/15/4-things-dave-ramsey-gets-wrong-about-investing)). A fair summary: a good behavioral scaffold with shaky technical assumptions.
