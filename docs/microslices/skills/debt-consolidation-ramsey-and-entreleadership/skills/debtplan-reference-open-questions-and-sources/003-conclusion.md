## Conclusion

The most useful thing the research changes is the question: not "which of the three is best" but "which risk can this situation afford." Consolidation is cheap insurance against interest and expensive insurance against behavior; Ramsey's method is cheap in dollars and expensive in income requirements; bankruptcy is expensive in credit and cheap in finality. South Carolina's wage-garnishment bar, three-year limitations period and exempt-income rules lower the practical leverage of unsecured creditors, which is precisely why converting card debt into secured or retirement-funded debt deserves more suspicion here than a rate comparison alone suggests, and why a small payment on an old balance can cost more than it looks.

The second shift is about confidence. Almost every strong claim in this field, from a snowball "working" to a millionaire study "proving" the steps to an 8% withdrawal rate being safe, rests on company data, self-reported surveys, or arithmetic that independent sources dispute. The reliable core is smaller and more practical: protect the floor, know who can reach which assets, price every option with fees included, and let a South Carolina bankruptcy attorney and a licensed nonprofit counselor check the specifics before anything irreversible, whether that is signing a secured loan, cashing out retirement, or stopping payments.
