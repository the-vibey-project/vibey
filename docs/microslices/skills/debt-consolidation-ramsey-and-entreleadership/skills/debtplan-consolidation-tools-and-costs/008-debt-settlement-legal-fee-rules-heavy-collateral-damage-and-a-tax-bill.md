## Debt settlement: legal fee rules, heavy collateral damage, and a tax bill

Settlement firms charge **15% to 25% of enrolled debt** over two to four years ([NFCC](https://www.nfcc.org/blog/which-debt-repayment-method-is-right-for-you-a-closer-look-at-debt-settlement/); [NerdWallet](https://www.nerdwallet.com/article/loans/personal-loans/is-debt-settlement-a-good-idea)). The FTC's rule is that a company cannot collect fees before settling your debt, and savings should sit in an account you own and can withdraw from ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-get-out-debt)). NFCC says for-profit settlement is not recommended "under any circumstances." Programs usually require stopping payments, so late fees accrue, creditors may sue, and a judgment could lead to wage garnishment or a lien where state law allows. NFCC estimates score drops "exceed 100 points in most cases," while GreenPath cites an NCLC average of 161 points; neither is a FICO or bureau figure. Two industry-linked success statistics cannot be reconciled: NFCC cites a study where clients settle 43% of accounts by month 36 and pay over 78% of the original balance, whereas a 2023 industry-commissioned report claims average net savings of 31.9% of what clients owed ([NerdWallet](https://www.nerdwallet.com/article/loans/personal-loans/is-debt-settlement-a-good-idea)). Ramsey calls settlement "a scam" and says firms typically charge 20% to 25% ([Ramsey](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/what-is-debt-relief)).

Recent enforcement shows the pattern to avoid. In July 2025 the FTC filed a complaint against a group operating as "Accelerated Debt," alleging false claims of cutting debt by up to 75% or 85%, impersonation of card issuers and agencies, and illegal advance fees; the FTC estimated about $100 million was taken. In April 2026 a court entered a temporary restraining order against an alleged student-loan relief scheme that had collected at least $8.8 million. These are allegations at the stage of the sources read ([Consumer Finance Monitor](https://www.consumerfinancemonitor.com/2025/07/28/ftc-takes-action-against-accelerated-debt-for-running-a-debt-relief-scam/); [Orrick](https://infobytes.orrick.com/2026-04-24/ftc-obtains-temporary-restraining-order-against-alleged-student-loan-debt-relief-scheme/)). The FTC's March 2026 alert lists the red flags: upfront fees, guaranteed results, and unsolicited calls or texts ([FTC](https://www.consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2026/03/looking-debt-relief-heres-how-avoid-scam)).

**Forgiven debt is generally taxable.** The IRS treats canceled debt as ordinary income for the year of cancellation, reportable even without a Form 1099-C. Debt discharged in a Title 11 bankruptcy case is excluded, and an insolvency exclusion applies "to the extent" liabilities exceeded the fair market value of assets immediately beforehand, claimed on Form 982 (IRS example: $10,000 liabilities, $7,000 assets, $5,000 canceled makes $3,000 excludable and $2,000 taxable) ([IRS Pub. 4681](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p4681); [IRS Topic 431](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc431)). The home-debt exclusion for qualified principal residence indebtedness applied only to discharges before January 1, 2026 (or written arrangements before that date), and the research found no evidence Congress extended it, though H.R. 917 is pending ([IRS Form 982 instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i982); [Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr917/BILLS-119hr917ih.htm)). The $600 reporting threshold comes from a secondary source only.
