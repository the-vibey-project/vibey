## What the evidence says about the no-debt rule

No study compares debt-free and leveraged small-firm outcomes, so the case is argued from adjacent data. The Federal Reserve's 2026 report on employer firms found 69.6% had outstanding debt, 80.8% of those applying for financing were approved, 59% of indebted firms used a personal guarantee, and 47.3% were profitable at end of 2024 ([Fed SBCS employer report](https://www.fedsmallbusiness.org/-/media/project/clevelandfedtenant/fsbsite/reports/2026/2026-report-on-employer-firms/2026-report-on-employer-firms.pdf)). The nonemployer report (2025 survey, released July 7, 2026) found about half of nonemployers had no debt, 31% did not regularly use external financing, and 64% used owner personal funds to meet financial challenges ([Fed SBCS nonemployer](https://www.fedsmallbusiness.org/reports/survey/2026/2026-report-on-nonemployer-firms)). Startup-financing surveys show personal and family savings dominate: Kauffman found 64.4% of employer firms used them in 2016, with 9.1% using personal credit cards with balances and 6.3% home equity; in the Kauffman Firm Survey 44% had no debt in year one yet 48% used personal debt ([Kauffman 2019](https://www.kauffman.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/Access-To-Capital_2019.pdf); [Kauffman KFS](https://www.kauffman.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/kfs_08.pdf)). That blurs "no debt" for founders, because personal cards and home equity count as personal debt used for business. A Census working paper found that higher invested capital (not specifically debt) is associated with nonemployer survival, and that incorporated forms survive more than sole proprietorships, a correlation that may reflect owner commitment rather than legal form ([Census CES-WP-25-26](https://www2.census.gov/ces/wp/2025/CES-WP-25-26.pdf)).

Survival data supply the reserve rationale. For establishments born in March 2013, 79.6% survived one year, 50.6% five years, and 34.7% ten years ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2024/34-7-percent-of-business-establishments-born-in-2013-were-still-operating-in-2023.htm)); closures are not necessarily failures. BLS has no nonemployer series; the closest substitutes put one-year inactive-or-exit rates for nonemployers at 36.4% for sole proprietors, 26.1% for sole proprietors with EINs, 20.3% for S corporations and 12.5% for partnerships in 2018 to 2019 ([NBER w34252](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w34252/w34252.pdf)), which should not be quoted as failure rates. Only 0.5% of nonemployer startups became employers within seven years ([Census](https://www2.census.gov/library/working-papers/2024/econ/garage-entrepreneurs-just-self-employed-investigation-into-nonemployer-entrepreneurship.pdf)). Nonemployer firms numbered **30,427,808 in 2023**, with nearly $1.8 trillion in receipts, about 6.4% of GDP ([Census](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2026/05/small-business-week.html)); dividing gives roughly $59,000 per establishment (self-computed, not a Census statistic). An even-handed reading: no-debt is a risk-reduction rule that limits personal-guarantee exposure at the cost of slower capital-intensive growth, and the cost is small for low-capital solo service businesses. A Substack critique argues the rule suits salaried households better than owners, but it offers opinion without data ([Commentary on Wealth](https://commentaryonwealth.substack.com/p/debt-free-doesnt-mean-stress-free)). For an LLC, the liability shield generally does not protect against a personal guarantee (a general principle to verify with counsel).
