## §16. ⚠️ The Canon Problem

**⚠️ The Western canon was assembled by particular people, in particular institutions, with
particular commitments — and that is a historical claim, not a polemical one.**
- **⚠️ Vasari essentially invented art history as a genre in 1550, and did so as a
  narrative of Florentine progress culminating in Michelangelo.** **The
  progress-toward-naturalism frame has been shaping the field ever since.**
- **⚠️ The craft/art hierarchy** — **textiles, ceramics, and decorative work relegated
  below painting and sculpture — tracks gendered and class divisions of labour closely
  enough that the correlation is hard to dismiss.**
- **⚠️ "Why have there been no great women artists?" (Nochlin, 1971) is the essential
  text**, and **its answer is the sophisticated one: not that women lacked genius, but
  that the institutional conditions of artistic production — academy access, life-drawing
  from the nude, apprenticeship, patronage, guild membership — were structurally
  closed.** ⚠️ **The question is answered by examining institutions, not by finding
  overlooked geniuses, though there were plenty of those too.**
- **⚠️ Non-Western art was long filtered through categories that distorted it** —
  **"primitive," ethnographic display rather than aesthetic display, and the anonymity
  imposed on makers whose names were simply never recorded by collectors.**

**⚠️ The honest position**: **canon revision is ordinary scholarly work and it has been
enormously productive.** ⚠️ **The genuine methodological risk on the other side — applying
present-day categories anachronistically, or judging works solely by their politics — is
also real and is criticized from within the field.** **Both cautions are live, and a
reader should hold both.**
