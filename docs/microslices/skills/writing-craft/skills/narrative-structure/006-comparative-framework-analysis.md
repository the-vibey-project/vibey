## Comparative framework analysis

### Aristotle's three acts

Historically, the hard three-act paradigm with explicit plot points was codified by Syd Field in *Screenplay* (1979), not Aristotle. Aristotle in *Poetics* says only that a whole has a beginning, middle, and end, and that a unified plot is one where each element follows from what came before by causal necessity. The three-act framing is a useful cognitive simplification: Departure = Act I, Initiation = Act II, Return = Act III.

### Freytag's five-act pyramid

Freytag's *Die Technik des Dramas* (1863) proposed five parts: exposition → rising action → climax → falling action → denouement. Designed for classical tragedy. **Important distinction**: in Freytag, the climax occurs near the structural midpoint. In Vogler, the Ordeal (structural equivalent of climax) sits in the late second act. Freytag is most useful as a **diagnostic for whether tension is actually rising** and as a frame for tragic arcs where the protagonist does not return transformed.

### Fichtean Curve

Popularized in John Gardner's *The Art of Fiction* (1983). Dispenses with extended exposition; stacks escalating crises with brief regroupings. Less a competitor to the Hero's Journey than a **tension management technique** to apply within the Road of Trials. The Vogler "Tests, Allies, and Enemies" stage is most successfully written as a Fichtean sequence.

**Note**: the attribution to Johann Gottlieb Fichte is contested — Fichte's philosophy was unrelated to storytelling, and experts on Fichte agree the German philosopher probably had nothing to do with this concept. Use the technique; treat the attribution skeptically.

### Propp's 31 functions

Vladimir Propp's *Morphology of the Folktale* (1928) is **descriptively more rigorous than Campbell** and methodologically more honest. 31 fixed functions, strict sequential order (though any may be omitted), seven character "spheres of action." Derived from structural analysis of Russian folktales.

**Where Propp surpasses Campbell**: granular cause-and-effect; functions Campbell does not name (Absentation, Interdiction, Violation, Reconnaissance, Trickery); the **False Hero** (no clean Campbellian equivalent — critical for analyzing mistaken identity plots and political fables). Where Campbell surpasses Propp: psychological depth. Propp has no equivalent for the Jungian substrate.

### Dan Harmon's Story Circle (8 steps)

Reduces the monomyth to eight movements arranged as a circle: You → Need → Go → Search → Find → Take → Return → Change. Developed by Harmon at Channel 101 (Los Angeles short film festival) to help directors who said they were unable to write plots for TV shows.

Strength: speed of application and emphasis on character desire as the engine. Weakness: under-specifies the Ordeal and tends to collapse Resurrection and Return.

### Murdock's Heroine's Journey

Maureen Murdock's *The Heroine's Journey* (Shambhala, 1990) — developed after Murdock showed Campbell her framework and he responded: "Women don't need to make the journey. In the whole mythological tradition the woman is there. All she has to do is to realize that she's the place that people are trying to get to."

Murdock's ten stages reframe the journey as **descent into the feminine and integration** rather than outward conquest: separation from the feminine → identification with the masculine → Road of Trials → finding the boon of illusory success → awakening to spiritual aridity → initiation and descent to the goddess → reconnection with the feminine → healing the mother-daughter split → healing the wounded masculine → integration.

**Her core formulation**: "The feminine journey is about going down deep into soul, healing and reclaiming, while the masculine journey is up and out, to spirit."

**Practical application for non-fiction**: for stories of organizational transformation, leadership reinvention, and recovery from professional crisis, Murdock's descent-and-integration model often works better than the Campbellian outward-conquest model.
