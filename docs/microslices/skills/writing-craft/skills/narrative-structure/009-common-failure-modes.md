## Common failure modes

**Mechanical application**: placing required beats at the right page count without earning them on the level of emotional truth. The result scans correctly and feels dead. **Corrective**: start from the Ordeal. Ask what specific psychological transformation the Ordeal must produce, then work backward to define what the Ordinary World must establish and what flaw the Call must expose.

**Misidentifying the Ordeal as the Climax**: the Ordeal is the symbolic death in the *middle* of the second act, not the final showdown. The final showdown is the Resurrection, which restages the Ordeal at higher stakes. Stories that put the Ordeal at the climax feel anticlimactic because the protagonist's transformation arrives too late to be tested in action.

**Weak Refusal of the Call**: signals a flat protagonist. Even an eager hero requires expression of risk. The Refusal is where the protagonist's specific flaw is named to the reader.

**Mishandling the Return**: the most common structural error in modern fiction and screenwriting. Campbell notes that "the responsibility has been frequently refused." A botched Return has either: the Ordinary World is not visibly changed by the boon, or the Hero is not visibly changed by the return. Both must show on the page.

**Threshold Guardian misuse**: treating every obstacle as a Shadow flattens the structure. Threshold Guardians test commitment; they do not destroy. Reserve the Shadow for the Ordeal.

**Mentor disposal timing**: kill the Mentor too early (Hero is unequipped) or too late (Hero cannot face the Ordeal alone). Canonical timing: at the threshold between the second act's first half and its second half, immediately before the Approach to the Inmost Cave.

**Over-literalizing metaphorical stages**: the Belly of the Whale does not require a literal whale. Campbell's stages are symbolic positions in the psyche; the writer's job is to find an event that performs that symbolic function in the world of the story.

**The false Hero trap in non-fiction**: the author or brand casts themselves as the Hero. This violates the rhetorical contract. The StoryBrand inversion is the corrective.
