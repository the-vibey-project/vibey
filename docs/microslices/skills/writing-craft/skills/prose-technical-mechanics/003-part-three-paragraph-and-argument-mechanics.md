## PART THREE — PARAGRAPH AND ARGUMENT MECHANICS

### 1. Paragraph Structure

A paragraph is one idea, developed. The unit is psychological as much as logical: it is what a reader can hold in working memory as a single movement of thought.

- **Topic sentence:** states the paragraph's argument, not just its subject; usually appears first, but skilled writers delay it (specific-to-general) or imply it (paragraphs of mood or scene)
- **Development:** evidence, example, analysis
- **Clincher/transition:** closes the unit and/or opens to the next

The first sentence inherits paragraph-level topic; the last sentence carries paragraph-level stress. These mirror the sentence-level given-new contract at a larger scale.

### 2. Toulmin Argument Structure

Stephen Toulmin's model atomizes the working argument into six functional parts:

- **Claim** — the proposition to be defended
- **Data / Grounds** — the evidence offered in support
- **Warrant** — the inferential principle linking data to claim
- **Backing** — further evidence supporting the warrant when contested
- **Qualifier** — the modal expression (*probably*, *usually*, *in most cases*) limiting the claim's scope
- **Rebuttal** — the conditions under which the claim would not hold

**The warrant is what most academic and professional writers omit** — the unstated assumption connecting data to claim. Persuasive writers learn either to state warrants explicitly (when they cannot be assumed) or to leave them implicit (when they will land more powerfully unspoken).

### 3. Classical Argumentative Arrangement

The classical Roman oration as codified by Cicero and Quintilian has six parts:

1. **Exordium** — introduction that secures attention and goodwill
2. **Narratio** — statement of the facts of the case
3. **Partitio / Divisio** — division; what the argument will and will not address
4. **Confirmatio** — proof; the positive case
5. **Refutatio** — refutation of opposing arguments
6. **Peroratio** — conclusion that summarizes and stirs the emotions

The structure survives because it answers a permanent question — how does a writer move a reader from non-belief to belief? — and the answer is invariant: orient, inform, prove, address objections, close.

### 4. Counterargument and Concession

**Straw man** — a weakened version of the opposing view that is easy to defeat. The amateur's approach.

**Steel man** — the strongest possible version of the opposing view, defeated on its best terms. The professional's approach.

**Concession signals** (*to be sure*, *admittedly*, *one must grant*, *critics will rightly object*) tell the reader the writer has considered the opposing case. Done well, concession is among the most powerful rhetorical moves available because it disarms hostility before the substantive case is made.

**The "yes, but" pivot:** acknowledges the opponent's point and turns. *Yes, the policy has reduced unemployment; but it has done so at a cost that...*
