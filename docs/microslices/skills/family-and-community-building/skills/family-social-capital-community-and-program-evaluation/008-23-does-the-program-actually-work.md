## §23. ⚠️ Does the Program Actually Work?

**⚠️ Community and family programs are dense with plausible interventions that don't
survive evaluation, and the field has learned some hard lessons.**
```
⚠️ THE CAUTIONARY CASES worth knowing
  Scared Straight — ⚠️ randomized evaluations found it INCREASED offending.
    A program that felt obviously right and made things worse
  D.A.R.E. (original) — ⚠️ repeatedly evaluated, no effect on drug use
  ⚠️ Some peer-group interventions for at-risk youth show DEVIANCY TRAINING:
    grouping troubled youth can worsen outcomes through peer reinforcement
```
**⚠️ The generalizable lesson: plausibility is not evidence, and untested programs can
cause harm rather than merely wasting money.**
**⚠️ What good evaluation requires:**
```
⚠️ A THEORY OF CHANGE stated before you start — what changes what, and how
⚠️ A COMPARISON GROUP. ⚠️ Pre-post with no control is nearly uninterpretable
   because of regression to the mean and maturation
⚠️ Distinguish OUTPUTS (people served) from OUTCOMES (lives changed)
⚠️ Attend to SELECTION — who enrolls, and who DROPS OUT (differential
   attrition destroys more evaluations than any other single problem)
⚠️ Measure implementation fidelity — did the program actually happen?
⚠️ Report null results. ⚠️ The field's file-drawer problem is severe
```
**⚠️ Realistic expectations**: ⚠️ **effect sizes for community interventions are typically
small, and scaled-up versions almost always underperform the original trial** — **the
"voltage drop" between efficacy and effectiveness is the norm, not an anomaly.**
**⚠️ And the participatory point, which is both ethical and practical**: **programs designed
with the community they serve have better take-up and fewer of the failure modes in §21.**
