## §20. Congregations and Voluntary Associations

**⚠️ Religious congregations remain the single largest category of voluntary association in
the US, and their civic role is well documented and often understated in secular
discussions of community.**
**⚠️ What the research supports:**
- **⚠️ Attendance — more than belief — predicts volunteering, charitable giving (⚠️ including
  to secular causes), civic participation and social support.** **The mechanism appears to
  be largely the network and the recurring gathering** (§17's repetition point) — **which
  is itself a finding worth noting.**
- ⚠️ **Congregations supply bonding capital reliably and bridging capital variably** —
  **some are highly homogeneous, some are among the few genuinely cross-class institutions
  left in a community.**
- **⚠️ They are disproportionately important civic infrastructure for lower-income and
  minority communities**, **and historically central to social movements.**
- ⚠️ **Selection is a live confound throughout** (§1 → `family-reading-claims-attachment-and-couple-relationships`): **joiners differ from non-joiners.**
  **Longitudinal work suggests some genuine effect, and the magnitude is contested.**

**⚠️ The trend, and its civic consequence**: **the Christian share of US adults has fallen
from roughly 78% two decades ago to around 62–65%, with the religiously unaffiliated
rising to roughly a quarter to 30%** (§24.2 → `family-reference`). ⚠️ **The relevant point for THIS document is
not theological — it's that congregations performed civic and social functions that no
comparable institution has replaced at similar scale.** **Whatever one's view of the
underlying religious question, that's an infrastructure gap.**
**⚠️ Other voluntary associations**: **unions, service clubs, fraternal organizations,
sports leagues, mutual aid, parent groups.** ⚠️ **The class divide here is significant and
growing** (§24.2 → `family-reference`).
