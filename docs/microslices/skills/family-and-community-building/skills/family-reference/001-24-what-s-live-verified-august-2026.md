## §24. What's Live — verified August 2026

### 24.1 ⚠️ Loneliness: strong framing, weaker intervention evidence
**⚠️ The policy attention is real and recent, and there is a caveat in the peer-reviewed
literature that almost never appears in the coverage.**

- **⚠️ The US Surgeon General's 2023 advisory declared loneliness a public health crisis**,
  **stating that social isolation and loneliness are independent risk factors for
  cardiovascular disease, dementia, depression and premature mortality**, ⚠️ **and famously
  comparing the mortality impact of social disconnection to smoking up to 15 cigarettes a
  day.**
- **⚠️ WHO declared loneliness a global public health concern in 2023 and launched a
  Commission on Social Connection**, **whose June 2025 report is cited as finding
  1 in 6 people worldwide experiencing persistent loneliness, linked to an estimated
  871,000+ deaths annually.**
- **Commonly cited risk figures: ⚠️ ~29% increased premature death risk from social
  isolation and ~26% from loneliness; ~29% increased heart disease risk and ~32% stroke
  risk.**
- **⚠️ It is not primarily an old-age problem.** **A 2026 eight-country study found nearly
  half of young adults reporting loneliness**, **and the researchers noted that much prior
  work relied on pre-2020 data or focused on adults over 50.**

> **⚠️ GOTCHA — here is the part the coverage omits, and it should change how you read
> everything above.** ⚠️ **A 2025 interdisciplinary literature review in a peer-reviewed
> journal states plainly that "meaningfully effective interventions for social isolation
> or loneliness have yet to be identified," and that the consensus that social connection
> is a human need equivalent to food, water and shelter "is unclear."**
> **⚠️ So: the ASSOCIATIONS are well replicated; the FRAMING as an equivalent basic need
> is a rhetorical position rather than a settled scientific one; and WHAT TO DO ABOUT IT
> remains genuinely unresolved.** ⚠️ **Reviews of interventions for older adults similarly
> describe significant knowledge gaps.**
> **⚠️ Also note reverse causation runs hard here** (§1 → `family-reading-claims-attachment-and-couple-relationships`): **depression and poor health
> reduce social contact, and much of the evidence cannot separate the directions.**
> **The "15 cigarettes" comparison in particular is a communication device derived from
> meta-analytic mortality estimates, not a measured equivalence.**

**⚠️ Practical read**: **take the problem seriously and treat confident intervention claims
sceptically.** **The better-supported moves are structural** (§17 → `family-social-capital-community-and-program-evaluation`, §19 → `family-social-capital-community-and-program-evaluation`) — **increasing
proximity, repetition and shared purpose** — **rather than awareness campaigns.**

### 24.2 ⚠️ The social connection trend data
**⚠️ The decline is measured, not merely asserted, and the newest data continues it.**

- **⚠️ The American Time Use Survey for 2025, released in July 2026, shows the share of US
  adults who communicated or socialized with others on an average day falling from 38% in
  2015 to 30% in 2025.**
- **⚠️ Average time spent socializing has fallen from about 45 to about 35 minutes per day
  over roughly two decades** — **and ⚠️ the decline is steepest among the young: 15- to
  24-year-olds went from about an hour a day to about 35 minutes.**
- **⚠️ Face-to-face socializing is reported down roughly 20% over two decades, with the
  decline exceeding 35% for unmarried men and people under 25.**
- **⚠️ Religious affiliation continues to fall** (§20 → `family-social-capital-community-and-program-evaluation`): **Christian identification from
  roughly 78% two decades ago to about 62–65%, with "nones" rising from around 6% to
  nearly 30%.**
- **⚠️ Time spent at home has risen substantially**, **which is the spatial correlate of
  all of the above.**

> **⚠️ GOTCHA — the most important finding here is about INEQUALITY, not the average, and
> it gets much less attention.** ⚠️ **The class divide in American social capital has
> grown: Americans without four-year degrees participate less in community life, are less
> civically active, have fewer close friends and less social support than degree-holders.**
> **⚠️ The mechanism is specific and worth understanding — three institutions (marriage,
> religious organizations, and labour unions) historically supplied robust social ties to
> Americans without degrees, and membership in all three has declined much more steeply in
> that group.** **⚠️ So this is not a uniform cultural drift; it's a concentrated loss of
> particular institutional scaffolding for particular people** (§17 → `family-social-capital-community-and-program-evaluation`'s point about adult
> friendship requiring structure).

**⚠️ An honest counterweight**: **Pew's 2026 work on civic engagement cautions that broad
claims about Americans being "engaged" or "disengaged" are difficult to assess**, ⚠️ **and
that participation has partly shifted form rather than simply vanishing** — **private
conversation rather than public posting, episodic rather than membership-based
involvement.** **Volunteering time, on some measures, has been comparatively stable.**
⚠️ **The strong version of the decline thesis is contested on measurement grounds; the
time-use trend itself is not.**
