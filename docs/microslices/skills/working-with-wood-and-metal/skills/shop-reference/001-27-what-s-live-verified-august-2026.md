## §27. What's Live — verified August 2026

### 27.1 ⚠️ US table saw regulation: a reversal
**⚠️ Directly relevant to §9 → `shop-woodworking-tools-sharpening-joinery-and-finishing`, and the outcome went the opposite way to what the sector
expected.**

- **⚠️ The background**: **SawStop petitioned the CPSC in 2003 for a performance standard on
  blade-contact injuries.** ⚠️ **A 2017 proposed rule, supplemented in 2023, would have
  required table saws to limit the depth of cut to 3.5 mm or less when a test probe
  contacts the spinning blade at a radial approach rate of 1 m/s.** ⚠️ **The 3.5 mm figure
  derives from UL's identification of 4 mm as the threshold between simple and severe
  finger lacerations, minus 0.5 mm for the epidermis — below it, cuts should not damage
  nerves or arteries requiring surgery.**
- **⚠️ CPSC testing showed the standard was achievable**: ⚠️ **SawStop saws produced depths
  of cut from 1.5 to 2.8 mm and Bosch REAXX from about 1.8 to 2.5 mm.**
- **⚠️ THE REVERSAL: on 20 August 2025 the CPSC announced it would WITHDRAW the rule**,
  ⚠️ **as part of dropping "several existing and pending rulemakings that no longer align
  with agency priorities, and which fail to advance safety" — with the table saw standard
  leading the list of six.**

> **⚠️ GOTCHA — the patent dimension is why this was never a simple safety question, and
> it's worth understanding before taking a side.** ⚠️ **Critics argued the rule would
> effectively mandate a patented technology, creating a government-mandated monopoly;
> SawStop sued Bosch, and in 2017 the ITC prohibited Bosch from importing REAXX saws for
> infringing SawStop patents — removing the main alternative from the US market.**
> ⚠️ **SawStop also filed suit against Felder in 2024 over the alternative technology CPSC
> had cited.** **⚠️ A bipartisan bill was introduced to bar any such rule until at least
> five years after the relevant patents expire, which one analysis calculated could mean
> 2043.**
> **⚠️ Note the counter-argument is equally concrete: CPSC estimated the rule would have
> prevented or mitigated over 49,000 injuries annually, and its own 2019 analysis found
> NO reduction in table saw injuries from 2010 to 2018 despite the voluntary modular
> blade guard standard adopted in 2010.**

**⚠️ The practical upshot for anyone with a table saw**: ⚠️ **AIM technology remains
available and voluntary rather than required, it is not being retrofitted or mandated,
and — importantly — **it addresses BLADE CONTACT, not KICKBACK**.** ⚠️ **The riving knife,
push stick and stance in §9 → `shop-woodworking-tools-sharpening-joinery-and-finishing` remain the controls that matter, and they cost nothing.**
**⚠️ Scale note: CPSC estimated roughly 33,400 emergency-department-treated table saw
injuries in 2015, and around 54,800 medically treated blade-contact injuries annually in
earlier analysis.**

### 27.2 ⚠️ Welding fume: the numbers behind the ventilation advice
**⚠️ §19 → `shop-welding-forging-heat-treatment-and-shop-setup`'s PPE paragraph, quantified — and the figures explain why casual stainless welding
in an unventilated garage is a genuinely bad idea.**

- **⚠️ Hexavalent chromium — Cr(VI) — forms when arc welding heats chromium-bearing metal,
  principally STAINLESS STEEL.** ⚠️ **IARC classifies all hexavalent chromium compounds as
  Group 1 carcinogens.**
- **⚠️ The OSHA permissible exposure limit is 5 µg/m³ as an 8-hour time-weighted average
  (29 CFR 1910.1026), with an ACTION LEVEL of 2.5 µg/m³ that triggers monitoring and
  medical surveillance.** ⚠️ **That PEL is a tenfold reduction from the previous limit and
  is reported as 1,000 times lower than the PEL for general welding fume.**
  ⚠️ **NIOSH's recommended exposure limit is reported at 0.2 µg/m³ — 25× more protective
  than OSHA's.**
- **⚠️ The exposure risk is not theoretical.** ⚠️ **An estimated 558,000 US workers are
  exposed to airborne Cr(VI); welders with high exposure are reported to face an 85%
  increased lung cancer risk, and stainless steel welders nearly double the general
  population's risk per meta-analysis.**
- **⚠️ How fast you reach it**: ⚠️ **one industry source quotes an air quality specialist
  saying that MIG or stick welding on stainless for more than about ten minutes will
  overexpose you — "the limit is that low."** ⚠️ **Treat that as illustrative rather than
  precise, but the order of magnitude is the point.**

> **⚠️ GOTCHA — the control hierarchy applies here exactly as in §2 → `shop-materials-safety-reading-wood-and-stock`, and OSHA is explicit
> about the order.** ⚠️ **LOCAL EXHAUST VENTILATION at the source is the primary engineering
> control, supplemented by general dilution ventilation — and OSHA has determined
> engineering and work-practice controls must be the PRIMARY means, not respirators.**
> ⚠️ **General dilution ventilation "cannot replace LEV for high-hazard operations."**
> **⚠️ Note also a documented trap: the safety data sheet for a chromium-containing alloy
> will list chromium in its neutral state, which is NOT a carcinogen — the Cr(VI) is
> CREATED by the arc.** ⚠️ **So an SDS that looks clean does not mean the operation is.**

**⚠️ The other fume hazards in the same breath**: ⚠️ **manganese (neurological effects,
which is why ACGIH's value is set to mitigate brain damage), nickel, and iron oxide
(⚠️ OSHA PEL 10 mg/m³ — the least toxic common case, which is why mild steel is the
comparatively benign scenario).** ⚠️ **European practice sets a general respirable dust
limit of 1.25 mg/m³ with 0.010 mg/m³ for Cr(VI) and 0.006 mg/m³ for nickel compounds
(TRGS 900), and applies a MINIMIZATION principle for carcinogens — exposure reduced as far
as technically possible even when the limit is formally met.**
**⚠️ For the hobbyist, the honest translation**: ⚠️ **weld mild steel with your head out of
the plume and a fan drawing across; ⚠️ do not weld stainless or galvanized without real
extraction and respiratory protection; and treat "it's only a short job" as exactly the
reasoning that produces cumulative exposure.**
