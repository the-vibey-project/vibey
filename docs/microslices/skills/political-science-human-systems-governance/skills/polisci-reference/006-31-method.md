## §31. Method

**§1–§25 → `polisci-what-the-field-establishes-power-the-state-and-theory`, `polisci-collective-action-commons-social-choice-and-voting`, `polisci-constitutional-design-electoral-systems-and-institutions`, `polisci-bureaucracy-parties-voter-behaviour-and-polarization`, `polisci-regimes-state-capacity-corruption-ir-and-field-problems` rests on the discipline's standard curriculum** — **collective action and social
choice theory, comparative institutional analysis, the behaviour literature, IR schools,
and the methodological self-criticism.** ⚠️ **The formal results (Arrow, Condorcet,
Gibbard-Satterthwaite) are proofs and need no verification; the empirical findings are
reported at the confidence §1 → `polisci-what-the-field-establishes-power-the-state-and-theory` assigns them.**

**Two searches were run in August 2026**, deliberately paired: ⚠️ **the current state of
democracy measurement, and the live scholarly dispute about whether those measurements are
trustworthy.** **⚠️ Reporting the first without the second would have been the easier file
and a worse one.**

**⚠️ A note on how I handled contested political questions.** ⚠️ **This document reports
what political scientists have found and where they disagree; it does not advance my own
positions on contested political or normative questions, and where a normative dispute is
live — judicial review, electoral system choice, federalism's merits, what legitimacy
requires — I have tried to give the strongest version of more than one side rather than
adjudicating.** ⚠️ **Where a finding involves a specific country's current politics, I have
attributed it to the source that produced it rather than asserting it in my own voice.**

**Confidence.** **High** in §5–§7 → `polisci-collective-action-commons-social-choice-and-voting`, which are the sections I'd most want read. ⚠️ **Collective
action logic explains an enormous amount of political structure, and Olson's asymmetry —
that concentrated interests organize more easily than diffuse ones — is the single most
useful thing here for making sense of why democratic outcomes so often disappoint
majorities.** ⚠️ **Ostrom's correction to Hardin belongs beside it, because "privatize or
nationalize" is a false binary that a great deal of real institutional design escapes.**

**High** in §1 → `polisci-what-the-field-establishes-power-the-state-and-theory` and §25 → `polisci-regimes-state-capacity-corruption-ir-and-field-problems`'s framing, which I'd defend as the honest position: ⚠️ **you cannot
randomize countries, the interesting variables are endogenous, and confident causal claims
about national outcomes deserve more scepticism than they usually get.**

**High** on §26.1's reporting of what V-Dem says, which comes from V-Dem's own report and
press release and is consistent across coverage: ⚠️ **the 2026 report's scale, the 1978
comparison, the 92/87 and 74%/7% figures, the 44 autocratizing versus 18 democratizing, and
the countries named.** ⚠️ **I have framed all of it as V-Dem's findings rather than as
established fact, and included BTI's notably different picture, because index disagreement
is itself evidence about how much weight any single number carries.**

**High** on §26.2's existence and content — ⚠️ **the Little and Meng article, the V-Dem-side
response, and the "streetlight" critique are all published in PS: Political Science &
Politics and are readable in full.** ⚠️ **I have taken care not to let either side stand as
the conclusion: the critics explicitly decline to claim decline is fictional, and the
responders raise real objections to using incumbent electoral performance to detect
within-democracy erosion.**
**⚠️ The position I'd defend is the one in the section: direction supported by multiple
lines of evidence, magnitude genuinely uncertain, and precise index figures carrying wider
error bars than their decimal places suggest.** ⚠️ **That is less satisfying than either
confident story, and I think it is what the evidence actually supports.**
