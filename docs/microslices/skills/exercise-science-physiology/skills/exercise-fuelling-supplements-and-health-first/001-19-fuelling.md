## §19. Fuelling

> **⚠️ This section describes physiology, not a prescription.** **Individual needs vary
> with size, activity, health status and goals, and anyone with a medical condition or a
> complicated relationship with food should work with a registered dietitian rather than
> a reference document** (§21).

**⚠️ Energy availability is the foundational concept** — **energy intake minus exercise
energy expenditure, relative to fat-free mass** — ⚠️ **and chronically low availability
drives the problems in §21 regardless of body composition.**
**⚠️ Protein**: ⚠️ **the research consensus for people training for strength or size sits
in roughly the 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day range, with limited additional benefit above that.**
**Distribution across meals appears to matter modestly; ⚠️ total daily intake matters far
more than timing.**
> **⚠️ GOTCHA — the "anabolic window" is essentially a myth as popularly stated.**
> ⚠️ **The claim that protein must be consumed within 30–60 minutes post-training does not
> hold up; the practically relevant window is many hours wide, and total daily intake
> dominates.** **⚠️ It was a surrogate-endpoint conclusion** (§1 → `exercise-physiology-muscle-energy-and-body-systems`) — **built on acute
> muscle protein synthesis rather than measured long-term outcomes.**

**⚠️ Carbohydrate** — **the limiting fuel for high-intensity and prolonged work; glycogen
availability affects performance and training quality.** ⚠️ **Carbohydrate needs scale with
training volume and intensity, and low-carbohydrate approaches impair high-intensity
performance specifically.**
**⚠️ Fat** — **essential; very low intakes affect hormonal function** (§21).
**⚠️ Around-session fuelling matters most when sessions are long, frequent, or close
together** — **and matters very little for a single moderate session in a well-fed person.**
