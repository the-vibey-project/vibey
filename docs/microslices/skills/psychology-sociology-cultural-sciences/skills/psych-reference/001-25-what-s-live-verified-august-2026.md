## §25. What's Live — verified August 2026

### 25.1 ⚠️ Where replication reform actually stands
**⚠️ The reforms work, and the field has not finished adopting them.**
- **⚠️ The strongest positive evidence remains the preregistration result** (§1.2 → `psych-epistemic-situation-and-replication-reform`):
  **four labs, 16 studies, 64 replications, 55 successes — an 86% replication rate**,
  against **the 30–70% typical of large-scale replication projects.**
- **⚠️ Recent estimates of the broader literature sit around half.** **A 2026 analysis
  reported roughly 50% of originally significant claims replicating; another put it at
  55.1%.** **Compare the Open Science Collaboration's 36% in 2015.**
- **⚠️ How to interpret ~50% is itself genuinely contested, and the debate is worth
  knowing.** **An optimistic reading: most original studies detected effects in the right
  direction and the problem is inflated effect sizes.** **A pessimistic reading: the
  central problems remain — implausibly high rates of significant results in journals,
  success rates inconsistent with typical statistical power, and repeated failures of
  headline findings.** ⚠️ **There's also a live argument that the "right" replication rate
  depends on your philosophy of science: a field consolidating phenomena should expect a
  higher rate than one aggressively testing risky theories.**
- **⚠️ A structural analysis argued that pre-reform parameter ranges IMPLY a replication
  rate of about 36% — matching what was observed** — **meaning a substantial part of the
  crisis was a predictable consequence of binary significance-based publishing rather
  than of bad researchers.**
- **⚠️ The reform is incomplete.** **Preregistration is still not the norm; reforms are
  not universally mandated; they do not retroactively fix the pre-2010s record; and
  incentives still favour positive novel results.** **One prominent critic's conclusion —
  that without enforceable field-wide standards, credibility remains a property of
  individual researchers rather than of the discipline — is harsh and hard to refute.**

**⚠️ Practical upshot, unchanged from §1.4 → `psych-epistemic-situation-and-replication-reform`**: **preregistered, large-N, independently
replicated, with reported effect sizes. Everything else is a hypothesis.**

### 25.2 ⚠️ Social media and adolescent mental health — a live dispute, presented as one
**⚠️ This is the most consequential contested empirical question in psychology right now,
it is genuinely unresolved, and I am going to give you both sides rather than pick.**
**It is also an unusually good case study in §2.4 → `psych-epistemic-situation-and-replication-reform` — what happens when a field cannot run
the experiment.**

**⚠️ The Haidt/Twenge position:**
- **Adolescent depression, anxiety and self-harm rose sharply in the US and several
  Western countries beginning in the early 2010s, coinciding with smartphone and social
  media adoption.**
- **⚠️ Cross-sectional studies number in the thousands and heavy users are consistently
  worse off**; **a 2022 meta-analysis reported odds of depression rising roughly 13% per
  additional daily hour of social media use.**
- **Longitudinal work (e.g. Riehm et al. 2019) has reported high use predicting roughly
  double the future risk of depression**, and **these findings were central to US Surgeon
  General warnings in 2023 and 2024.**
- **⚠️ The "blender study" critique**: **null results often pool all screen time and all
  demographics together; disaggregating by platform, sex and age reveals harms that
  aggregate analyses mask.**
- **Haidt and Rausch argue recent work — including experiments run by Meta itself —
  provides increasingly strong causal evidence, and that harms are occurring at
  population scale.**

**⚠️ The Odgers/Przybylski/Orben position:**
- **⚠️ Most of the evidence is correlational, and the effect sizes are very small.**
  **Odgers: hundreds of researchers have searched for the large effects claimed, and
  produced "a mix of no, small and mixed associations."**
- **⚠️ Reverse causation is at least as plausible as causation.** **Adolescents with
  existing mental health problems use these platforms differently and more** — **a
  Registered Report on nationally representative UK data (N=3,340) with clinical-rater
  diagnoses found exactly that pattern: adolescents with mental health conditions
  reported more social media time and less satisfaction with online friendships.**
- **⚠️ The international data don't fit well.** **An analysis across 72 countries found no
  consistent measurable association between wellbeing and social media rollout.**
- **⚠️ A selection-effect and moral-panic critique**: **the pattern resembles previous
  cycles about novels, radio, television and video games.**
- **⚠️ A pointed methodological objection**: **a review of the experimental studies cited
  as causal evidence found that almost all involved participants aged 18–35, mostly
  college students, and none involved children under 13 — while the policy prescriptions
  target children and young teens.**

**⚠️ And the dispute has become adversarial in a way that is itself informative.**
**Haidt and colleagues published a formal commentary in 2026 arguing three widely-cited
Vuorre and Przybylski papers should not be used to assess this question, on both
conceptual grounds and methodological ones — including Meta's involvement in the research
design of one study.** ⚠️ **Note that the conflict-of-interest concern cuts in a direction
worth registering: industry involvement in the null-result literature is a real issue, and
so is advocacy incentive in the harms literature.**

> **⚠️ GOTCHA — how to hold this honestly.** ⚠️ **"Correlation isn't causation" and "moral
> panic" are both true observations and neither is an argument that the harm is absent.**
> **Equally, a coincident time series and thousands of small correlations are not causal
> evidence.** **What would settle it — large, long, randomized, platform-specific trials
> in the actual target age group — is difficult, expensive and partly unethical, which is
> why it hasn't been done.**
> **⚠️ What both camps largely agree on**: **effects are heterogeneous rather than
> universal**; **girls and early adolescents appear more affected**; **what you do on a
> platform matters more than time spent**; **and displacement of sleep, exercise and
> in-person contact is a plausible mechanism independent of content.** ⚠️ **Anyone
> presenting this as settled in either direction is telling you about their priors.**
