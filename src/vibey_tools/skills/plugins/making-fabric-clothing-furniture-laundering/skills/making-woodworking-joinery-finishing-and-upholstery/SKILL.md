## §16. ⚠️ Wood Movement

> **⚠️ THE single most important fact in woodworking, and the reason most amateur
> furniture eventually splits itself apart.**
> ⚠️ **Wood absorbs and releases moisture with ambient humidity, FOREVER — a hundred-year-old
> table still moves seasonally.** **⚠️ It moves substantially ACROSS the grain and
> essentially not at all ALONG it.**
> **⚠️ Tangential movement (roughly along the growth rings) is about TWICE the radial
> movement, which is why flatsawn boards cup and quartersawn boards stay flat.**
> **⚠️ A wide solid panel can move by a quarter of an inch or more across its width between
> summer and winter.**
> **⚠️ THEREFORE: never fix a solid wood panel rigidly on all four sides across its grain.**
> **It will either split itself or tear out its fastenings.**

```
⚠️ THE SOLUTIONS, all of which are the same idea
  FRAME AND PANEL  ⚠️ the panel FLOATS in a groove. The oldest and best
     solution to the problem, and why every traditional door looks like that
  BREADBOARD ENDS  attached with elongated holes or a single fixed pin
  ⚠️ TABLETOP FASTENERS  buttons, Z-clips or figure-8s that let the top
     slide relative to the base
  ELONGATED SCREW HOLES  wherever cross-grain fastening is unavoidable
  ⚠️ PLYWOOD / MDF  dimensionally stable — which is the real reason
     sheet goods dominate casework
```
**⚠️ Moisture content**: ⚠️ **acclimatize lumber to the space it will live in BEFORE
building** — **kiln-dried to roughly 6–8% for heated interiors, higher for outdoors.**
**A moisture meter is cheap and prevents expensive failures.**
**⚠️ Reading a board**: **grain direction for planing (⚠️ plane "downhill" or you tear
out), cup, bow, twist, crook; ⚠️ and reaction wood, which moves unpredictably and should
be rejected.**

## §17. Joinery

```
⚠️ MECHANICAL STRENGTH COMES FROM LONG-GRAIN TO LONG-GRAIN GLUE SURFACE.
   ⚠️ End grain glues poorly — it's like gluing a bundle of straws end-on.
   Every good joint exists to create long-grain contact and/or
   mechanical interlock
⚠️ MORTISE AND TENON  the frame joint. Strong, resists racking
⚠️ DOVETAIL   ⚠️ mechanically interlocking in one direction — resists
   being pulled apart even without glue. Drawers
⚠️ HALF-LAP · BRIDLE · BOX/FINGER JOINT
DOWEL · BISCUIT · ⚠️ DOMINO (loose tenon)
POCKET SCREW  ⚠️ fast, adequate, and visibly not fine work
BUTT JOINT + fastener  ⚠️ weakest; end grain
⚠️ HOUSING/DADO and RABBET  for casework and shelving
```
**⚠️ The design question is always which forces the joint must resist**: ⚠️ **racking
(frames), tension (drawer fronts), shear (shelves), or compression (legs)** — **and
choosing a joint that resists the wrong force is how a chair loosens.**
**⚠️ Chairs are the hardest furniture** because ⚠️ **every joint is loaded in racking by a
moving person, with long lever arms and no panel to stiffen the structure.**

## §18. Adhesives and Fasteners

```
⚠️ PVA (yellow/white)  the default. ⚠️ Stronger than the wood itself in
   long grain. Not gap-filling; needs clamping pressure. Type II/III
   for water resistance
⚠️ HIDE GLUE  ⚠️ REVERSIBLE with heat and moisture — which is why
   antiques can be repaired and modern glued furniture often can't.
   Liquid or hot hide; the conservator's choice
POLYURETHANE  ⚠️ foams (the expansion is NOT gap-filling STRENGTH),
   moisture-cured, messy
EPOXY  ⚠️ genuinely gap-filling, waterproof, good for end grain and
   dissimilar materials
CA  fast, brittle, for small repairs
```
**⚠️ Clamping**: ⚠️ **pressure, not squeeze-out, is the objective; even distribution
matters more than force; and ⚠️ a joint that needs heavy clamping to close is a joint
that's cut wrong and will be under permanent stress.**
**Fasteners**: **pilot holes (⚠️ always, in hardwood), pocket screws, knockdown fittings,
and ⚠️ the fact that screws into end grain hold poorly.**

## §19. Wood Finishing

```
⚠️ FILM FINISHES  sit ON the wood — protective, repairable-by-recoating
  ⚠️ SHELLAC   fast, non-toxic when cured, ⚠️ dissolved by alcohol,
     excellent as a barrier/sealer coat under almost anything
  LACQUER      fast, sprayed, ⚠️ solvent-heavy
  ⚠️ VARNISH / POLYURETHANE  durable, slow, ⚠️ hard to repair invisibly
  WATER-BASED  low odour, ⚠️ raises the grain (sand between coats)
⚠️ PENETRATING  soak IN — easy to apply and repair, less protective
  ⚠️ OIL (tung, linseed)  ⚠️ "Danish oil" and "teak oil" are marketing
     names, not defined products — read the actual contents
  WAX  ⚠️ a maintenance coat, not a finish on its own
```
> **⚠️ GOTCHA — oily rags from linseed or tung oil can SPONTANEOUSLY COMBUST.** ⚠️ **The
> oil cures by exothermic oxidation, and a crumpled rag insulates the heat.** **⚠️ Spread
> rags flat to dry, or submerge them in water in a sealed metal container.** **This starts
> real fires every year and it is the one genuine safety hazard in this section.**

**⚠️ Surface prep is most of the result**: ⚠️ **sand progressively without skipping grits,
remove all dust, and ⚠️ raise the grain deliberately with a damp cloth before the first
water-based coat.** **⚠️ Blotch-prone woods (pine, cherry, maple) need a conditioner or a
shellac washcoat before stain.**

## §20. Upholstery

**⚠️ The traditional stack, bottom to top**: **frame → webbing → springs (⚠️ coil, tied
eight ways, or sinuous/zigzag) → burlap → edge roll → stuffing → muslin → wadding →
top cover.**
**⚠️ Foam**: **density vs firmness are DIFFERENT properties** — ⚠️ **density predicts
LONGEVITY, firmness predicts feel, and cheap furniture uses low-density foam that feels
fine and collapses within a couple of years.**
**⚠️ Fabric selection for upholstery**: **abrasion resistance measured in double rubs
(⚠️ Wyzenbeek or Martindale — different tests, not interconvertible), cleaning codes
(⚠️ W water-based, S solvent, WS either, X vacuum only), and ⚠️ railroading (running the
fabric sideways to avoid seams on wide pieces).**
**⚠️ Reupholstery is worth it when the FRAME is good** — **hardwood, joined rather than
stapled** — **and rarely otherwise.**

## §21. Seating and Frames

**⚠️ Ergonomic basics**: **seat height ~16–18", depth ~18–22", ⚠️ seat angle and back angle
(a reclined seat needs a reclined back or you slide out), lumbar support, and armrest
height.**
**⚠️ Structural**: ⚠️ **triangulation and stretchers resist racking; corner blocks stiffen
frames; and the failure mode of nearly every chair is the joint between leg and rail
working loose under repeated racking** (§17).
**⚠️ Wobble diagnosis**: ⚠️ **a wobbling chair is usually a loose joint, not a short leg —
and gluing a loose joint without first cleaning out the old glue produces a repair that
fails again within months.**
