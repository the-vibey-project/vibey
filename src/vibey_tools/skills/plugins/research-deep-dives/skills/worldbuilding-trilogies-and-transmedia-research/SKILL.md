# Worldbuilding, Trilogies, and Transmedia Storytelling

## Executive synthesis

The strongest fictional universes are not encyclopedias. They are **systems under
pressure**: geography and resources shape economies; economies shape institutions;
institutions shape incentives; incentives shape characters; characters make choices that
change the system. Lore becomes dramatically useful when it creates constraints, unequal
knowledge, competing interpretations, and consequences.

Three principles organize almost everything that follows:

1. **Build causally, reveal selectively.** The creator may need a deep model, but the
   audience usually needs only the specific information required to feel orientation,
   curiosity, stakes, and payoff. Tolkien's “Secondary World” idea is useful here, but it
   should not be reduced to maps and nouns: the world must produce “secondary belief”
   through internally coherent experience. [Tolkien Estate commentary on *On
   Fairy-Stories*](https://www.tolkienestate.com/scholarship/verlyn-flieger-on-fairy-stories/)
2. **A trilogy is three complete experiences in one larger transformation.** Each volume or
   season needs its own protagonist objective, escalating problem, climax, and emotional
   change. The series-level arc should change the meaning and cost of the next installment;
   “part one ends before the story starts” is not a structure.
3. **Transmedia is expansion, not repetition.** A novel, film, television season, and game
   should exploit what that medium does best. Henry Jenkins's influential formulation treats
   transmedia as a story distributed across media in which each text makes a distinctive,
   valuable contribution; later scholarship also stresses that this creates questions of
   canon, ownership, labor, and audience participation. [Jenkins overview and research
   discussion](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13642529.2021.1963597)

## 1. What a world is made of

Use a layered model. Do not fill every layer to the same depth; deepen what the story will
touch.

### The physical layer

Define scale, climate, terrain, travel time, energy, food, disease, hazards, and the limits
of technology or magic. The important question is not “what is on the map?” but “what does
this make possible or impossible?” A mountain range creates political borders, transport
costs, dialect differences, military chokepoints, and myths. A magic system changes labor,
war, medicine, policing, class, and religion—not merely battle choreography.

### The material and institutional layer

Track who owns land, tools, information, infrastructure, bodies, and violence. Specify how
people obtain food, shelter, status, education, and security; who adjudicates disputes; what
is legal but shameful; and what is illegal but necessary. Institutions should have budgets,
procedures, internal factions, failure modes, and believable reasons ordinary people comply.

### The cultural layer

Give groups distinctive practices rather than decorative “flavor”: kinship, inheritance,
childhood, work, mourning, hospitality, humor, art, taboo, timekeeping, names, and ideas of
personhood. Avoid treating a real-world culture as a costume kit. Research real histories and
communities, use multiple sources, and ask who has authority to describe the culture.

### The epistemic layer

Separate **what is true**, **what different groups believe**, and **what the audience knows**.
This is one of the best engines of mystery and politics. A creation myth can be false but
socially powerful; a “fact” can be true only from one faction's perspective; a prophecy can
become causal because people act on it.

### The symbolic and emotional layer

Decide what recurring images, places, objects, sounds, colors, rituals, and stories mean to
different people. A world feels authored when its visual and linguistic motifs evolve with
the characters rather than functioning as wallpaper.

### The change layer

Write a timeline of pressures: migration, ecological shock, war, technological discovery,
religious reform, economic collapse, succession crisis, or contact with an outside power.
History should leave residue—ruins, laws, accents, trauma, inherited privilege, missing
records—not just dates in an appendix.

## 2. From setting to story

For every major world element, complete this chain:

> condition → institution → incentive → choice → consequence → new condition

Example: scarce water → ration authority → officials can sell access → a protagonist falsifies
records → a district survives but legitimacy collapses → rival communities form a federation.

This prevents “lore gravity,” where interesting facts sit inertly outside the plot. A useful
worldbuilding test is to remove a fact: if no character choice, relationship, image, mechanic,
or consequence changes, the fact may belong in private notes rather than the audience's path.

### The minimum viable world bible

Keep a short authoritative document, then link to optional deep material:

- one-sentence premise and thematic question;
- rules and exceptions of the physical/technological/magical system;
- map with travel assumptions;
- timeline of only causally relevant history;
- factions, resources, goals, methods, internal disagreements, and red lines;
- character sheets focused on desire, fear, knowledge, contradiction, and change;
- glossary and pronunciation guide;
- canon ledger: confirmed, provisional, contradictory, deliberately unknown;
- continuity log: dates, ages, injuries, travel, relationships, objects, and unresolved seeds;
- adaptation matrix: what is literal, translated, omitted, or newly invented in each medium.

Treat the bible as a production instrument, not a substitute for scenes. Let uncertainty be
intentional and label it. “Not yet decided” is safer than accidental canon.

## 3. Writing a trilogy

### Series architecture

Start with the ending's emotional and political meaning, not necessarily every plot event.
Define:

- the initial order and the order that could replace it;
- the protagonist's false belief, wound, desire, and eventual transformation;
- the antagonist's credible alternative future;
- the irreversible decision at the end of each installment;
- what each installment reveals, changes, and makes newly impossible;
- the cost of victory and the unresolved question that legitimately carries forward.

A practical macro-arc is:

| Installment | Dramatic job | World job | Ending |
|---|---|---|---|
| I — rupture | Establish desire, rules, allies, and opposition | Show the system from a limited viewpoint | A local goal succeeds or fails, but the protagonist commits to a larger conflict |
| II — complication | Test the first book's assumptions; force coalition, betrayal, or moral compromise | Reveal competing histories and the cost of changing the system | The old strategy becomes impossible; the protagonist pays a visible price |
| III — transformation | Confront the core contradiction and make the final choice | Reconfigure the system, not merely defeat a person | Resolve the central question while showing the new order's consequences |

This is a design lens, not a law. Three-act structure is common in screenwriting, but
research and practice also use sequences, four-act models, episodic structures, braided plots,
and non-Western forms. Use a structure because it clarifies audience experience, not because
page percentages prove quality. [Research discussion of trilogy and three-act limitations](https://clic.research.vub.be/sites/default/files/2024-12/Seriality_8.1_JLIC_final_rev24.pdf)

### Installment architecture

Each book/film/season should answer:

1. Who wants what now?
2. Why can they not simply get it?
3. What choice makes the conflict irreversible?
4. What does the climax prove about the protagonist's belief?
5. What changes in the world afterward?

Use a scene or sequence card with: viewpoint, immediate objective, obstacle, turn, new
information, emotional movement, and consequence. A chapter that only explains history is
usually weaker than a conflict in which people disagree about history.

### Escalation without inflation

Do not make every volume “bigger” only by adding armies, planets, or villains. Escalate along
different axes: intimacy, moral cost, public visibility, irreversibility, knowledge, time
pressure, and institutional stakes. The third volume should feel larger because the reader
understands more and choices cost more—not because the map has doubled.

### Foreshadowing and payoff

Maintain a ledger with four states: planted, developed, paid off, intentionally unresolved.
Foreshadow through behavior, image, rule, rumor, contradiction, or consequence. A twist is
fair when the evidence was available and the revelation changes interpretation rather than
merely withholding a noun.

## 4. Medium-specific worldbuilding

### Novels

Prose can enter minds, compress centuries, modulate exposition, and exploit language. Give
each viewpoint a different vocabulary and blind spot. Reveal the world through desire and
friction: a character notices prices, borders, clothing, weather, or ritual because it affects
them. Use appendices only for readers who want extra depth; the narrative must stand without
them.

### Film

Film communicates through image, performance, sound, editing, production design, and spatial
continuity under severe time constraints. Design a visual grammar: silhouettes, materials,
architecture, light, movement, and recurring compositions. Replace explanation with readable
behavior and objects. Write for production reality: locations, crowd scale, VFX complexity,
stunts, animals, weather, and continuity are storytelling constraints. A film universe needs
one emotionally complete movie before it needs a sequel hook.

### Television

TV needs a durable engine: a repeatable source of conflict that can generate episodes without
freezing character growth. Build season arcs, episode arcs, A/B/C plots, act-outs or streaming
turns, and a writers-room continuity system. Decide which mysteries are season questions and
which are episode questions. A series bible should explain not just backstory but the kinds of
stories the show can keep telling, the characters' changing relationships, and the limits of
the premise.

### Video games

Games add player agency, exploration, systems, failure, repetition, sound, interface, and
the possibility that players encounter content out of order. Narrative must coexist with
mechanics: what the player does should express or complicate the fiction. Build a “story
grammar” for quests, locations, rewards, environmental clues, dialogue states, save/load
behavior, and pacing. Branching is expensive; often the best design combines authored
milestones with local choice, systemic reactivity, and multiple interpretations.

GDC's narrative guidance emphasizes the relationship among gameplay, player choice, quest
design, environmental storytelling, dialogue, performance, and cross-disciplinary workflow.
The practical implication is that narrative designers must collaborate with level, systems,
audio, art, engineering, UX, and production—not hand writers a finished world late in the
pipeline. [GDC Narrative & Performance](https://gdconf.com/narrative-performance/)

Games also need a clear agency contract: what can the player change, what remains fixed, how
does the world respond, and what does failure mean? GDC's discussion of authorial control
describes drama as a combination of pacing, lighting, sound, challenge, reward, and player
autonomy rather than cutscenes alone. [GDC on authorial control](https://gdconf.com/article/gdc-speaker-spotlight-telltale-s-dave-grossman-on-authorial-control/)

Open worlds need density and purpose, not just acreage. Locations should offer navigational
identity, resources, social history, gameplay affordances, and reasons to return. “Living
world” design succeeds when systems produce stories the player can attribute to the world,
not only when the world contains many facts. [GDC on *Horizon Forbidden West* world design](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1029117/Game-Narrative-Summit-Creating-a)

## 5. Transmedia and franchise design

Distinguish four operations:

- **Adaptation:** translate substantially the same story into another medium.
- **Extension:** add a new canonical story, character, period, or viewpoint.
- **Reframing:** retell or reinterpret events through a different perspective or genre.
- **Participation:** invite audiences to play, perform, remix, investigate, or contribute.

Plan the universe as a graph, not a release list. Every node needs a reason to exist, a
self-contained entry point, and a distinctive contribution. A game should not be a film with
combat inserted; a companion novel should not merely narrate deleted scenes. Decide whether
the canon is centralized, tiered, contradictory by design, or open to multiplicity.

### A transmedia matrix

| Medium | Best at | Typical world contribution | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Novel | interiority, language, breadth | hidden motive, history, unreliable perspective | exposition dump |
| Film | image, performance, compression | iconic visual culture and emotional event | overloading runtime and budget |
| TV | duration, ensemble, social process | institutions, subcultures, changing relationships | mythology without episode engine |
| Game | agency, systems, space, repetition | lived rules, choice, exploration, emergent consequence | ludonarrative contradiction or fake choice |
| Comic/animation | stylization, impossible scale | myth, alternate viewpoint, visual experimentation | optionality that fragments canon |
| Audio/podcast | voice, intimacy, ambiguity | testimony, propaganda, oral history | confusing sound design or accessibility gaps |

Give audiences multiple entry points. “Required homework” is usually a failure of design;
deep cross-media knowledge should reward fans, while each primary work remains legible.
Manage canon with versioned documents, named authorities, conflict-resolution rules, and an
explicit policy for retcons. Continuity is a tool for trust, not a moral obligation to preserve
every early idea.

## 6. Production and collaboration

The practical unit is not the lone genius but the shared decision system. Establish a creative
director/showrunner/game director, a continuity or lore owner, medium leads, and a process for
disagreement. Maintain change logs, decision records, sensitivity and research notes, asset
dependencies, and a glossary that writers, artists, actors, designers, translators, and QA
can actually use.

Prototype the riskiest promise early:

- a short scene for voice and character;
- a storyboard or animatic for visual grammar;
- a playable slice for game agency and pacing;
- a table read for dialogue and ensemble chemistry;
- a location/production test for physical feasibility.

Test for comprehension, emotional investment, curiosity, agency, and payoff—not only whether
people can recite lore. External research should be treated as research: record sources,
dates, uncertainty, cultural consultation, and what was changed in response.

## 7. Common failure modes

- **Encyclopedia before drama:** build only the world elements the current story can pressure.
- **Aesthetic culture:** distinctive clothes and invented words without institutions or power.
- **Magic/technology without second-order effects:** ask what it does to work, law, class,
  warfare, family, disability, medicine, and belief.
- **Mystery-box evasion:** promise meaningful answers; do not confuse ambiguity with omission.
- **Trilogy middle-book paralysis:** give volume two an independent moral crisis and ending.
- **Escalation inflation:** larger map, weaker intimacy; escalate choices and consequences.
- **Canon worship:** preserve contradictions only when they generate meaning; otherwise clarify.
- **Adaptation literalism:** translate function, not surface form.
- **Game cutscene domination:** let mechanics, space, and player decisions carry meaning.
- **False choice:** communicate constraints honestly; reactivity can be local and still matter.
- **Cultural extraction:** do not borrow a living people's sacred material as exotic texture.
- **Production fantasy:** budget, schedule, localization, accessibility, performance capture,
  QA, and pipeline constraints belong in the creative design from the beginning.

## 8. A disciplined creation workflow

1. Frame the promise in one sentence: audience, genre, emotional experience, and central
   contradiction.
2. Research the real-world inspirations with a search log and source-quality notes; distinguish
   primary evidence, scholarship, testimony, and creative reference.
3. Build the causal spine: environment → resources → institutions → beliefs → conflict.
4. Create a small world bible and a canon/uncertainty ledger.
5. Design the protagonist, antagonist, factions, and trilogy-level transformation together.
6. Outline each installment as a complete experience with an irreversible ending.
7. Prototype the medium's hardest promise before expanding the lore.
8. Draft scenes/quests/episodes that reveal the world through conflict and action.
9. Test for orientation, curiosity, emotional clarity, agency, and continuity.
10. Run a second-order-effects audit, sensitivity review, production audit, and adaptation
    audit.
11. Revise for the smallest coherent canon; delete beautiful material that does no work.
12. Publish a clear entry point, then expand only when the next medium adds genuine value.

## High-value reading and viewing path

Begin with Tolkien's *On Fairy-Stories* and Ursula K. Le Guin's talks and essays on craft,
then compare them rather than treating either as a universal formula. Study a completed
trilogy in two forms—novels and adaptations—and map what was compressed, externalized,
reordered, or invented. Read Jenkins on transmedia and Klastrup/Tosca on transmedial worlds;
then examine a franchise's canon policy and audience practices. For games, study GDC's
narrative sessions and play one linear narrative game, one systemic/open-world game, and one
branching or choice-centered game while recording where agency, space, mechanics, and story
agree or conflict. For research quality, use the SIFT/CRAAP-style discipline from research
methodology: trace claims to originals, date fast-moving information, preserve disagreement,
and never turn a plausible recollection into a cited fact.

## Bottom line

Build less than you imagine, but make what you build causally consequential. Give every
installment a real ending and every medium a job only it can do. Preserve mystery by designing
unknowns, not by withholding answers indefinitely. Let the audience encounter the universe
through people making costly choices inside systems that resist them. That is the bridge from
worldbuilding to story, from trilogy to saga, and from a franchise plan to a living world.
