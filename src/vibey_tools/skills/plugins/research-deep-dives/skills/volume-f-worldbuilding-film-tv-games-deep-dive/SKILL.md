# Volume F — Worldbuilding, Trilogies, Film, Television, Games, and Transmedia

Research edition: 1.0
Date: 2026-09-27

## 1. Worldbuilding is a causal system

A fictional world is not a list of names. It is a model of how matter, ecology, technology, institutions, incentives, beliefs, identities, and memory interact. A world feels real when consequences propagate.

Define:

- physical constraints: geography, climate, resources, disease, travel, energy;
- biological constraints: bodies, reproduction, food, lifespan, disability, species relations;
- technical systems: tools, infrastructure, communications, weapons, production, maintenance;
- economic systems: labor, property, money, trade, scarcity, debt, distribution;
- political systems: authority, legitimacy, law, coercion, representation, succession;
- social systems: family, class, gender, status, migration, education, religion, community;
- cultural systems: language, ritual, art, history, taboo, humor, memory;
- narrative systems: desire, conflict, revelation, causality, theme, point of view.

The strongest worldbuilding begins with a few generative constraints and derives consequences. If magic, faster-than-light travel, immortality, artificial intelligence, or abundant energy exists, ask how it changes labor, war, family, law, class, geography, religion, insurance, childhood, and death.

## 2. The world bible

A useful bible is a change-controlled source of truth, not an encyclopedia nobody can use. It should contain:

- one-page premise and thematic promise;
- audience, genre, tone, content boundaries;
- world rules and exceptions;
- timeline with confidence status;
- geography and travel times;
- factions, institutions, incentives, resources, and internal conflicts;
- character biographies, wants, fears, secrets, relationships, and arcs;
- glossary and pronunciation;
- technology, magic, ecology, economy, and legal constraints;
- visual and sonic references;
- continuity ledger;
- unresolved questions and deliberate mysteries;
- rights, source, and adaptation notes.

Mark every fact as public, private, secret, disputed, unreliable, or retconnable. That prevents the common error of confusing what the author knows with what the audience should know.

## 3. History, institutions, and lived texture

Write history as contested memory. Different groups should remember conquest, migration, disaster, religion, and law differently. A calendar, map, census, legal code, tax system, family structure, food supply, building material, and funeral practice often make a world more convincing than dozens of invented proper nouns.

Institutions need:

- purpose;
- resources;
- formal rules;
- informal norms;
- enforcement;
- succession;
- failure modes;
- people who benefit;
- people who resist;
- mechanisms of reform.

Avoid making every culture a single personality or making one real-world culture a costume. Borrow structures carefully, research the source traditions, and decide what is transformation rather than imitation.

## 4. Characters as agents in systems

A character is not just a biography. Build a chain:

wound or condition → belief → desire → strategy → choice → consequence → adaptation.

Give characters different information, status, risks, obligations, and interpretations of the same event. A protagonist may be morally sincere while embedded in harmful institutions. An antagonist should have capabilities, incentives, relationships, and a theory of the world; “evil because evil” produces thin conflict.

Track:

- what each character wants now and ultimately;
- what they think is true;
- what they misperceive;
- what they owe;
- what they fear losing;
- what they can do;
- who can constrain them;
- what changes their mind;
- what they cannot recover.

## 5. Trilogy architecture

A trilogy is three complete dramatic experiences that also form one larger transformation. Each installment needs its own protagonist-level objective, escalating pressure, climax, emotional movement, and ending. The series needs a larger question whose answer changes across the three works.

A workable macro-structure:

- Part I: discovery, commitment, first irreversible cost, partial victory or apparent defeat;
- Part II: consequences, divided loyalties, expansion of the world, reversal, loss, and a new understanding of the conflict;
- Part III: integration of personal and systemic stakes, final choice, consequences, and thematic resolution.

Do not reserve all meaningful change for part three. Each installment should change the political map, relationships, knowledge, and character capability. Planting is not payoff unless the audience can understand the relationship retrospectively.

A trilogy outline should track:

- external plot;
- internal character arcs;
- faction and institutional change;
- mystery/reveal schedule;
- resource and geography changes;
- thematic claims and counterclaims;
- continuity constraints;
- what each ending closes and opens.

## 6. Film adaptation

Film is a time-bounded audiovisual form. It communicates through image, sound, performance, editing, production design, and omission as much as dialogue. Adaptation is transformation, not literal transfer.

A film development package commonly includes logline, synopsis, treatment, screenplay, character materials, lookbook, budget assumptions, schedule, rights chain, and pitch materials. Production adds development, financing, legal clearance, casting, preproduction, principal photography, postproduction, marketing, distribution, and delivery.

Budget is driven by locations, cast, crew, days, stunts, animals, vehicles, sets, costumes, prosthetics, visual effects, sound, insurance, travel, contingencies, postproduction, music rights, deliverables, and reshoots. A fictional world should be designed around what can be made, not only imagined.

## 7. Television and streaming

Television requires repeatable dramatic engines, episode-level satisfaction, season-level progression, and a sustainable production process. A series bible usually includes premise, world, characters, season arc, episode concepts, tone, and future possibilities. A writers’ room converts architecture into episodes, scenes, revisions, and production-aware scripts.

The showrunner or lead creative team coordinates story, production, staffing, notes, continuity, and often postproduction. Writers’ room staffing and duration are governed by contracts and change over time; consult current guild terms rather than relying on old assumptions. [WGA writers-room provisions](https://www.wga.org/uploadedfiles/contracts/Showrunners-Guide-to-2023-MBA-Writers-Room-Provisions.pdf).

Episodic design questions:

- what resets and what accumulates;
- what the audience learns each episode;
- what emotional promise repeats;
- what prevents the engine from exhausting;
- how production limits shape locations and cast;
- how a season ending creates desire without making the current season feel incomplete.

## 8. Games and interactive worlds

Games add player agency, rules, feedback, failure, repetition, affordances, and systems simulation. A game world must work when players do unexpected things. Narrative can be delivered through dialogue, environment, quests, mechanics, animation, sound, UI, systemic behavior, and player-created sequence.

Design layers:

- fantasy: what the player imagines doing;
- verb set: move, fight, build, trade, investigate, negotiate, create;
- rules: what actions are possible;
- feedback: how the system communicates consequences;
- progression: how capability and knowledge change;
- economy: resources, scarcity, sinks, rewards;
- level structure: space, pacing, navigation, encounter composition;
- narrative: authored, systemic, emergent, or hybrid.

A cutscene may contradict the player’s actual agency. Avoid ludonarrative conflict by aligning mechanics, stakes, and story or making the conflict intentional.

Game development requires design documentation, prototypes, technical architecture, asset pipelines, tools, version control, testing, accessibility, performance budgets, localization, certification, deployment, telemetry governance, and post-launch support. Procedural generation can increase scale but does not replace authored goals, constraints, curation, or quality assurance. [Unreal procedural content example](https://www.unrealengine.com/electric-dreams-environment).

## 9. Transmedia

Transmedia is not copying the same plot into different products. Each medium should contribute distinctive information or experience while remaining understandable at its entry point.

Plan:

- canonical core;
- medium-specific contributions;
- audience entry points;
- continuity authority;
- spoiler and reveal policy;
- rights and licensing;
- production dependency;
- accessibility and localization;
- archival and preservation strategy.

A novel can provide interiority; film provides performance and audiovisual compression; television provides accumulation; games provide agency and systems; comics provide controlled visual sequence; live events provide social presence. Do not make a viewer buy five products to understand the basic story unless that exclusivity is intentional and ethically clear.

## 10. Production pipeline

### Development

Research, concept, rights, premise, treatment, script or design document, feedback, package, budget, financing, and greenlight.

### Preproduction

Breakdown, schedule, budget lock, casting, locations, design, storyboards, previs, technical tests, contracts, safety plan, insurance, and department coordination.

### Production

Daily execution, continuity, dailies, data management, safety, change control, performance, photography, sound, asset capture, and documentation.

### Post

Editorial, sound design, dialogue, music, color, visual effects, animation, localization, accessibility, legal clearances, quality control, mastering, and delivery.

### Release and maintenance

Marketing, distribution, platform delivery, audience support, patches, preservation, rights tracking, residuals, and lessons learned.

Every handoff can lose information. Use naming conventions, version control, metadata, backups, review gates, and an authoritative decision log.

## 11. Rights, labor, and business

A project needs a rights chain: underlying property, adaptation rights, trademarks, music, archival material, likeness, locations, artwork, software, fonts, and contracts. Chain of title, indemnities, insurance, completion requirements, guild agreements, residuals, and revenue definitions matter.

Residuals are compensation for reuse or exhibition beyond the original use and are governed by contracts. Current terms change; SAG-AFTRA provides contract and residual resources. [SAG-AFTRA residuals](https://www.sagaftra.org/sag-aftra-tv-and-theatrical-residuals-quick-guide).

Treat workers as a production system, not disposable inputs. Scheduling, rest, safety, harassment prevention, accessibility, credit, compensation, consent, and power asymmetry affect both ethics and quality. Artificial intelligence clauses, likeness, voice, and training-data rights require current legal and guild review.

## 12. Continuity and change control

Maintain a continuity database with entity IDs, dates, locations, relationships, injuries, possessions, technology, rules, and source authority. Record contradictions as intentional, unresolved, error, or retcon. A retcon should state what changed, why, what downstream material is affected, and whether the audience needs explanation.

Use automated checks where possible: date conflicts, impossible travel, duplicate names, incompatible ages, faction membership, resource counts, and versioned canon. Human review remains necessary for meaning and dramatic coherence.

## 13. Audience and theme

Theme is not a slogan; it is a question dramatized through competing values and consequences. Ask what the story rewards, punishes, normalizes, reveals, and leaves unresolved.

Audience research can test comprehension, emotion, pacing, usability, accessibility, and market fit. It should not replace artistic judgment or reduce a story to a single metric. For games, playtesting observes actual behavior; for film and television, screenings can reveal confusion and pacing problems but are influenced by sample and context.

## 14. Common failures

- encyclopedic lore with no causal consequences;
- a trilogy where only the final part matters;
- characters who exist only to explain the world;
- invented cultures treated as homogeneous;
- magic or technology without labor, maintenance, or political consequences;
- mystery boxes without planned answers;
- television episodes with no engine beyond the season mystery;
- games that tell players they lack agency while mechanically rewarding everything;
- transmedia that hides essential plot in another medium;
- production plans that ignore rights, safety, labor, postproduction, or data management;
- visual scale pursued without budget, schedule, or asset strategy;
- continuity treated as trivia rather than causal integrity.

## 15. Practical creation workflow

1. Define the thematic promise and audience.
2. Write the world’s physical and institutional constraints.
3. Build a timeline and causal history.
4. Define factions, resources, and conflicts.
5. Create characters as agents with unequal information and power.
6. Choose the medium’s unique expressive affordances.
7. Prototype scenes, episodes, mechanics, or visual language.
8. Test comprehension, emotion, agency, accessibility, and production feasibility.
9. Build the continuity and rights ledger.
10. Plan the smallest producible version.
11. Expand only after the core works.
12. Preserve, document, and maintain the world after release.
