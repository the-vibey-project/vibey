# Volume D — Religion, Ancient Societies, and European Civilizations

Research edition: 1.0
Date: 2026-09-27

## Method

Doctrine is what a tradition formally teaches; history is what institutions and people did over time; culture is patterned meaning and practice; social life is everyday organization, relationships, labor, status, and material conditions. These are related but not interchangeable. No category in this volume describes a single personality shared by every member.

## 1. Catholic doctrine

Catholic doctrine is organized around Scripture, apostolic Tradition, the teaching office of bishops in communion with the bishop of Rome, the Creed, liturgy, sacraments, moral theology, canon law, and theological development. The Catechism presents four organizing pillars: profession of faith, sacraments, moral life, and prayer.

Core teachings include one God in Trinity, the incarnation, death and resurrection of Jesus, the Church as one, holy, catholic and apostolic, grace, resurrection, judgment, and eternal life. Catholic doctrine distinguishes dogma, authoritative teaching, theological opinion, discipline, and local custom.

The seven sacraments are Baptism, Confirmation, Eucharist, Penance, Anointing of the Sick, Holy Orders, and Matrimony. The Eucharist has a central role in communion and worship. [Catechism](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en.html); [seven sacraments](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_two/section_two.html).

Catholic social doctrine emphasizes human dignity, common good, solidarity, subsidiarity, participation, work, care for the poor, family, peace, and the social responsibility of property. These principles are both theological claims and subjects for social-historical study.

## 2. Catholic history and social life

Major periods include apostolic and patristic Christianity; late Roman and post-Roman Latin Christianity; monasticism; medieval papacy, parish, canon law and scholasticism; Renaissance and Reformation conflict; the Council of Trent and Catholic reform; empire and missions; modern nation-states; Vatican I; Vatican II; and post-conciliar pluralism.

Catholic history includes continuity and development, charity and coercion, education and censorship, art and empire, reform and abuse, popular devotion and institutional power. It cannot be reduced to either triumphal institutional history or a single narrative of oppression.

Social life has included parish, family, feast, pilgrimage, devotional associations, schools, hospitals, religious orders, immigrant communities, labor movements, political parties, and mutual aid. Catholic culture varies by rite, geography, class, ethnicity, gender, practice, and historical era.

## 3. Greek Orthodox doctrine

Eastern Orthodox theology centers on the Trinity, incarnation, resurrection, the Church, Scripture within Holy Tradition, the Ecumenical Councils, episcopal communion, divine liturgy, icons, asceticism, saints, and participation in divine life often discussed as theosis. Orthodox ecclesiology is conciliar and does not recognize a universal papal jurisdiction in the Roman Catholic sense.

Orthodoxy often calls sacraments holy mysteries. Seven are commonly counted, but Orthodox sources emphasize that the whole life of the Church is sacramental and mystical rather than limiting sacramentality to a numbered list. Baptism, Chrismation, Eucharist, confession, marriage, ordination, and unction are central mysteries. [OCA Orthodox Faith](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith); [OCA sacraments](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/worship/the-sacraments/the-sacraments).

## 4. Greek Orthodox history and social life

Historical layers include early Christianity in the eastern Mediterranean, Constantinople and the Byzantine Empire, the Ecumenical Councils, the gradual divergence between Greek East and Latin West, Ottoman millet contexts, modern national churches, communist and post-communist societies, and diaspora.

Greek Orthodox can refer to theology, an ecclesial jurisdiction, Greek ethnic identity, a national church, or a cultural inheritance. These must be separated. Parish life may include liturgy, fasting, icons, saints, godparenthood, food traditions, language, burial, charitable work, festivals, and ethnic associations. Diaspora churches can function as religious, cultural, linguistic, and social institutions simultaneously.

## 5. Protestant doctrine

The Protestant Reformations were multiple movements, not one church. Lutheran, Reformed, Anglican, Anabaptist, Baptist, Methodist, Pentecostal, evangelical, liberal, and independent traditions differ in authority, sacraments, polity, salvation, worship, and social ethics.

Shared tendencies often include criticism of late-medieval ecclesial authority, emphasis on Scripture, preaching, reform of worship, and varied formulations of justification by faith. “Sola” formulas are shorthand whose meaning differs by tradition. Protestantism has no single worldwide magisterium.

## 6. Protestant history and culture

The Reformations intersected with printing, literacy, urban politics, monarchies, peasant revolt, confessional state formation, colonialism, slavery, education, capitalism, revivalism, abolition, nationalism, missions, and mass media. Protestant culture has generated both reform movements and systems of discipline, exclusion, imperialism, and social control.

The “Protestant work ethic” is a historical argument, not a complete causal explanation of capitalism. Compare religious ideas with institutions, state power, labor, technology, markets, and colonial extraction. Distinguish theology, lived religion, denominational organization, and stereotype.

## 7. Ancient Jewish history

Ancient Jewish history includes Israelite and Judahite kingdoms, Assyrian and Babylonian imperial contexts, Persian rule, the Second Temple period, Hellenistic kingdoms, Hasmonean state formation, Roman rule, the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE, and rabbinic reorganization in late antiquity.

Major themes include covenant, Torah, temple and priesthood, prophecy, wisdom, purity, Sabbath, festivals, kinship, land, law, diaspora, synagogue, scribal practice, sectarian diversity, trade, taxation, slavery, gender, and imperial rule.

Ancient Judaism was not monolithic. Priests, Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes, apocalyptic groups, diaspora communities, peasants, artisans, women, enslaved people, and later rabbis occupied different worlds. Use biblical texts alongside archaeology, inscriptions, Dead Sea Scrolls, Josephus, Philo, rabbinic literature, papyri, and Greek and Roman sources. Later texts may preserve earlier traditions but are not automatic transcripts of earlier life.

Sources: [Israel Museum Dead Sea Scrolls](https://www.imj.org.il/en/wings/shrine-book); [SBL Bible Odyssey](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/).

## 8. The first 200–300 years of Christianity

Early Christianity included diverse Jesus movements, house communities, Jewish and gentile communities, traveling teachers, bishops, deacons, patrons, women benefactors, enslaved and free members, ascetics, and competing theological schools.

Important developments include baptismal practice, Eucharistic worship, local leadership, apostolic succession claims, canon discussions, apologetics, martyr narratives, penitential discipline, care for the poor, Christological debate, and changing relations with Judaism and Roman civic religion.

Do not project fourth-century conciliar language unchanged into the first century. “Persecution” varied by place and time. Evidence includes New Testament writings, apostolic fathers, apologists, inscriptions, archaeology, papyri, martyr acts with source criticism, and Roman sources. Constantine changed the political environment in the fourth century; he did not make earlier diversity disappear overnight.

Sources: [Early Christian Writings](http://www.earlychristianwritings.com/); [Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/).

## 9. Roman history

Roman history can be divided into traditional Kingdom narratives, Republic, Principate, later Empire, Late Antiquity, and post-imperial transformation. The Republic developed offices, assemblies, magistracies, patronage, citizenship, military expansion, slavery, elite competition, and legal institutions. The Empire combined central authority with local elites, taxation, armies, cities, law, roads, imperial cult, and diverse local arrangements.

Everyday life included household religion, marriage, gendered legal status, education, baths, spectacles, associations, funerals, neighborhood politics, military communities, immigrants, and enslaved people. Elite literary sources overrepresent elite men; archaeology, inscriptions, papyri, coins, and material culture broaden the record.

Roman agriculture, labor, trade, taxation, grain supply, and infrastructure connected countryside and city. “Roman culture” was simultaneously a language of imperial integration and a field of local adaptation.

Sources: [Perseus Digital Library](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/); [Roman Inscriptions of Britain](https://romaninscriptionsofbritain.org/); [Metropolitan Museum Roman art](https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/hd/roem/hd_roem.htm).

## 10. German history and culture

German history covers many regions and polities: medieval German-speaking lands, the Reformation, Habsburg and Prussian power, the Holy Roman Empire, Enlightenment and Romanticism, nineteenth-century nationalism and unification, empire and colonialism, World War I, Weimar, Nazism and the Holocaust, division into East and West Germany, reunification, and contemporary federal Germany.

German culture includes regional dialects, religion, guilds, universities, music, philosophy, industrial labor, family, migration, class, gender, civic associations, food, festivals, and media. It should not be treated as a timeless national personality.

The Nazi period must be studied through dictatorship, propaganda, racial law, war, genocide, forced labor, resistance, collaboration, and memory. Nazi “Aryan culture” was an ideological construction, not a neutral description of German society.

Sources: [German Historical Institute](https://www.ghi-dc.org/); [German History in Documents and Images](https://germanhistorydocs.org/); [USHMM](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/).

## 11. Holy Roman Empire

The Holy Roman Empire was a changing political order conventionally dated from 962 to 1806. It was not a centralized nation-state and was not simply Germany. It contained kingdoms, duchies, bishoprics, cities, dynastic lands, estates, and overlapping courts and jurisdictions.

The emperor’s authority interacted with princes, electors, territorial estates, cities, churches, courts, imperial institutions, and local custom. The Golden Bull, Peace of Augsburg, Peace of Westphalia, Imperial Circles, and Imperial Diet illustrate change but do not reduce the system to one constitutional document.

Religion, law, university life, urban crafts, rural lordship, pilgrimage, printing, festivals, household economy, warfare, and migration shaped social life. Confessional coexistence was uneven. Political fragmentation could produce conflict and also competition, patronage, cultural production, and institutional pluralism.

Sources: [German History in Documents and Images](https://germanhistorydocs.org/en/the-holy-roman-empire-1648-1815/introduction); [Britannica HRE](https://www.britannica.com/place/Holy-Roman-Empire).

## 12. Aryan history and the Nazi racial invention

“Aryan” is historically associated with Indo-Iranian linguistic and cultural contexts. European racial theorists turned linguistic relationships into a supposed biological race. Nazi ideology then manipulated the term into a hierarchy, defined belonging and exclusion, and connected it to antisemitism, eugenics, conquest, forced labor, and genocide.

There is no legitimate biological “Aryan race” hierarchy. Research may examine Indo-Iranian languages, texts, migrations, religions, state formation, agriculture, kinship, trade, gender, and law. These are diverse societies over long periods, not one Aryan civilization.

Sources: [USHMM Aryan](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/aryan-1); [USHMM Nazi racism](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/nazi-racism).

## 13. Comparative analytical framework

Compare societies by institutions and mechanisms rather than stereotypes:

- authority: who can command and why;
- legitimacy: religious, dynastic, legal, electoral, customary, or coercive;
- household and kinship;
- land and labor;
- slavery, caste, class, and status;
- gender and age;
- religion and ritual;
- law and dispute resolution;
- cities, trade, and taxation;
- military organization;
- education and literacy;
- minority inclusion and exclusion;
- ecological and technological constraints;
- memory and identity after political change.

A useful comparison names the cases, period, evidence, unit of analysis, and counterfactual. Avoid comparing “the Romans,” “the Germans,” or “the Jews” as if each were a single person.

## 14. Common errors

- Treating doctrine as identical to lived practice.
- Treating one denomination as representative of all Christians.
- Treating “Greek Orthodox” as only an ethnic identity or only a theology.
- Treating the Holy Roman Empire as a modern nation-state.
- Treating Rome as culturally uniform across centuries and provinces.
- Treating German history as beginning with 1871.
- Treating “Aryan” as a valid race rather than a term with linguistic history later racialized.
- Treating ancient texts as transparent records rather than situated sources.
- Ignoring enslaved people, women, workers, migrants, minorities, and rural populations.
- Using civilizational labels to smuggle in claims of innate superiority.
