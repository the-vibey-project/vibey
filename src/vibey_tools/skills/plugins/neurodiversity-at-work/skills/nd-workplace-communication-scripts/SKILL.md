# Neurodiversity at Work: Communication Scripts and the How I Work One-Pager

> **Part 1 of 2** of the *Neurodiversity at Work* reference (plugin `neurodiversity-at-work`). Sibling skill: `nd-accommodation-and-disclosure` (formal accommodation requests, US ADA process, disclosure staging, interview accommodations).
>
> **Currency:** Research citations and the Job Accommodation Network (JAN) material are as of **October 2026**. The scripts themselves are stable.

> **⚠️ Scope.** Educational. Not medical, clinical, psychological or legal advice. Nothing here diagnoses anyone or replaces a clinician, an employment lawyer or your HR team. Evidence strength is marked where it is known; ⚠️ flags claims that are contested, mixed, or rest on a single study.

> **The three ideas:**
> 1. **Write your preferences down once instead of performing them all day.** Masking (camouflaging) is consistently linked to exhaustion and burnout; stating how you work, in writing, removes much of the constant "translation" (§1, §3, §8).
> 2. **Miscommunication between neurotypes is two-way.** Asking the other person to declare *their* style is not extra work or a favour; it shares the load the double empathy problem describes (§1, §4).
> 3. **Standardise the wording.** A small library of tested scripts drops the cognitive cost of each routine interaction close to zero. Adapt them until they sound like you; over-formal scripting can become its own kind of mask (§3, §10).

## 1. Why this works: the evidence in brief

How to read the labels below: **Established** = repeated findings across independent groups; **Emerging** = recent or few studies, consistent so far; **⚠️ Mixed / single-source** = findings conflict, or rest on one study or one site.

### 1.1 Autistic burnout is distinct from ordinary occupational burnout (Emerging)
- **Raymaker et al. (2020)**, the foundational AASPIRE study in *Autism in Adulthood* (doi: 10.1089/aut.2019.0079), defined autistic burnout as a syndrome resulting from chronic life stress and a **mismatch of expectations and abilities without adequate supports**, characterised by **chronic exhaustion, loss of skills, and reduced tolerance to stimulus**.
- **Mantzalas, Richdale and Dissanayake (2022)**, *Autism Research* (doi: 10.1002/aur.2722), proposed a Conceptual Model of Autistic Burnout drawing on the Job Demands-Resources framework and Conservation of Resources theory.
  - **Risk factors:** camouflaging, sensory load, change and transition demands, lack of accommodation.
  - **Protective factors:** autonomy, predictability, accommodation, and time alone.
- **Higgins et al. (2021)**, a grounded Delphi study in *Autism* 25(8): 2356–2369 (doi: 10.1177/13623613211019858), produced a lived-experience definition.
- **Arnold et al. (2023)**, *Autism* 27(7): 1933–1948 (doi: 10.1177/13623613221147401), developed the first peer-reviewed burnout instrument.
- **Bougoure et al. (2025)**, *Autism* (doi: 10.1177/13623613251355255), validated the **AASPIRE Autistic Burnout Measure** in 379 autistic adults: excellent internal consistency (ω = 0.98) and reasonable consistency over 12 months (r = 0.59).
- ⚠️ **Caveat:** this literature is young (2020 onward) and was mostly qualitative or community-developed until very recently. The first validated psychometric instrument is brand new.

### 1.2 Masking (camouflaging) is a major modifiable risk factor (Established link; ⚠️ ranking is interpretive)
- **Hull et al. (2017)**, "Putting on My Best Normal", *Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders* 47(8): 2519–2534 (doi: 10.1007/s10803-017-3166-5), described camouflaging as **masking and compensation**; short- and long-term consequences included exhaustion, challenging stereotypes, and threats to self-perception.
- The **Camouflaging Autistic Traits Questionnaire** (CAT-Q; Hull et al., 2019, doi: 10.1007/s10803-018-3792-6) showed internal consistency α = 0.94 and preliminary test-retest reliability r = 0.77, with convergent validity against autistic traits, wellbeing, anxiety and depression.
- Later reviews (**Cook, Hull, Crane and Mandy, 2021**, *Clinical Psychology Review*) repeatedly link sustained camouflaging to exhaustion, depression, anxiety and autistic burnout.
- **Practical reading:** the main workplace lever is *externalising your preferences* (writing them down, scripting routine asks) so you stop performing a constant translation.
- ⚠️ Calling reduced camouflaging *the* single highest-impact burnout intervention is a synthesis of mostly correlational studies, not the result of a head-to-head trial. Treat it as a strong working hypothesis.

### 1.3 The double empathy problem: communication breakdowns are mutual (Rapport: Established; information transfer: ⚠️ Mixed)
- **Milton (2012)**, "On the ontological status of autism: the double empathy problem", *Disability & Society* 27(6): 883–887 (doi: 10.1080/09687599.2012.710008), recast autistic/non-autistic miscommunication as a **disjuncture in reciprocity between two differently disposed social actors**, not a one-sided deficit.
- **Crompton, Ropar, Evans-Williams, Flynn and Fletcher-Watson (2020)**, "Autistic peer-to-peer information transfer is highly effective", *Autism* 24(7): 1704–1712 (doi: 10.1177/1362361320919286): in N = 72 participants at a single site, autistic people passed information to each other as effectively as non-autistic people did, with breakdowns only in mixed-neurotype pairs.
- **Crompton et al. (2025)**, a Registered Report in *Nature Human Behaviour* (doi: 10.1038/s41562-025-02163-z), N = 311 across three sites:
  - **Replicated:** greater interpersonal rapport in matched-neurotype pairs.
  - **Not replicated at significance:** the information-transfer difference between neurotype conditions. This is a **partial** replication.
- **Practical reading:** scripts that name your style up front, and ask for the other person's, put both people on the same footing and move the empathy burden off you alone.

### 1.4 What helps across neurotypes (from JAN guidance)
- JAN's executive-functioning guidance (askjan.org/articles/Executive-Functioning-Deficits.cfm) describes executive functions as the high-level processes for planning, organising, strategising, attending to and remembering details, starting and stopping actions, and abstract thinking. It lists **checklists, reminders, colour-coded systems, recorded directives, task separation, and apps for concentration**.
- Its social-skills guidance includes **adjusting the supervisory method** and **adjusting the method of communication** to suit the employee.
- These are relevant to ADHD and other executive-function profiles as much as to autism. Most of the research in §1.1–§1.3 was done with autistic adults; the scripts below are useful well beyond that group, but the burnout and masking evidence should not be assumed to transfer unchanged.

## 2. How to use the library

- **Conventions:** plain, direct, low-ambiguity wording. Square brackets are fill-in points. `[chat tool]` means whatever messaging platform your workplace uses.
- **Build a snippets file.** Put the scripts you use most into a text expander or notes file so you can paste them instead of composing from scratch.
- **Most scripts need no disclosure.** Sections 3–9 are ordinary professional preferences that anyone could state. Disclosure and formal requests live in the sibling skill `nd-accommodation-and-disclosure`.
- **Make them yours.** Change the wording until it sounds like you. A script that sounds like a template can itself become masking.
- **Not every script fits every workplace.** Adjust for cultural, industry and seniority norms.

## 3. Stating your own preferences

**3.1 First message to a new colleague.**
> "Good [morning/afternoon], [Name]. I want to make our collaboration efficient from day one. I work best with written instructions and concrete deliverables. Would you prefer that I send questions by email, post them in [chat tool], or schedule a short call? I am happy to match your preferred channel."

**3.2 First message to a new manager.**
> "Hello [Name]. To make sure I give you my best work, here is a short summary of how I work most effectively: I prefer written or asynchronous communication for anything detailed; I do well with explicit deadlines and explicit priorities; I produce my highest-quality work in uninterrupted blocks; and I appreciate direct, specific feedback. I can adjust to your preferences as well. What works best for you for status updates, weekly cadence, and urgent issues?"

**3.3 Sharing a How I Work one-pager** (template in §8).
> "Attached is a one-page reference of how I work most effectively. It covers preferred channels, response time expectations, how I like to receive feedback, and what helps me deliver the highest quality. Please feel free to share yours; I will use it the same way."

**3.4 Stating a preference without disclosing anything.**
> "I retain details more accurately when they are in writing. Could you send the requirements by email or in a [chat tool] message so I can refer back to them while I work?"

**3.5 Asking for processing time.**
> "I want to give you a careful answer rather than a fast one. Can I respond by [end of day / tomorrow morning / Friday]?"

**3.6 Asking for direct language.**
> "To make sure I have it right: are you asking me to [restate the task in concrete terms]? I want to avoid acting on an assumption."

## 4. Asking others about their preferences

Asking the other person to declare their style is the reciprocal half of §3, and the practical answer to the double empathy problem (§1.3). Ask a few at a time, not all at once.

### 4.1 Core dimensions
| Dimension | Example question |
|---|---|
| **Direct vs inferred** | "Do you prefer that I say things plainly, or would you rather I soften and you read between the lines?" |
| **Sync vs async** | "For most questions, do you prefer a message I can send any time, or a quick live conversation?" |
| **Camera** | "On video calls, do you mind if cameras are off during working sessions?" |
| **Precision vs brevity** | "Do you want the full detail, or the short version with detail on request?" |
| **Why vs what/how** | "When you hand me something, do you prefer to start with the reasoning, or with the concrete steps?" |
| **Cadence** | "How often would you like updates on this: daily, weekly, or at milestones?" |
| **Response speed** | "How quickly do you expect a reply to a message, and how quickly can I expect one from you?" |
| **Visual vs text vs audio** | "Do you take things in best from a diagram, written text, or a conversation?" |

### 4.2 Extended questions
- **Decision-making style.** "When you have a decision to make, do you prefer that I bring two or three options with a recommendation, or that I present the full analysis and let you choose?"
- **Risk and escalation threshold.** "At what point do you want to be looped in on a problem? When I first see it, when I have a plan, or only if the plan does not work?"
- **Disagreement style.** "When you and I disagree on an approach, do you prefer that I push back in the moment, send a written counter-proposal afterwards, or bring in a third reviewer?"
- **Documentation level.** "How much documentation do you expect for a typical change? A one-line note, a paragraph, or a full write-up?"
- **Meeting purpose.** "Do you use this recurring meeting for decisions, status, or relationship? Knowing this helps me prepare the right inputs."
- **Feedback timing.** "Do you prefer to give feedback in the moment, at a regular one-on-one, or in writing?"
- **After-hours boundary.** "What is your expectation for messages sent after [business hours / on weekends]? I want to respect your time and protect mine."
- **Channel for urgency.** "If something is urgent, what is the best way to reach you, and what counts as urgent in your view?"
- **Conflict recovery.** "If we have a miscommunication, how do you prefer to resolve it: a quick call, a written follow-up, or letting it cool down first?"
- **Trust and autonomy.** "For a task at this scope, would you like check-ins at milestones, daily, or only on completion?"

## 5. Getting instructions clear and in writing

### 5.1 Clarifying ambiguous or implicit instructions
- **General clarifier.** "To confirm scope before I start: the deliverable is [concrete artifact], the deadline is [date], and the success criterion is [measurable outcome]. Is that correct?"
- **When wording is vague.** "Can you give me a concrete example of what good looks like for this? I want to align on the bar before I invest time."
- **When priority is unclear.** "I have [A], [B], and [C] open. If you had to rank them this week, what is the order?"
- **When implicit context is suspected.** "Is there context about the [customer / stakeholder history / prior decisions] I should know before I proceed?"
- **When a request is conditional.** "Just to confirm: if [condition], you want [action], and if not, you want [other action]. Is that the logic?"
- **After a thumbs-up or one-word reply.** "Thanks. To make sure I read this correctly: does that mean approved as written, or approved with the changes we discussed [when]?"

### 5.2 Converting verbal instructions to written
- **In the moment.** "Let me echo this back so we are aligned: [restate]. I will send a short recap by email after this call. Please reply with corrections if I got anything wrong."
- **After the call.** "Recap of our [time] discussion. Decisions: [list]. Action items and owners: [list]. Deadlines: [list]. Open questions: [list]. Please reply 'confirmed' or with edits within [24/48 hours]."
- **Asking for instructions in writing** (mirrors JAN's Written Instructions accommodation, askjan.org/solutions/Written-Instructions.cfm). "I retain and execute on written instructions more reliably. Could you send the requirements for [task] in an email or [chat tool] message? I will use it as my source of truth and reduce the risk of rework."

### 5.3 Asking for context: why versus what/how
- **Asking for the why.** "Before I implement, can you give me the why behind this decision? It helps me make better calls downstream when edge cases come up."
- **Asking for the what/how.** "I have enough on the why. What I need now is the concrete what: the exact inputs, outputs, and acceptance criteria."
- **Asking for stakeholder context.** "Who is the audience for this, and what decision are they trying to make? That will shape how I write it up."

## 6. Time, focus and meetings

### 6.1 Requesting processing time
- **In a meeting.** "I want to give you a thoughtful answer rather than a reflexive one. Can I get back to you by [specific time]?"
- **On a high-stakes decision.** "This is significant enough that I want to think it through and check the data. Can I send you a written recommendation by end of business [day]?"
- **When pressed to commit on the spot.** "I can commit to [the smallest concrete thing I am sure about] now and confirm the full scope by [time]."
- **When asked an unexpected question.** "I have not thought about that before. Give me until [time] and I will come back with a real answer."

### 6.2 Meetings: agendas, alternatives, duration, cameras
- **Asking for an agenda.** "Happy to attend. Could you share the agenda and desired outcome ahead of time so I come prepared with the right inputs? If it is mainly status, I can also send a written update and save us the calendar block."
- **Proposing an async substitute.** "Would a short written update or a recorded walkthrough work in place of this meeting? Happy to attend if a live decision is needed."
- **Declining a non-essential meeting.** "I will not be able to make this one. Could you record it or share the notes? I will follow up on any action items that involve me."
- **Renegotiating duration.** "Can we book 25 minutes instead of an hour? If we need more, we can extend at the end."
- **Camera, partial.** "Cameras on for the first five minutes to greet everyone, then cameras off when we share screens and work through detail. I find I focus and contribute better that way."
- **Camera, off.** "I am going to keep my camera off for this one to focus. I will stay engaged on audio and chat."
- **One-on-one instead of a group call** (mirrors JAN's One-on-One Communication strategy). "I would like to talk this through one-on-one before we bring it to the group. Are you free for fifteen minutes today?"
- **Meeting-free blocks.** "I am blocking [day/time] for deep work on [project]. Please use email or [chat tool] for anything that is not on fire during those hours."

### 6.3 Managing interruptions and protecting focus
- **Setting expectations up front.** "When my status shows 'focused', I am in deep work. I check messages at [interval]. For anything urgent, please [urgent channel]."
- **Deflecting an interruption in the moment.** "I am in the middle of something I need to finish in the next [time]. Can I come find you at [specific time]?"
- **Closing an unscheduled drop-in.** "Got it. Let me finish what I am on and I will get you a real answer by [time]. Sending a calendar invite."
- **Muting a busy channel.** "I am going to mute [channel] for the rest of this [sprint / week] to stay focused. Please tag me directly if you need me."

### 6.4 Meeting overload and capacity signalling
- **Weekly capacity message to a manager.** "Heads-up on capacity. I have [N] hours of meetings this week, which is leaving me [M] hours of focus time. To hit [deliverable] on time, I need to either decline [meeting X], move it to async, or push [deliverable] to [new date]. What is your preference?"
- **Asking for a meeting-free day.** "I would like to keep [one weekday] meeting-free for deep work. I can flex if something is truly urgent. Are you good with that?"
- **Capping back-to-back calls.** "I do not schedule more than [three] calls in a row to keep quality high. If we need another, I will move it to the next morning."
- **End-of-day wind-down.** "I am wrapping for the day. I will pick this up first thing tomorrow at [time]."

## 7. Boundaries, feedback, disagreement and repair

### 7.1 Saying no without over-explaining
- **Simple no.** "I cannot take this on right now. My current commitments are [A] and [B], and adding this would put one of them at risk. Can [name] cover, or can we deprioritise [A]?"
- **No to scope creep.** "That is outside the current scope. I can do it as a follow-on after we ship the current milestone, or we can renegotiate the deadline."
- **No to an after-hours request.** "I will pick this up first thing tomorrow."
- **No to a meeting.** "I will not attend, but I will read the notes and respond on any action items."
- **No when you lack the bandwidth to explain.** "I cannot do this. Let me know how to help find another path."
- **Why short works:** explanations invite negotiation; a clear no does not.

### 7.2 Asking for specific, concrete feedback
- **General request.** "Can you give me feedback that is specific and concrete: one thing to keep doing, one thing to stop, one thing to start. Examples help more than adjectives."
- **Mid-project.** "Before I go further, is the direction right? Specifically, is [A] meeting the bar, is [B] the right approach, and am I missing anything you expected to see?"
- **On a deliverable.** "What in this would you change if it were yours? Please be direct; I prefer precision over softening."
- **In a one-on-one.** "If you had to rate my work this [period] on three dimensions, [quality], [communication], and [collaboration], what would each be and why?"

### 7.3 Raising issues and disagreements
- **With a peer, low stakes.** "I see this differently. Here is my reasoning: [one or two sentences]. What am I missing?"
- **With a peer, substantive disagreement.** "Before we commit, I want to flag a concern with [approach]. Specifically, [risk]. I would propose [alternative]. Can we spend ten minutes on this before we move on?"
- **With a manager.** "I want to raise a concern about [issue]. I am bringing it to you because [reason]. The impact I am seeing is [impact]. What I would recommend is [recommendation]. What is your perspective?"
- **With a client or external stakeholder.** "I want to be transparent about a risk we have identified in [area]. Here is what we know, here is what we recommend, and here is what we need from your team to mitigate it."
- **Repair after a heated exchange.** "I want to come back to our exchange earlier. I was direct in a way that may not have landed well. My intent was [intent]. Can we restart on [topic]?"

### 7.4 Repair scripts: recovering from a misunderstanding
- **You misunderstood.** "I want to correct course. I had been reading [their message] as [my read], and now I see you meant [correct read]. Here is what I will change in my work going forward."
- **They misunderstood you.** "I think I was unclear earlier. What I meant was [intent]. Sorry for the confusion. Does that change anything on your side?"
- **Tone repair.** "Reading my last message back, the tone may have come across as sharper than I intended. To be clear, the concern stands, but I respect the work you put in and I want to land on a good answer together."
- **After a missed social cue.** "I think I may have missed something in the conversation earlier. If I came across as [dismissive / abrupt / off topic], please tell me directly and I will adjust."

## 8. Delivery: deadlines, status, change and handoffs

### 8.1 Deadlines, scope, turnaround
- **Renegotiating a deadline early.** "Based on what I have found since we scoped this, the realistic delivery date is [date], not [original date]. The driver is [reason]. I can hit the original date if we cut [X] or add [Y]. Which would you prefer?"
- **Renegotiating scope.** "To hit [date], we need to either remove [item] from this milestone or add [resource]. What is your preference?"
- **Stating a turnaround commitment.** "I will deliver [artifact] by [date and time]. If I see slippage, I will notify you no later than [earlier checkpoint]."
- **Asking a client or stakeholder about their turnaround.** "What is your expected response time on a question from me, and what is your turnaround on review of a deliverable? I want to build that into the schedule."

### 8.2 Status updates and proactive progress communication
- **Weekly written update.** "Week of [date]. Completed: [list]. In progress: [list]. Blocked or at risk: [list with cause and what I need]. Next week: [list]. Decisions I need from you: [list]."
- **Same-day slippage flag.** "Heads up: [task] is at risk of slipping by [duration] because of [cause]. My plan to recover is [plan]. I do not need anything from you yet, but I wanted you to know now."
- **Closing the loop.** "[Task] is done. It is in [location]. Checked with [method]. Known limitations: [list]. Recommended next step: [next step]."

### 8.3 Handling unexpected changes and transitions
- **First reaction.** "Thanks for letting me know. I want to make sure I understand the change correctly: was [old], now [new], effective [when]? Let me think through what this means for [current commitments] and come back to you by [time] with an updated plan."
- **Asking for the reason.** "To plan well, can you give me the reason behind the change? It helps me make better trade-off decisions downstream."
- **Asking for it in writing.** "Could you send the change in writing, even as a quick [chat tool] message? I want to make sure I work from the current version of the plan, not the previous one."

### 8.4 Delegating, handing off and clarifying ownership
- **Handing off.** "I am handing this to you. Current state: [where it is]. What is done: [list]. What is left: [list]. Known issues: [list]. Reference docs: [links]. I am available for questions through [date], best by [channel]."
- **Asking for clear ownership.** "Who is the directly responsible person on this? I want one person to ask for decisions rather than guessing."
- **Confirming you are taking ownership.** "I am taking [task]. By [date] I will deliver [artifact]. I will check in at [milestone]."

## 9. Social energy and capacity signals

### 9.1 Small talk, social entry and exit
- **Low-effort entry.** "Hi [Name], good to see you. Did you have a chance to look at [topic]?"
- **Graceful exit.** "I need to get back to [task]. Talk soon."
- **Start of a call.** "Happy to chat for a minute. I have a hard stop in [N] minutes. Where do you want to start?"
- **Declining a social event without offence.** "I am going to skip the [team lunch] today, I need the recharge. See you tomorrow."
- **Honest small-talk substitute.** "I am not great at small talk; I would rather hear what you are working on. What is on your plate?"

### 9.2 End-of-day and end-of-week signals
- **End of day.** "Logging off. Picking up tomorrow with [top priority]. If anything is urgent overnight, [escalation path]."
- **End of week.** "Wrapping the week. Status: [one or two sentence summary]. Next week I will start with [task]. Have a good weekend."
- **Pre-emptive recharge day.** "I am taking [day] as a recharge day to maintain capacity for the [project] push next week. Out of office on email. Back [day]."

## 10. The How I Work one-pager

The recommended communication-preferences artifact. **Keep it to one page, plain prose, no jargon.** Share it with your direct manager and immediate working team, pair it with the §4 questions so it invites a reply, and update the date when it changes. It describes preferences, not a diagnosis; sharing it is not disclosure.

```
HOW I WORK MOST EFFECTIVELY
Last updated: [date]. Please ask me about anything that is unclear.

Role and current focus.
[Title]. Currently working on [one-line summary].

Best channel for each kind of message.
- Quick question: [chat tool].
- Question that needs a substantive answer: email or a [chat tool] post with full context.
- Decision that needs discussion: [25]-minute one-on-one with an agenda.
- Urgent issue: [phone call / direct mention with the word "urgent"].

Response time you can expect.
- Chat during working hours: within [two hours].
- Email: same working day for items received before [time], next morning otherwise.
- After hours or weekends: I do not monitor; please use [channel] for true emergencies.

Meetings.
- I do my best work with an agenda sent at least [24 hours] in advance.
- I keep my camera on for the first few minutes for hellos, then often switch it
  off so I can focus, especially during detailed or technical discussion.
- I prefer [25]-minute meetings over [60]-minute meetings.
- For decisions that can be made async, please consider a written proposal first.

How I take in information.
- I retain and act on written instructions more reliably than verbal ones. After a
  verbal discussion I will send a short written recap; please reply with corrections.
- I prefer direct, specific language. If you mean "no", please say "no". If something
  is a hard deadline, please say so.
- I prefer concrete examples over abstract descriptions.

How I produce my best work.
- I have [two] protected deep-work blocks per day (typically [times]). During those
  blocks I do not check messages.
- I do my highest-quality work when I have the "why" up front and clear acceptance
  criteria.
- I prefer to bring you two or three options with a recommendation rather than a
  single answer or an open menu.

How I prefer feedback.
- Direct, specific, with examples.
- I would rather hear a hard truth in private than a softened version in public.
- One thing to keep doing, one to stop, one to start, is my favourite format.

What I will do for you.
- Send a weekly written status ([day]) covering completed, in progress, at risk,
  next week, and decisions needed.
- Flag slippage as soon as I see it, not at the deadline.
- Respect the same boundaries you set for me.

What helps me deliver the best work.
- Clear scope and a clear priority order when multiple items compete.
- Decisions in writing, or a recap by email.
- Notice of unexpected changes as soon as they happen, with the reason.

What I would like to know about you.
- How do you prefer status updates: written, verbal, dashboard?
- How direct should I be with bad news?
- When do you want to be looped in: at first sign, with a plan, or only if the plan fails?
- What does urgent mean for you, and what is the right channel for it?
```

**Adapting it:** delete any line that is not true for you. An ADHD-leaning version might add reminders, checklists or body-doubling preferences; a sensory-leaning version might add lighting, noise or headphone notes. Keep only what you want colleagues to act on.

## 11. Rolling it out in stages

| Stage | When | What | Disclosure needed? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Scripts and one-pager** | Week 1 | Adopt §3, §4, §5 and §8.2 scripts immediately; build your snippets file; share the How I Work one-pager with your manager and immediate team, paired with §4 questions | **No** |
| **2. Soft request** | Weeks 2–4 | Ask your manager, in writing, for the lowest-friction adjustments (written instructions, advance agendas, flexible schedule with protected focus blocks, camera-optional internal meetings). Wording: `nd-accommodation-and-disclosure` §4 | Optional; no diagnosis required |
| **3. Formal process** | Months 2–3, if needed | If burnout indicators rise or informal requests fail, file a formal written accommodation request. Process and letters: `nd-accommodation-and-disclosure` §5–§6 | Usually some, to HR |
| **4. Ongoing self-check** | Weekly | Run the §12 check and act on its triggers | No |

⚠️ The week and month markers are a suggested pacing, not evidence-based thresholds. Move faster if you are already depleted.

## 12. Weekly burnout self-check (two minutes)

Track four numbers each week:
1. **Hours in meetings.**
2. **Hours of deep work.**
3. **Hours spent masking** (your own rough estimate).
4. **How recovered you felt** at the start of the work week.

**Act on these triggers:**
- **Meetings exceed deep work two weeks in a row:** send the weekly capacity message (§6.4).
- **Recovery incomplete at the start of the week, two weeks in a row:** take a recharge day (§9.2).
- **Indicators rising:** sleep disruption, masking feeling more costly, weekend recovery bleeding into the working week, reduced tolerance for sensory input. These are consistent with the burnout features the AASPIRE measure captures (Bougoure et al., 2025); if they persist, move to Stage 3 (§11).
- **Protective factors to protect:** autonomy over your schedule and protected solitude (Mantzalas et al., 2022).

⚠️ This is a personal tracking habit, not a validated screening tool, and the trigger thresholds are rules of thumb. If you are losing skills, struggling to function, or feel unsafe, talk to a clinician you trust.

---

## 13. For managers and colleagues

Most of the scripts above are one side of a two-way exchange. Things that make them land:
- **Answer the preference questions (§4) honestly**, and share your own one-pager. Reciprocity is the point (double empathy, §1.3).
- **Put instructions in writing**, or reply "confirmed" to a written recap. It costs little and removes guesswork (JAN Written Instructions).
- **Replace thumbs-up and one-word replies** with an explicit decision when it matters: approved as written, approved with changes, or not approved.
- **Say the real thing.** If you mean no, say no; if a deadline is hard, say it is hard; give the reason for a change when you announce it.
- **Send agendas with a desired outcome**, and accept a written update in place of a status meeting.
- **Treat processing-time requests as diligence**, not evasion. A considered answer tomorrow usually beats a reflexive one now.
- **Give feedback specifically, privately and with examples.** Keep-stop-start is a good default.
- **Respect focus blocks and camera-off working sessions.** They are low-cost (most accommodations cost nothing; see `nd-accommodation-and-disclosure` §1.2 and §2).
- **Do not ask for, or speculate about, a diagnosis.** A stated preference is enough to act on.

## 14. Limits and caveats

- **Evidence base.** Burnout research is young (§1.1). The double empathy information-transfer finding is mixed after a larger replication (§1.3). Most cited studies are of autistic adults, not ADHD or other profiles.
- **Context.** These scripts assume a broadly professional, written-communication-friendly workplace. Adjust for culture, sector, seniority and language.
- **Scripts are starting points, not constraints.** Over-formal scripting can become a form of masking. Shorten, soften or roughen them until they sound like you.
- **Preferences are not rights.** Informal requests can be declined. If an adjustment is necessary rather than preferred, the formal route in `nd-accommodation-and-disclosure` gives it more protection.
