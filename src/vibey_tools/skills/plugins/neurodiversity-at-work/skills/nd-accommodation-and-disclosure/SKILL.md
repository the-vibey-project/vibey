# Neurodiversity at Work: Accommodations and Disclosure

> **Part 2 of 2** of the *Neurodiversity at Work* reference (plugin `neurodiversity-at-work`). Sibling skill: `nd-workplace-communication-scripts` (everyday script library, the How I Work one-pager, burnout self-check).
>
> **Currency:** Research citations, Job Accommodation Network (JAN) material and US legal framework as summarised in **October 2026**. Laws, agency guidance and filing deadlines change; confirm the version in force.

> **⚠️ Scope.** Educational. **Not legal, medical or clinical advice.** The legal material in §3 and the hiring material in §7 describe the **United States** (ADA, EEOC, JAN) only. Other jurisdictions work differently. Before relying on any protection, especially if you face discipline, a performance process or dismissal, talk to an employment lawyer, a union or an official advice service where you work. ⚠️ flags claims that are contested, mixed, or rest on a single study.

> **The three ideas:**
> 1. **You do not have to name a diagnosis to ask for help.** Under the US ADA a request only has to say you need a change at work for a reason related to a medical condition, in plain language (§3, §4).
> 2. **Ask for specific, concrete adjustments.** A clear list of functional needs is what gets acted on; most of the useful ones cost nothing (§1, §2).
> 3. **Disclosure is a staged, personal decision with real downside risk.** Disclose the minimum needed, to the fewest people needed, in writing, and escalate only when a softer step has not worked (§1.3, §5, §7).

## 1. What the evidence says

### 1.1 Accommodations work, and the onus usually falls on the employee
- **Davies et al. (2022)**, *PLOS ONE* 17(8): e0272420 (doi: 10.1371/journal.pone.0272420), surveyed 181 autistic adults in the UK about requesting and receiving workplace adjustments. The onus fell on them to:
  1. identify their need for adjustments;
  2. establish which specific adjustments would help;
  3. request those adjustments from their employer.
  Adjustments, when received, materially improved retention.
- **Heinze (2025)**, a systematic review in *Cureus* 17(12): e99353 (doi: 10.7759/cureus.99353), pooled ten empirical studies from 2010–2025 and concluded that accommodations were linked to better job acquisition, stability, satisfaction and productivity. The strongest evidence was for:
  - individualised sensory and environmental adaptations;
  - flexible scheduling;
  - adjustments to how supervisors communicate;
  - written instructions.
  ⚠️ One review of a small evidence base; treat the ranking as indicative.
- **Practical reading:** because you will probably have to do steps 1–3 yourself, arrive with the list already made (§2, §6).

### 1.2 Most accommodations cost nothing (employer-reported)
- JAN's **Workplace Accommodations: Low Cost, High Impact** report (updated April 2024; data through 31 December 2023; 1,000+ employer responses):
  - **56%** of employers said the accommodations they made cost **nothing ($0)** to implement.
  - **67%** said the accommodations were **very or extremely effective**.
- ⚠️ These are employer self-reports from organisations that contacted JAN, not a random sample. They are still the most widely cited figures, and useful when an employer worries about cost.

### 1.3 Disclosure has documented risks
- **Romualdez et al. (2021)**, *Autism in Adulthood* 3(2): 157–167 (doi: 10.1089/aut.2020.0063), reported qualitatively that some autistic adults who disclosed experienced a lack of support, understanding and acceptance; bullying; and failure to receive reasonable adjustments.
- ⚠️ This is qualitative; no percentage is reported, so it cannot tell you how *likely* a bad outcome is. It does show the negative path is real and should be weighed.
- ⚠️ **No consensus on the right strategy.** The autism advocacy literature is split on when and whether to disclose. The staging below is a cautious default, not the only defensible approach.

### 1.4 Disclosure plus a concrete list beats a vague disclosure (⚠️ synthesis)
Combining the above: a disclosure that arrives with a short, specific list of functional adjustments gives an employer something to act on. A bare label ("I am autistic", "I have ADHD") leaves them to guess, and the guess may be wrong. This is a practical synthesis of §1.1–§1.3, not a tested comparison.

## 2. The JAN accommodation menu

JAN (the Job Accommodation Network, askjan.org) is a US service. Its legal guidance is US-specific, but the accommodation ideas travel well.

### 2.1 High-leverage accommodations (from JAN's autism guidance, askjan.org/disabilities/Autism-Spectrum.cfm)
| Accommodation | What it looks like | JAN page |
|---|---|---|
| **Written instructions** | Communicating by email when possible, meeting transcripts, agendas for trainings, checklists for tasks | askjan.org/solutions/Written-Instructions.cfm |
| **Flexible schedule** | Adjusted start and end times; work done during the hours when you are most mentally alert | askjan.org/solutions/Flexible-Schedule.cfm |
| **Telework** | Some or all days remote | (Autism page) |
| **Uninterrupted off work time** | Protected blocks with no meetings or messages | (Autism page) |
| **One-on-one communication** | Complex or sensitive topics handled one-to-one before group settings | (Autism page) |
| **Noise-cancelling headsets** | Sensory load reduction | (Autism page) |
| **Modified break schedule** | Breaks timed to need | (Autism page) |
| **Job restructuring** | Reassigning marginal (non-essential) functions | (Autism page) |
| **Supervisory methods** | Adjusting how instructions, feedback and check-ins are given | (Autism page) |

### 2.2 Executive-function supports (askjan.org/articles/Executive-Functioning-Deficits.cfm)
Relevant to ADHD and other executive-function profiles as much as to autism:
- checklists and reminders;
- a colour-coded system;
- recorded directives;
- task separation (breaking large tasks into steps);
- apps for concentration;
- adjusting the supervisory method and the method of communication to suit the employee.

### 2.3 A starter set to ask for first
The four lowest-friction requests, none with a cash cost:
1. **Written instructions** for assignments, and written confirmation of verbal instructions.
2. **Advance agendas** for meetings.
3. **A flexible schedule** with protected focus blocks (for example, two per day).
4. **Camera-optional internal meetings.**

## 3. The US legal framework (ADA) in brief

> **⚠️ US only, and not legal advice.** This summarises the general shape of US federal law. State and local law may add protections. Confirm with HR, an employment lawyer, or the EEOC before relying on it, especially in any adverse-action situation.

### 3.1 Who is covered
- **ADA Title I** (as broadened by the **ADA Amendments Act of 2008**, ADAAA) applies to private employers with **15 or more employees** and to state and local government employers.
- The **Rehabilitation Act** extends similar protections to **federal agencies and federal contractors**.
- Employers must provide **reasonable accommodations** during the application process and on the job, unless doing so would cause **undue hardship**.
- **Autism and ADHD** can qualify as disabilities when they substantially limit major life activities such as concentrating, thinking, communicating, learning or interacting with others. The post-2008 standard makes this threshold easier to meet than it once was. Whether a particular person is covered is an individual, fact-specific question.

### 3.2 How a request works
- **No magic words.** Per JAN's Dos and Don'ts of Disclosure (askjan.org/articles/Dos-and-Don-ts-of-Disclosure.cfm), you only have to let your employer know you need an adjustment or change at work for a reason related to a medical condition. You can use plain English and do not have to mention the ADA or say "reasonable accommodation". JAN's page on recognising requests (askjan.org/articles/Recognizing-an-Accommodation-Request-Under-the-ADA.cfm) makes the same point.
- **No diagnosis required up front.** A request can describe the functional limitation and the adjustment, without naming a condition.
- **The interactive process** is the legal term for the back-and-forth between you and the employer that settles which accommodations are put in place. Best practice is to **propose specific accommodations in writing**.
- **Documentation.** The employer may ask for **reasonable documentation** that you have a covered disability that needs accommodation. It cannot demand your full medical records, and should accept documentation from an appropriate health-care, rehabilitation or vocational professional.
- **Effectiveness is ongoing.** If an agreed accommodation does not work, you can ask to reopen the interactive process (template §6.5).

### 3.3 Disclosure timing and confidentiality
- The ADA does **not** require disclosure at any particular time. You can disclose during an application, after an interview invitation, after an offer, or only when an accommodation is needed on the job.
- **Pre-offer questions designed to draw out disability information are prohibited.**
- Disclosure to coworkers is rarely necessary, and the employer must keep medical information confidential; it is not theirs to share for you.

### 3.4 Deadlines if something goes wrong
- The window to file an ADA discrimination charge with the **EEOC** is generally **180 days**, extended to **300 days** where a state or local agency enforces an equivalent law. Check the current rule promptly; do not wait to see how things play out.

### 3.5 Outside the US
Other jurisdictions have their own frameworks and vocabulary. The UK's Equality Act 2010 framework uses the term "reasonable adjustments", and EU member states have their own national laws. Thresholds, procedures, documentation rules and deadlines all differ. Use a local official advice service or lawyer, and treat §3.1–§3.4 as US-only.

## 4. Scripts: from no disclosure to soft disclosure

Ordered from least to most disclosure. Square brackets are fill-in points. `[chat tool]` is your workplace messaging platform.

**4.1 Preference only, no disclosure.** (More in `nd-workplace-communication-scripts` §3.)
> "I retain details more accurately when they are in writing. Could you send the requirements by email or in a [chat tool] message so I can refer back to them while I work?"

**4.2 Soft disclosure, no diagnosis stated (preferred opening).** Modelled on JAN's Dos and Don'ts of Disclosure.
> "I need an adjustment at work because of a medical condition. Specifically, I work most effectively with written instructions, advance agendas, and the ability to use uninterrupted focus blocks. Could we set up a short conversation to discuss?"

**4.3 Disclosure naming the condition (only when you choose to).**
> "I am [autistic / have ADHD / condition]. I am sharing this because I want to request a small set of accommodations that will let me do my best work and reduce the risk of burnout. None of them are expensive or unusual. Specifically, I would like to formalise: [written instructions for assignments]; [advance agendas for meetings]; [flexible scheduling around deep-work blocks]; [the option to keep my camera off during working sessions]; and [one-on-one communication for complex topics]. I am happy to go through the interactive process to refine these."

Tips:
- Lead with the functional need; the label is optional context.
- Keep the list short and concrete; you can add more later.
- If you have more than one condition, you only need to raise what is relevant to the accommodations you are asking for, framed as present functional needs (⚠️ general practice, single-source).

## 5. Staged disclosure on the job

A cautious default ladder. Move up a rung only when the rung below has not worked or your needs have grown.

| Stage | When | Action | Who learns what |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. No disclosure** | From day one | Use the everyday scripts and the How I Work one-pager (`nd-workplace-communication-scripts`) | Colleagues learn preferences only |
| **2. Soft disclosure** | Weeks 2–4 | Use §4.2 with your direct manager; ask in writing for the §2.3 starter set | Manager learns there is a medical reason, not what it is |
| **3. Formal interactive process** | Months 2–3, or when triggered | File the §6.1 written request with HR and your manager; reference JAN by URL | HR learns what documentation requires; manager learns the accommodations |
| **4. Ongoing** | Continuous | Check effectiveness; use §6.5 if an accommodation stops working | No new disclosure |

⚠️ The timings are a suggested pacing, not evidence-based thresholds.

### 5.1 Triggers to move from Stage 2 to Stage 3
Move to the formal process if any of these happen:
- **(a)** two reasonable informal requests are not honoured;
- **(b)** a conduct or performance concern is raised that connects to an unmet need;
- **(c)** burnout indicators (sleep disruption, masking feeling more costly, recovery time bleeding into the working week, reduced tolerance for sensory input) stay elevated for **more than four weeks**.

The formal interactive process is your protection. Under US law it does not require the word "ADA", but a written request creates a dated record.

### 5.2 Disclosing to whom
- **Manager:** a personal call. Many people disclose the need (Stage 2) without the label.
- **HR:** the usual recipient of documentation in a formal process.
- **Coworkers:** rarely necessary. Your preferences can be shared without any medical context.

## 6. Formal letters and follow-ups (US-oriented templates)

Adapt to your situation. These are templates, not legal advice. Keep copies of everything you send and receive, with dates.

### 6.1 Written accommodation request (modelled on JAN's sample letter, askjan.org/media/accommrequestltr.cfm)
```
Date: [date]
To: [HR contact] and [direct manager]
Subject: Request for reasonable accommodation [under the ADA]

I am writing to request reasonable accommodations [under the Americans with
Disabilities Act]. I have a medical condition that affects [functional areas, for
example: communication processing, sensory regulation, executive functioning,
sustained social interaction].

To perform the essential functions of my role at my highest level, I am requesting
the following accommodations:
(1) written instructions for assignments, and confirmation of verbal instructions
    in writing;
(2) advance meeting agendas of at least [24 hours] where feasible;
(3) a flexible schedule that allows me to do focused work during my most alert
    hours;
(4) [two] protected, uninterrupted focus blocks per day of at least [90] minutes;
(5) the option to keep my camera off during internal working sessions;
(6) one-on-one communication for complex disagreements before group escalation;
(7) [continued telework on [days], consistent with current policy].

I am happy to provide medical documentation if needed and to take part in the
interactive process to refine these. Please let me know within [seven to fourteen
days] who will be coordinating this request.

Thank you,
[Name]
```
- **Reference JAN by URL** (for example the Workplace Accommodation Toolkit, askjan.org/toolkit/). It shows you have done the homework and tells HR where to look.
- **Delete what you do not need.** Every line should be a real need.

### 6.2 JAN's other template-of-record documents
- Sample Language for Accommodation Request Letters: askjan.org/articles/Sample-Language-for-Accommodation-Request-Letters.cfm
- Employee Accommodation Inquiry Letter: askjan.org/publications/consultants-corner/Employee-Accommodation-Inquiry-Letter.cfm
- Workplace Accommodation Toolkit: askjan.org/toolkit/
- Employees' Practical Guide to Requesting and Negotiating Reasonable Accommodation: askjan.org/publications/individuals/employee-guide.cfm

### 6.3 Inquiry letter if HR does not respond (from JAN's Employee Accommodation Inquiry Letter)
> "I am writing to inquire about the status of the accommodation request I submitted on [date]. Please let me know if you need additional information. I am willing to provide further information if necessary so the request can be processed and accommodations can be put into place quickly."

### 6.4 Confirming agreed accommodations (after a meeting or an offer)
> "Thank you for meeting on [date]. To confirm what we agreed: [accommodation 1], [accommodation 2], [accommodation 3], starting [date]. We will review how these are working on [date]. Please let me know if I have recorded anything incorrectly."

### 6.5 Telling the employer an accommodation is not effective
> "The accommodation we agreed on [date], [accommodation], is not fully resolving the original limitation. Specifically, [what is still occurring]. I would like to reconvene the interactive process to explore [alternative]. Could we meet within the next two weeks?"

## 7. Hiring: disclosure and interview accommodations (US)

> **⚠️ US only, and not legal advice.** The same ADA framework (§3) covers the application process.

### 7.1 Staged disclosure when applying
| Stage | Action | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Before applying** | Do not disclose on your CV or public profile unless applying through a dedicated neurodiversity hiring programme | The **voluntary self-identification** form on some US applications is governed by separate federal-contractor reporting rules (Section 503 of the Rehabilitation Act) and is meant to be kept apart from hiring decision-makers |
| **2. After an interview invitation** | Send a written accommodation request to the recruiter, in **functional, not diagnostic**, language (§7.3) | A single named contact is easier than rotating contacts |
| **3. Diagnosis** | Only if pressed, and only to HR or the recruiter, not the hiring manager or panel | Framed as present functional needs (§7.4) |
| **4. After the offer** | Re-confirm accommodations for onboarding and ongoing work in a dated email (§6.4) | Creates a record of the interactive process |
| **5. On the job** | Follow the §5 ladder | Telling the manager is a personal call; coworkers rarely need to know |

⚠️ **A pragmatic view, not evidence:** for experienced candidates, a well-framed request is often read as professional self-awareness, and an employer that reacts badly to a reasonable request has told you something useful about working there.

### 7.2 Interview accommodations commonly granted as reasonable
Drawn from EEOC guidance, JAN practical guides and industry practice:
- interview questions **provided in advance**, or shown in the chat during virtual interviews;
- **extended time** on assessments;
- a **take-home or asynchronous** assessment instead of timed live exercises (for example, live coding);
- **written design documents or structured walk-throughs** in place of open-ended behavioural interviews;
- **breaks of 5–10 minutes** between rounds;
- **written follow-ups** instead of on-the-spot answers;
- **video instead of phone** (or the reverse);
- **one point of contact** rather than rotating recruiters;
- a **structured format** with predictable question categories;
- confirmation of a **quiet environment**;
- a **no-camera option** to manage sensory fatigue;
- **permission to take notes** throughout.

Some large employers run dedicated neurodiversity hiring programmes that replace the standard interview loop with multi-day hiring events, multi-week workshops, cohort-based simulation exercises, take-home stages or paid work trials. Many also publish an accessibility or accommodation request form on their careers site. Check before you apply.

### 7.3 Interview accommodation request (template)
> "Thank you for the interview invitation for [role]. I would like to request the following accommodations to ensure I can fully demonstrate my capabilities: (1) interview questions shared in writing [24 hours] in advance, (2) display of questions in the chat during virtual interviews, (3) a [10]-minute break between consecutive interview slots, and (4) a take-home or asynchronous component in lieu of timed live exercises. [These adjustments are reasonable accommodations under the ADA.] I am happy to discuss alternatives."

### 7.4 If pressed for the diagnosis (to HR or the recruiter only)
> "I have a formally diagnosed [condition] that affects [information processing under time pressure / unstructured social situations / functional area]. The accommodations I have requested address those functional limitations."

## 8. For managers and HR: receiving a request

- **Recognise it.** A request does not need the word ADA or the phrase reasonable accommodation. "I need a change at work because of a medical condition" is enough to start the process (US).
- **Respond promptly and in writing.** Name who will coordinate, and when.
- **Engage in the interactive process.** Discuss the specific adjustments proposed; if one will not work, offer an alternative rather than a flat no.
- **Ask only for what you need.** Reasonable documentation of the need, not full medical records, and not a diagnosis you do not require.
- **Keep it confidential.** Do not tell the team why someone has an adjustment.
- **Expect low cost.** Most of these adjustments cost nothing (§1.2); written instructions, agendas and focus time are good management for everyone.
- **Check effectiveness.** Set a review date; treat a not-effective notice (§6.5) as normal, not as a complaint.
- **Interview panels:** provide questions in writing when asked, keep formats structured, and assess the skill the role needs, not performance under unfamiliar social pressure.

## 9. Limits and caveats

- **Not legal advice.** US federal coverage generally starts at 15 employees and has exceptions; state law can add more. Confirm coverage with HR or counsel before relying on it, especially if any adverse action is in view.
- **Disclosure is personal.** Documented risks (§1.3) are real; the decision and its timing are yours.
- **Evidence limits.** The accommodation evidence base is small and mostly autism-focused (§1.1); the cost data is employer self-report (§1.2); the risk data is qualitative (§1.3).
- **Jurisdiction.** Everything legal here is US-specific (§3.5).
- **Templates are starting points.** Edit until they describe your actual needs in your own words.
