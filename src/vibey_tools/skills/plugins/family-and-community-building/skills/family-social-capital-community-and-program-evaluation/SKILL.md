## §16. Social Capital

**⚠️ The resources available to people through their relationships and networks — and the
crucial distinction is what KIND:**
```
⚠️ BONDING   ties within a homogeneous group. Family, close friends, co-ethnics,
   congregation members. ⚠️ Provides SUPPORT — "getting by"
⚠️ BRIDGING  ties ACROSS groups. Different backgrounds, classes, communities.
   ⚠️ Provides OPPORTUNITY and information — "getting ahead"
⚠️ LINKING   ties across POWER differentials — to institutions, officials,
   professionals. ⚠️ Provides ACCESS and voice
```
**⚠️ The distinction is not academic.** ⚠️ **A community rich in bonding capital and poor
in bridging capital can be warm, mutually supportive, and economically stuck — and can
also be insular or hostile to outsiders.** **Bonding capital has a dark side, and
"community" is not automatically good: gangs, cartels and exclusionary associations are
high in bonding capital.**
**⚠️ The economic-mobility finding worth knowing**: **large-scale analyses of social
networks have found that CROSS-CLASS friendship — "economic connectedness" — is among the
stronger neighbourhood-level correlates of upward mobility, more so than measures of
cohesion or civic density.** ⚠️ **That is bridging capital doing work that bonding capital
does not.**
**⚠️ Putnam's decline thesis** (§24.2 → `family-reference`) — **and the honest note that it has been contested
on measurement grounds (⚠️ some argue association forms shifted rather than disappeared),
though the core time-use trend has held up.**

## §17. Networks and Belonging

**⚠️ Network structure predicts a great deal** (see a psychology reference §19):
**strength of weak ties (Granovetter — ⚠️ weak ties bridge and carry novel information,
which is why they matter for jobs), structural holes, homophily (⚠️ birds of a feather —
which means networks reproduce stratification without anyone intending it), and
Dunbar-style limits on relationship tiers.**
**⚠️ Belonging is distinct from contact.** ⚠️ **You can be surrounded by people and not
belong** (§18) — **belonging requires being known, mattering to others, and having a
recognized place.**
**⚠️ What actually builds relationships, from the research on friendship formation:**
```
⚠️ PROXIMITY      repeated unplanned encounter. The strongest single factor,
   and it's why the loss of shared physical places matters (§19)
⚠️ REPEATED UNPLANNED CONTACT  ⚠️ scheduled one-off meetings build far less
   than incidental repetition. This is why work and school produce friendships
   and adult life after them often doesn't
⚠️ SHARED VULNERABILITY / joint activity toward a common goal
⚠️ TIME            estimates run to dozens of hours for casual friendship and
   substantially more for close friendship. ⚠️ There is no shortcut
```
**⚠️ The adult friendship problem, mechanistically**: ⚠️ **adults lose the institutional
scaffolding — school, dorms, early-career workplaces — that supplied proximity and repeated
unplanned contact for free.** **Which means adult friendship requires deliberately
manufacturing what was previously automatic: standing commitments, recurring events,
membership in something that meets regularly.** ⚠️ **"Structure beats willpower" again.**

## §18. ⚠️ Loneliness and Social Isolation

**⚠️ Keep the two concepts distinct, because they have different causes and different
remedies:**
```
⚠️ SOCIAL ISOLATION  OBJECTIVE — few relationships, infrequent contact
⚠️ LONELINESS        SUBJECTIVE — the GAP between the connection you have
   and the connection you want. ⚠️ You can be isolated and content, or
   surrounded by people and profoundly lonely
```
**⚠️ The health evidence** — **see §24.1 → `family-reference` for the current state and the important caveat**:
**meta-analytic work associates both with increased mortality risk, and with elevated
cardiovascular, dementia and depression risk.** ⚠️ **The mechanisms proposed include chronic
stress activation, inflammation, sleep disruption, health behaviours, and reduced
practical support.**
**⚠️ Who is most affected**: **young adults report loneliness at rates comparable to or
higher than older adults** (⚠️ **which inverts the stereotype and is now well replicated
across countries**), **along with people with disabilities, caregivers, recent movers, and
those in poor health** — ⚠️ **and reverse causation runs strongly here.**
**⚠️ The intervention evidence is weaker than the problem framing suggests**, **and §24.1 → `family-reference`
says so directly.** **What has the better support: ⚠️ addressing maladaptive social
cognition (loneliness biases attention toward social threat, which is self-perpetuating);
group activities with a shared purpose rather than socializing-for-its-own-sake; and
structural changes that increase proximity and repeated contact** (§17, §19).
**⚠️ If this section describes you**: **loneliness is a signal, not a character defect —
it functions like hunger, indicating an unmet need.** ⚠️ **And the evidence points away
from "try harder socially" and toward joining something that meets REGULARLY around a
shared activity, because that supplies the repetition §17 says is required.** **If it's
persistent and accompanied by low mood, that's worth raising with a doctor or counsellor.**

## §19. Third Places

**⚠️ Oldenburg's concept: the "third place" is neither home (first) nor work (second) —
it's the informal public gathering place.** **Pubs, cafés, barbershops, libraries, parks,
places of worship, community centres, bowling alleys.**
**⚠️ The defining characteristics, and they explain why substitutes often fail:**
```
⚠️ Neutral ground — nobody is host, nobody obliged
⚠️ LEVELING — status outside doesn't determine status inside
⚠️ Conversation is the main activity
⚠️ ACCESSIBLE and accommodating — low cost, easy to drop in
⚠️ REGULARS who set the tone
⚠️ Low profile, playful mood, "a home away from home"
```
**⚠️ Why they matter mechanistically** (§17): **third places manufacture PROXIMITY and
REPEATED UNPLANNED CONTACT**, ⚠️ **which is precisely the mechanism friendship formation
requires and which adult life otherwise removes.** **They also generate bridging capital
(§16) because they mix people who wouldn't otherwise meet.**
**⚠️ The decline is structural, not attitudinal**: **car-dependent land use, zoning that
separates residential from commercial, rising commercial rents, the shift of retail
online, and the substitution of home entertainment.** ⚠️ **Which means the remedy is also
structural — this is a land use, transport and licensing question as much as a cultural
one.**
**⚠️ Libraries deserve specific mention** — ⚠️ **among the last genuinely free, non-commercial,
non-sectarian indoor public spaces in most communities**, **and they function as
de facto social infrastructure well beyond books.**
**⚠️ "Social infrastructure" (Klinenberg) is the same idea in policy language**, **and his
Chicago heat-wave analysis is the standard demonstration: neighbourhoods with comparable
demographics had very different death rates, and the difference tracked the physical
infrastructure that sustained everyday contact.**

## §20. Congregations and Voluntary Associations

**⚠️ Religious congregations remain the single largest category of voluntary association in
the US, and their civic role is well documented and often understated in secular
discussions of community.**
**⚠️ What the research supports:**
- **⚠️ Attendance — more than belief — predicts volunteering, charitable giving (⚠️ including
  to secular causes), civic participation and social support.** **The mechanism appears to
  be largely the network and the recurring gathering** (§17's repetition point) — **which
  is itself a finding worth noting.**
- ⚠️ **Congregations supply bonding capital reliably and bridging capital variably** —
  **some are highly homogeneous, some are among the few genuinely cross-class institutions
  left in a community.**
- **⚠️ They are disproportionately important civic infrastructure for lower-income and
  minority communities**, **and historically central to social movements.**
- ⚠️ **Selection is a live confound throughout** (§1 → `family-reading-claims-attachment-and-couple-relationships`): **joiners differ from non-joiners.**
  **Longitudinal work suggests some genuine effect, and the magnitude is contested.**

**⚠️ The trend, and its civic consequence**: **the Christian share of US adults has fallen
from roughly 78% two decades ago to around 62–65%, with the religiously unaffiliated
rising to roughly a quarter to 30%** (§24.2 → `family-reference`). ⚠️ **The relevant point for THIS document is
not theological — it's that congregations performed civic and social functions that no
comparable institution has replaced at similar scale.** **Whatever one's view of the
underlying religious question, that's an infrastructure gap.**
**⚠️ Other voluntary associations**: **unions, service clubs, fraternal organizations,
sports leagues, mutual aid, parent groups.** ⚠️ **The class divide here is significant and
growing** (§24.2 → `family-reference`).

## §21. Community Organizing

```
⚠️ ALINSKY / IAF (broad-based)  ⚠️ builds POWER through institutional
   membership — congregations, unions, schools. One-to-one relational
   meetings are the core method. Self-interest properly understood; agitate;
   pick winnable fights; ⚠️ "no permanent allies, no permanent enemies"
⚠️ ABCD (Asset-Based Community Development)  ⚠️ start from what a community
   HAS — skills, associations, institutions — rather than from a needs
   assessment. ⚠️ Direct reaction against deficit framing that positions
   residents as clients rather than producers
FAITH-BASED ORGANIZING  ⚠️ uses congregations as the membership base (§20)
POPULAR EDUCATION (Freire)  ⚠️ conscientization; people analyze their own
   conditions rather than receiving analysis
MUTUAL AID  ⚠️ horizontal, solidarity rather than charity, often rapid
COLLECTIVE IMPACT  ⚠️ cross-sector alignment with a backbone organization.
   Popular with funders; ⚠️ evidence for it is notably mixed
```
**⚠️ The relational meeting is the underrated technique.** ⚠️ **One-to-one conversations,
30 minutes, to learn what someone actually cares about and what they'd act on — not to
recruit them to your agenda.** **Organizers who skip this and go straight to mobilization
build brittle campaigns.**
**⚠️ Organizing vs mobilizing vs advocacy is a distinction that matters**: ⚠️ **mobilizing
turns out existing supporters; organizing DEVELOPS new leaders and builds durable capacity;
advocacy speaks on behalf of others.** **They are different activities with different time
horizons, and conflating them is why many campaigns evaporate after one victory.**
**⚠️ The recurring failure mode**: ⚠️ **outside professionals doing FOR rather than WITH,
producing programs that end when the grant does.** **The ABCD critique of deficit framing
is aimed precisely at this, and it's a critique with real force.**

## §22. Collective Efficacy

**⚠️ Sampson's construct — social cohesion PLUS shared willingness to intervene for the
common good — is one of the better-supported findings in community research.**
⚠️ **It predicts lower neighbourhood violence and better outcomes even controlling for
poverty and demographic composition.**
**⚠️ The key insight is that cohesion alone is insufficient**: ⚠️ **you need both trust AND
the expectation that neighbours will act** — **that someone would say something if children
were skipping school or a fight broke out.** **Warm but passive neighbourhoods don't get
the effect.**
**⚠️ What builds it**: **residential stability (⚠️ high turnover undermines it
mechanically), physical spaces that support encounter (§19), local organizations, and
homeownership or long tenancies.** ⚠️ **Which means displacement and housing instability
have community-level costs beyond the individual household.**
**⚠️ Broken windows is the adjacent theory and is much weaker** (see a psychology reference
§21) — ⚠️ **the collective efficacy research suggests informal social control by residents,
not disorder policing, is what does the work.**

## §23. ⚠️ Does the Program Actually Work?

**⚠️ Community and family programs are dense with plausible interventions that don't
survive evaluation, and the field has learned some hard lessons.**
```
⚠️ THE CAUTIONARY CASES worth knowing
  Scared Straight — ⚠️ randomized evaluations found it INCREASED offending.
    A program that felt obviously right and made things worse
  D.A.R.E. (original) — ⚠️ repeatedly evaluated, no effect on drug use
  ⚠️ Some peer-group interventions for at-risk youth show DEVIANCY TRAINING:
    grouping troubled youth can worsen outcomes through peer reinforcement
```
**⚠️ The generalizable lesson: plausibility is not evidence, and untested programs can
cause harm rather than merely wasting money.**
**⚠️ What good evaluation requires:**
```
⚠️ A THEORY OF CHANGE stated before you start — what changes what, and how
⚠️ A COMPARISON GROUP. ⚠️ Pre-post with no control is nearly uninterpretable
   because of regression to the mean and maturation
⚠️ Distinguish OUTPUTS (people served) from OUTCOMES (lives changed)
⚠️ Attend to SELECTION — who enrolls, and who DROPS OUT (differential
   attrition destroys more evaluations than any other single problem)
⚠️ Measure implementation fidelity — did the program actually happen?
⚠️ Report null results. ⚠️ The field's file-drawer problem is severe
```
**⚠️ Realistic expectations**: ⚠️ **effect sizes for community interventions are typically
small, and scaled-up versions almost always underperform the original trial** — **the
"voltage drop" between efficacy and effectiveness is the norm, not an anomaly.**
**⚠️ And the participatory point, which is both ethical and practical**: **programs designed
with the community they serve have better take-up and fewer of the failure modes in §21.**
