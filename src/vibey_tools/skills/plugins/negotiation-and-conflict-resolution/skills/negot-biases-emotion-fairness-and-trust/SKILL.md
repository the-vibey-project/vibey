> **The three ideas that organize this document:**
> 1. **⚠️ YOUR LEVERAGE IS YOUR ALTERNATIVE, NOT YOUR ARGUMENT** (§3 → `negot-framings-batna-zopa-interests-and-preparation`). **What you can do if
>    this deal fails determines what you can accept. Almost every improvement in negotiating
>    position comes from improving or accurately assessing that alternative, not from
>    technique at the table.**
> 2. **⚠️ POSITIONS ARE WHAT PEOPLE SAY THEY WANT; INTERESTS ARE WHY** (§4 → `negot-framings-batna-zopa-interests-and-preparation`). **Positions
>    conflict far more often than interests do. Nearly all value creation comes from finding
>    where underlying interests differ, which is invisible until someone asks.**
> 3. **⚠️ MOST NEGOTIATIONS ARE REPEATED, AND MOST PEOPLE FORGET THAT** (§14). **Tactics
>    that win a single exchange routinely destroy the relationship that produces future
>    ones. The counterparty's willingness to deal with you again is usually worth more than
>    the marginal concession.**

## §11. ⚠️ Biases

```
⚠️ ⚠️ FIXED-PIE BIAS (§2)  ⚠️ assuming conflict where interests
   are compatible. ⚠️ The most costly one
⚠️ ⚠️ REACTIVE DEVALUATION  ⚠️ the SAME proposal is valued LOWER
   when it comes from the other side. ⚠️ Demonstrated
   experimentally, and it explains why proposals rejected at
   the table are accepted when a mediator floats them (§22)
⚠️ ANCHORING (§6) · ⚠️ and it affects the person who SET the
   anchor too
⚠️ ⚠️ OVERCONFIDENCE  ⚠️ in one's own case, in the likely
   outcome of litigation (§23), and in the accuracy of one's
   read of the other side
⚠️ ⚠️ SELF-SERVING FAIRNESS BIAS  ⚠️ people genuinely believe
   the fair outcome is the one that favours them. ⚠️ Not
   cynicism — an honest perceptual distortion, and it is why
   both sides in a dispute can be sincere
⚠️ LOSS AVERSION and FRAMING  ⚠️ the same terms framed as a
   loss from a reference point are resisted far more than when
   framed as a smaller gain — ⚠️ so the reference point is
   itself worth negotiating
⚠️ ⚠️ ESCALATION OF COMMITMENT  ⚠️ sunk costs driving continued
   investment in a failing negotiation or conflict (§21)
⚠️ ⚠️ AND THE HONEST CAVEAT  ⚠️ some negotiation and
   priming-adjacent findings have replicated poorly in the
   broader psychology literature. ⚠️ The effects above are
   among the better-supported, and specific effect SIZES
   should be held loosely (§28.2)
```

## §12. Emotion

**⚠️ Emotion is information, not noise** — ⚠️ **it signals what matters, and suppressing it
entirely removes data both sides need.**
**⚠️ Anger**: ⚠️ **the research finds expressed anger can extract concessions in one-shot
distributive settings — ⚠️ AND it damages relationships, increases impasse and retaliation,
and reduces the other side's willingness to deal again.** ⚠️ **It is a tactic that borrows
from the future** (§14).
**⚠️ Anxiety** is the underrated one: ⚠️ **anxious negotiators make lower first offers,
concede faster and exit earlier — ⚠️ which is why preparation and practice pay in confidence
as much as content.**
**⚠️ Emotional regulation techniques**: ⚠️ **naming the emotion, taking breaks, separating
the person from the problem, and going to the balcony.**
**⚠️ Acknowledging the other side's emotion** costs nothing and does real work — ⚠️ **and
note that acknowledgement is not agreement, which is the distinction that makes it safe.**

## §13. ⚠️ Fairness and Standards

**⚠️ People will reject profitable deals they consider unfair.** ⚠️ **Ultimatum game results
are robust across cultures: low offers are frequently rejected even at a cost to the
rejector — which means a purely economic model of the counterparty is wrong.**
**⚠️ Distributive fairness has competing norms**, ⚠️ **and this is why fairness arguments
persist: EQUALITY (equal shares), EQUITY (proportional to contribution), and NEED. ⚠️ Each
side sincerely favours the norm that benefits it** (§11).
**⚠️ PROCEDURAL fairness matters independently of outcome** — ⚠️ **people accept unfavourable
results markedly better when they had voice, were treated with respect, and understood the
process.** ⚠️ **This is one of the most robust findings in the field and it is nearly free
to apply.**
**⚠️ Objective standards** — ⚠️ **market rates, precedent, independent valuation, published
formulas — convert a contest of wills into a discussion of what applies, and give both sides
a face-saving rationale.**
**⚠️ Reciprocity** is the underlying engine of §7 → `negot-anchoring-concessions-value-creation-and-closing`, ⚠️ **and it is strong enough that
unreciprocated generosity reads as weakness rather than as goodwill.**

## §14. Trust and Reputation

**⚠️ §1 → `negot-framings-batna-zopa-interests-and-preparation`'s third organizing idea.** ⚠️ **Most negotiations are embedded in continuing
relationships or reputational networks, and single-shot logic misapplied to a repeated game
is the standard failure.**
**⚠️ Two kinds of trust** worth distinguishing: ⚠️ **trust in COMPETENCE (they can deliver)
and trust in INTEGRITY (they will).** ⚠️ **They fail differently and are repaired
differently.**
**⚠️ Trust is asymmetric** — ⚠️ **slow to build, fast to destroy, and hard to rebuild — which
is the practical argument against short-term tactics that succeed.**
**⚠️ Repair** requires ⚠️ **acknowledgement, explanation, and — the part usually missing —
a structural change that makes recurrence less likely; ⚠️ apology without change reads as
management.**
**⚠️ Verification is compatible with trust.** ⚠️ **Contracts, milestones and audit rights are
not accusations; treating verification as an insult is itself a tactic** (§15 → `negot-hard-tactics-multiparty-culture-power-and-remote`).
