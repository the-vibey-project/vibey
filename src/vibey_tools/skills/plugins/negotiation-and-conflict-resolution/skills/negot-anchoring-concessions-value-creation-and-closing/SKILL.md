## §6. ⚠️ First Offers and Anchoring

**⚠️ The anchoring effect**: ⚠️ **an initial number pulls subsequent judgements toward it,
even when arbitrary — Tversky and Kahneman's finding, applied to negotiation as the claim
that whoever offers first sets the frame.**
**⚠️ The standard advice** is to make an ambitious first offer, ⚠️ **and it is right more
often than not — but the qualifications are substantial and §28.2 → `negot-reference` has the current
evidence.**
> **⚠️ GOTCHA — "always make the first offer" is the most confidently repeated piece of
> negotiation advice and it is conditional.** ⚠️ **It holds best in simple, single-issue,
> distributive negotiations where you have good information about value.** **⚠️ It weakens
> as complexity rises, and it can REVERSE when you are poorly informed about what the item
> is worth — because moving first reveals your ignorance and forgoes the information their
> offer would have given you** (§28.2 → `negot-reference`).

**⚠️ Precision matters as well as magnitude**: ⚠️ **precise numbers signal informedness and
tend to draw smaller counter-adjustments — with the documented caveat of a "too-much-
precision" effect where excessive precision can reduce perceived competence.**
**⚠️ Justify the offer.** ⚠️ **An anchor with a rationale is far more effective than a number
alone, and an extreme offer with no rationale reads as bad faith and raises impasse risk.**
**⚠️ If they anchor first**: ⚠️ **do not counter immediately from their number. Name the
anchor, re-frame around standards or interests, and introduce your own reference point.**

## §7. Concessions

**⚠️ The pattern signals more than the amounts.** ⚠️ **Decreasing concession sizes signal
approach to a limit; constant-sized concessions invite continued pushing; a large late
concession suggests there was more available all along.**
**⚠️ Reciprocity is the governing norm** (§13 → `negot-biases-emotion-fairness-and-trust`): ⚠️ **unreciprocated concessions train the
other side to expect more, and the correct response to a non-reciprocal counterpart is to
stop conceding, not to concede faster.**
**⚠️ Label your concessions** — ⚠️ **make clear that it is a concession, that it is costly,
and what you expect in return; ⚠️ an unlabelled concession is often not registered as one.**
**⚠️ Conditional language is the workhorse**: ⚠️ **"if you can do X, then I could consider Y"
— which explores without committing.**
**⚠️ Never concede in response to pressure alone**, ⚠️ **only in response to a reason or a
reciprocal move; otherwise pressure is being rewarded and will continue** (§15 → `negot-hard-tactics-multiparty-culture-power-and-remote`).

## §8. Creating Value

**⚠️ Trades across differently-valued issues** are the core mechanism (§2 → `negot-framings-batna-zopa-interests-and-preparation`) — ⚠️ **log-rolling:
each side concedes on what it values less for what it values more.**
**⚠️ Differences are the raw material, and this is the counterintuitive part**: ⚠️ **value
comes from differences in PRIORITIES, in FORECASTS about the future, in RISK TOLERANCE, and
in TIME PREFERENCE.** ⚠️ **Two parties who agree about everything have less to trade.**
**⚠️ CONTINGENT CONTRACTS** exploit differing forecasts: ⚠️ **if you believe sales will hit
the target and I do not, we can bet on it rather than argue — earnouts, performance clauses
and milestone payments are all this.** ⚠️ **They also protect against bluffing, because
someone confident in a claim should accept a contingency on it.**
**⚠️ MESOs (multiple equivalent simultaneous offers)** — ⚠️ **present two or three packages
of equal value to you; their preference reveals their priorities without your having to ask,
and offering choices reads as flexible rather than as concession.**
**⚠️ Post-settlement settlement**: ⚠️ **once a deal is agreed, ask whether both would accept
a jointly better alternative, with the current deal as the fallback.**

## §9. ⚠️ The Negotiator's Dilemma

**⚠️ The structural tension**: ⚠️ **creating value requires sharing information about
priorities; claiming value is helped by concealing it.** ⚠️ **Open with someone
exploitative and you lose; close up with someone cooperative and you both lose.**
**⚠️ The practical resolution** is ⚠️ **RECIPROCAL, INCREMENTAL disclosure — share
information about PRIORITIES and RANKINGS rather than about reservation values, share in
small steps, and match their behaviour.**
> **⚠️ GOTCHA — sharing priorities is safe in a way that sharing your walk-away point is
> not.** ⚠️ **"Delivery timing matters more to me than price" enables trades.** **⚠️ "I could
> go as low as X" hands over the entire distributive advantage and cannot be taken back.**

**⚠️ Asking questions is undervalued and low-risk**: ⚠️ **the party asking questions learns
more and, contrary to intuition, is often perceived more favourably.**
**⚠️ Silence after a proposal** is a real technique and works because ⚠️ **people fill
silence, often with concessions or information.**

## §10. Closing and Commitment

**⚠️ Commitment devices** — ⚠️ **public statements, mandate limits, published policies —
work by making it credible that you CANNOT concede** (see a military reference §8 on the
same logic in deterrence). ⚠️ **The risk is that a commitment you cannot back away from
produces impasse.**
**⚠️ Deadlines** concentrate concessions: ⚠️ **a large share of movement in many negotiations
occurs near the deadline, which means the party with more time pressure is disadvantaged —
and time pressure is often asymmetric and concealed.**
**⚠️ Building a golden bridge** (§8's face point): ⚠️ **make it easy for them to say yes by
giving them a way to explain the outcome to their own side.**
**⚠️ Document precisely and promptly.** ⚠️ **Ambiguity that felt like flexibility at the
table becomes a dispute later — and the party that drafts has real influence over what
"agreed" means.**
**⚠️ Implementation is part of the negotiation**: ⚠️ **an agreement the other side cannot
actually perform is worse than no agreement, and checking feasibility is not a lack of
trust.**
