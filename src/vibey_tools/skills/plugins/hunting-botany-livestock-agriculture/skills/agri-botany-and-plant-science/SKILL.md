## §1. Plant Structure

```
ROOTS    ⚠️ anchorage, water and nutrient uptake, storage. Root HAIRS do
         most of the absorbing; ⚠️ mycorrhizal fungi extend reach enormously (§10)
STEMS    support and transport. ⚠️ XYLEM carries water UP (dead cells, driven
         by transpiration); PHLOEM carries sugars BOTH WAYS (living, pressure-driven)
LEAVES   ⚠️ the photosynthetic organ. STOMATA regulate the fundamental
         tradeoff: open to admit CO₂, and lose water (§3)
FLOWERS/FRUIT/SEED   reproduction (§5)
MERISTEMS ⚠️ where growth happens. Apical (tips) vs lateral (girth) vs
         intercalary (⚠️ at the BASE in grasses — which is exactly why grass
         survives grazing and mowing and most other plants don't). §22
```
**⚠️ Apical dominance** — **the growing tip suppresses lateral buds via auxin** — **is why
pruning or browsing a terminal bud produces bushier growth.** **This single mechanism
underlies pruning, pinching, hedging and much browse response in wildlife habitat.**

## §2. Photosynthesis and the Three Pathways

```
⚠️ 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂   (the summary, not the mechanism)
Light reactions (thylakoid) → ATP + NADPH + O₂
Calvin cycle (stroma) → fixes CO₂ using RuBisCO
```
**⚠️ RuBisCO is the most abundant protein on Earth and it is bad at its job** — **it also
binds oxygen, causing photorespiration, which wastes energy.** ⚠️ **Photorespiration gets
worse as temperature rises and as stomata close in heat.** **Two workarounds evolved:**
```
C3   ⚠️ most plants — wheat, rice, soybeans, most trees, cool-season grasses.
     Efficient in cool, moist, moderate light. Suffers in heat
C4   ⚠️ maize, sorghum, sugarcane, warm-season grasses (bermudagrass,
     switchgrass). Concentrates CO₂ around RuBisCO. ⚠️ Better in HEAT and
     HIGH LIGHT and more water-efficient; costs energy, so worse when cool
CAM  ⚠️ cacti, agave, pineapple. Stomata open at NIGHT, storing CO₂ as acid.
     Extreme water efficiency, slow growth
```
**⚠️ Practical consequence that matters for pasture and food plots**: **cool-season (C3)
and warm-season (C4) forages peak at different times of year.** ⚠️ **A pasture of only one
type has a production gap** — **the "summer slump" in cool-season systems is a C3
physiology fact, not a management failure** — **and mixed swards exist to cover it** (§22 → `agri-livestock-nutrition-health-and-welfare`).

## §3. Water Relations

**⚠️ Water moves down a potential gradient, and the numbers are extreme**: **soil (~−0.03
MPa at field capacity) → root → xylem → leaf → atmosphere (⚠️ often −50 to −100 MPa).**
**The cohesion-tension mechanism pulls a continuous water column up the plant under
tension.**
**⚠️ The central tradeoff**: **stomata must open for CO₂ and that loses water.**
**Transpiration ratio — hundreds of grams of water per gram of dry matter — means ⚠️ the
overwhelming majority of water a crop uses is spent buying CO₂, not building tissue.**
**⚠️ Wilting point vs field capacity**: **plant-available water is the difference between
them, and it's a soil property (§10 → `agri-soil-fertility-and-crop-production`), which is why sandy soils need more frequent
irrigation regardless of total rainfall.**
**⚠️ Water stress reduces yield most at critical growth stages** — **flowering and grain
fill for most crops** — **so irrigation timing beats irrigation volume.**

## §4. Plant Nutrition

```
MACRO (primary)     ⚠️ N, P, K — the numbers on a fertilizer bag, in that order
MACRO (secondary)   Ca, Mg, S
MICRO               Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B, Mo, Cl, Ni
FROM AIR/WATER      C, H, O — ⚠️ the BULK of plant dry matter comes from CO₂,
                    not from soil. Fertilizer is a minority of the mass
```
**⚠️ Liebig's Law of the Minimum**: **growth is limited by the scarcest resource** —
⚠️ **so adding more of an abundant nutrient does nothing.** **The corollary matters: a
soil test tells you which barrel stave is short, and applying N to a P-limited field wastes
money and pollutes.**
**⚠️ Mobility determines where deficiency shows**: **mobile nutrients (N, P, K, Mg) are
remobilized from old tissue, so deficiency appears in OLD leaves first; immobile ones (Ca,
B, Fe, Mn) show in NEW growth first.** ⚠️ **This is a genuinely useful diagnostic — look at
which leaves are affected before guessing.**
**⚠️ pH governs availability more than total content.** **Most nutrients are most available
around pH 6–7; ⚠️ phosphorus is locked by aluminium and iron in acid soils and by calcium
in alkaline ones.** **A soil can be rich in P and the plant can still be starving.**

## §5. Reproduction and Pollination

**Alternation of generations; ⚠️ complete vs incomplete flowers; monoecious vs dioecious
(⚠️ dioecious species need both sexes present — the classic reason a holly or kiwi never
fruits).**
**Pollination**: **wind (grasses, conifers, corn — copious light pollen), insect, bird,
bat, self.** ⚠️ **Self-incompatibility systems exist in many species, which is why some
fruit trees need a compatible pollinizer variety and not just another tree.**
**Seed dormancy and its breaking**: ⚠️ **stratification (cold period), scarification
(abrasion of hard coat), light requirement, and chemical inhibitors.** **Dormancy is an
adaptation, and "the seed didn't germinate" is often "you skipped a required signal."**
**Vegetative propagation** — **cuttings, layering, grafting, tubers** — ⚠️ **produces
clones, which means uniformity and shared vulnerability** (§8).

## §6. Ecology and Succession

**⚠️ Succession is the organizing concept for both habitat management and weed pressure.**
**Primary (bare rock/sand) vs secondary (after disturbance).** ⚠️ **Early successional
stages are dominated by fast-growing, high-seed, short-lived species — which is exactly
what "weeds" are, and why disturbance creates weed pressure by definition** (§9).
**⚠️ For wildlife this inverts the intuition**: ⚠️ **early successional habitat is what most
game species need** — **deer browse, quail and turkey brood cover, rabbit** — **and mature
closed-canopy forest is comparatively poor habitat for them.** **Which is why habitat
management involves deliberate disturbance: prescribed fire, timber harvest, disking** (§24 → `agri-wildlife-management-and-hunting`).
**Edge effect** (⚠️ **more species at boundaries, and it favours generalists and
predators**), **carrying capacity, and competition.**

## §7. ⚠️ Identification — and a Hard Limit

> **⚠️ STOP. Never eat a plant or fungus identified from any text description, including
> this one. This is not liability boilerplate.**
> ⚠️ **The lethal lookalikes are genuinely difficult**: **water hemlock and poison hemlock
> resemble edible members of the carrot family, and water hemlock is among the most toxic
> plants in North America.** **Death cap and destroying angel mushrooms resemble edible
> species and cause liver failure with a symptom-free window that makes people think
> they're fine.** ⚠️ **Pokeweed, nightshades and many others are commonly misjudged.**
> **⚠️ What is actually required**: **multiple keyed features verified against a regional
> dichotomous key, cross-checked with a second independent source, ideally confirmed in
> person by someone experienced with that species in your region.** **Photo-ID apps are
> useful for narrowing and are NOT sufficient for consumption decisions.**

**⚠️ Taxonomy basics worth knowing**: **binomial nomenclature (Genus species), the
hierarchy, and ⚠️ why common names are unreliable — "cedar" means different genera in
different regions, and one plant may have a dozen local names.** **Use Latin names when
precision matters.**
**Dichotomous keys, herbarium specimens, and ⚠️ the fact that vegetative features are far
less reliable than floral ones** — **which is why "I identified it from the leaves" is
weak evidence.**

## §8. Plant Pathology

**⚠️ The disease triangle: susceptible host + virulent pathogen + favourable environment.**
⚠️ **Remove any one and there is no disease** — **and environment is usually the one you
can most cheaply influence** (spacing, airflow, irrigation timing, avoiding wet foliage).
```
FUNGAL     ⚠️ the majority. Rusts, smuts, mildews, blights, wilts
BACTERIAL  ⚠️ fire blight, bacterial spot; hard to treat, manage culturally
VIRAL      ⚠️ usually vector-borne (aphids, thrips); no cure — remove plants
NEMATODES · ABIOTIC (⚠️ nutrient, herbicide drift, cold, compaction — and these
   are routinely misdiagnosed as disease, and treated with fungicide uselessly)
```
**⚠️ Monoculture and clonal uniformity amplify epidemics** — **the Irish potato famine
(one clone, *Phytophthora infestans*), southern corn leaf blight in 1970 (a shared
cytoplasmic male-sterility source), and the banana industry's repeated Panama disease
crises are all the same structural lesson.** ⚠️ **Genetic uniformity is an efficiency gain
purchased with correlated risk.**

## §9. Weeds

**⚠️ A weed is a plant where you don't want it — an economic category, not a botanical
one.** **Annual vs perennial matters enormously for control: ⚠️ perennials with rhizomes
regenerate from fragments, so tillage propagates them.**
**Control**: **cultural (rotation, competition, cover crops, timing), mechanical, chemical,
biological.**
**⚠️ Herbicide resistance is evolution operating on a very short timescale, and it is now a
first-order agronomic problem.** ⚠️ **Repeated use of a single mode of action selects
resistant biotypes** — **glyphosate-resistant Palmer amaranth and waterhemp being the
best-known** — **and the mitigation is rotating and mixing MODES OF ACTION (herbicide
group numbers), not brand names.** **Many products with different names share a mode of
action.**
