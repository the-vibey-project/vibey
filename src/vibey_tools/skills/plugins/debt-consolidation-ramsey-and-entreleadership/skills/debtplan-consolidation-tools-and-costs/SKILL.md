## Part 1. Debt consolidation

## Five different tools share one name, and only two reduce what you owe

The Federal Trade Commission describes a debt consolidation loan as combining several debts into one loan with one monthly payment, notes that some versions use your home as collateral, and warns that lenders may charge "points" equal to 1% of the amount borrowed each ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-get-out-debt)). A cleaner taxonomy, assembled from the FTC, CFPB, and credit-bureau sources in the research, separates five approaches. Consolidation replaces several debts with one new credit product and repays everything in full. A debt management plan (DMP) keeps the original debts but has a nonprofit negotiate lower rates and fees, again repaying principal in full. Settlement pays less than owed, usually after payments stop. Bankruptcy discharges eligible debts through a court. Self-directed hardship negotiation with creditors sits alongside these. Only settlement and bankruptcy reduce principal; consolidation and DMPs reduce interest cost and simplify payments. (The research did not retrieve an authoritative definition separating "refinancing" from "consolidation"; the usual distinction is that refinancing replaces one loan and consolidation merges several.)

| Approach | Repays full principal? | Typical cost | Credit effect (sourced) | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Balance transfer card | Yes | 3% to 5% transfer fee | Hard inquiry; utilization can fall | Rate reverts to roughly 15% to 29% after 15 to 21 months |
| Personal loan | Yes | About 6% to 36% APR; online origination fees up to 12% | Moves revolving debt to installment | Re-running up cleared cards |
| Home equity, HELOC, cash-out refi | Yes | About 8.1% to 8.7% (home equity); about 6.75% to 7.375% plus points (cash-out refi) | Scores rose short-term in CFPB study | Foreclosure; refinancing the whole mortgage |
| 401(k) loan | Yes | Lost investment growth; tax if unpaid | None directly | Taxable "deemed distribution" on job loss |
| Nonprofit DMP | Yes | SC fee ceilings of $50 setup, up to $70 per month | Notation not scored negative, but card closures can raise utilization | Cannot cover secured debts; plan must be affordable |
| Settlement company | No (pays less) | 15% to 25% of enrolled debt | NFCC: drops "exceed 100 points in most cases" | Lawsuits, taxable forgiven debt |
| Chapter 7 / Chapter 13 | Chapter 7 discharges; Chapter 13 repays over 3 to 5 years | Court fees plus attorney | Report for up to 10 years (Chapter 7) | Non-exempt assets; some debts survive |

Sources for the table are developed in the sections below.

## October 2026 conditions make borrowing to consolidate more expensive than a year ago

The Federal Open Market Committee raised its target range 25 basis points to **3.75% to 4.00% on September 16, 2026**, by a 12-0 vote, saying inflation "remains elevated" ([Federal Reserve press release](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20260916a.htm); [Federal Reserve open market page](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/openmarket.htm)). That is a hike, not the cut many households expected after the 2024 to 2025 easing, and it was confirmed on two Federal Reserve pages. Variable-rate products (credit cards, HELOCs) follow policy rates, and new loan and balance-transfer pricing tends to follow as well. The Fed's G.19 release shows the average credit card rate was **20.94% on all accounts and 22.15% on accounts assessed interest in the second quarter of 2026**, while the commercial bank 24-month personal loan rate was 11.86% ([Federal Reserve G.19](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g19/20260908/g19.pdf)). Revolving credit stood at $1,354.4 billion, growing at a 4.9% annualized pace.

The New York Fed reports total household debt of **$18.771 trillion in Q2 2026**, with credit card balances of $1.263 trillion, HELOC balances of $459 billion, and student loans of $1.651 trillion. About 4.7% of debt was in some stage of delinquency, and the flow into serious delinquency (90 or more days) was 6.97% for credit cards, 3.00% for auto, 1.52% for mortgages (up from 1.29% a year earlier), and 1.15% for HELOCs ([NY Fed, August 11, 2026](https://www.newyorkfed.org/newsevents/news/research/2026/20260811)). The student loan figure of 7.83% is distorted by the re-reporting of defaulted loans. Mortgage rates rose too: Freddie Mac's 30-year fixed average was **7.28% the week of October 1, 2026, versus 6.34% a year earlier** ([Freddie Mac](https://www.freddiemac.com/pmms)). The research did not retrieve inflation, unemployment, or forecast data, so the broader macro backdrop is not characterized here.

## Balance transfer cards: cheap if you pay off inside the window, a rate reset if you do not

NerdWallet's October 2026 roundup (updated October 1) lists top balance-transfer cards offering 0% for **15 to 21 months**, transfer fees of **3% to 5%**, $0 annual fees, and post-intro variable APRs from roughly 15% to 29%. Examples include the Citi Diamond Preferred (21 months, 3% fee in the first four months then 5%, 16.74% to 27.49% regular APR), BankAmericard (21 billing cycles, 5%, 15.24% to 26.24%), Wells Fargo Reflect (21 months, 5%, up to 28.49%), and Chase Freedom Unlimited (15 months, 3% or $5 in the first 60 days then 5%) ([NerdWallet](https://www.nerdwallet.com/best/credit-cards/balance-transfer)). The page says "good to excellent" credit, roughly 690 or better, is generally needed, and reports that approved applicants in a 2024 sample averaged 727. You generally cannot transfer between cards from the same issuer. This is a marketing roundup subject to affiliate relationships; offers change and should be verified with the issuer.

A worked example (self-computed from the NerdWallet fee range and the Fed's 22.15% rate): a 5% fee on a $10,000 transfer is $500 up front. Against roughly 22% card interest, which costs about $183 per month on $10,000, the fee is recovered in about three months if the balance is genuinely paid down during the 0% window. The corollary is the trap: any balance left when the promotion ends reprices to a variable rate similar to what you were escaping. The research found no verified data on credit-limit caps, whether new purchases accrue interest, or balance-transfer re-accumulation rates. The closest evidence is a Federal Reserve presentation (slides, not a refereed paper) finding that borrowers who kept making purchases on a balance-transfer card faced higher delinquency and default risk ([Seok and Wang, Fed Board](https://gflec.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/Balance-Transfer_GW_Final.pdf)).

## Personal loans: the term matters as much as the rate

Bankrate's data dated October 7, 2026 show a **12.54% average rate** for a 700-FICO borrower on a $5,000, three-year loan, with rates from just under 6% (740+) to 35.99% for weak credit; online lenders may deduct origination fees up to 12% from proceeds, credit unions typically charge no fees, and federal credit unions cap APRs at 18% ([Bankrate](https://www.bankrate.com/loans/personal-loans/interest-rate-statistics)). (The page's title says June 2026 despite October data, so treat the figures as 2026 rather than precisely October.) On a $15,000, four-year loan, Bankrate illustrates a 6% rate at $352 per month and $1,909 interest, 12.5% at $398 and $4,138, and 35% at $584 and $13,059. Because fees are deducted up front, compare APR, which includes them, rather than the stated rate.

The following table is **self-computed** (standard fixed-payment amortization, $30,000 principal, no fees, no new borrowing) and is not drawn from a source:

| APR | Term | Monthly payment | Total interest |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8% | 36 months | $940 | $3,843 |
| 8% | 60 months | $608 | $6,498 |
| 12% | 36 months | $996 | $5,871 |
| 12% | 60 months | $667 | $10,040 |
| 12% | 84 months | $530 | $14,485 |
| 18% | 60 months | $762 | $15,708 |
| 23% | 36 months | $1,161 | $11,806 |
| 23% | 60 months | $846 | $20,743 |
| 30% | 60 months | $971 | $28,236 |

Read against cards at 23%, a 12% five-year loan saves roughly $10,700 in interest, but an 84-month version of the same loan lowers the payment 37% below the card-equivalent while costing $4,445 more than the 60-month version. A 5% origination fee on $30,000 would be $1,500, meaning you would need to borrow about $31,579 to net $30,000. The minimum-payment trap is also arithmetic: at 23%, monthly interest on $30,000 is about $575, so a $500 payment never reduces the balance. A loan priced above your card APR (a 30% installment offer against 23% cards) is a worse deal even if the payment is lower. Academic work supports the term-length point: on LendingClub, borrowers who chose 60-month loans carried APRs about 3.3 points higher, and adding a 60-month option reduced 36-month loans by 14.5% in the studied range ([Hertzberg, Liberman, Paravisini, Philadelphia Fed WP 18-05](https://www.philadelphiafed.org/-/media/frbp/assets/working-papers/2018/wp18-05.pdf)).

## Secured and retirement-funded options trade a lower rate for a worse failure mode

Home equity loans averaged **8.136% (10-year), 8.665% (15-year), and 8.239% (HELOC) as of September 7, 2026** ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/article/home-equity-rates-09-07-2026/)). Bankrate's October 8, 2026 cash-out refinance samples for a 30-year fixed ran **6.75% to 7.375% (APRs 6.918% to 7.648%)** with about 1 to 1.9 points and $4,975 to $8,594 of upfront costs, and cash-out rates typically run 0.25 to 0.5 points above ordinary refinance rates ([Bankrate](https://www.bankrate.com/mortgages/cash-out-refinance-rates/)). Those four-lender samples are not a market average. The structural point is that a cash-out refinance reprices the *entire* mortgage at today's rate, so for anyone holding a lower-rate first mortgage the true cost of the cash taken is higher than the headline; a HELOC leaves the first mortgage alone but carries a variable rate. Both turn unsecured debt into debt secured by the home. The FTC warns that missed payments on a home-secured consolidation loan could cost you the home ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-get-out-debt)). The CFPB's January 2025 study of cash-out refinancing (2014 to 2021) found that paying other debts was the most common stated reason and that card and auto balances fell significantly afterward and scores rose in the following quarter, but also flagged foreclosure risk and closing costs ([Orrick summary of CFPB report](https://infobytes.orrick.com/2025-01-31/cfpb-study-examines-cash-out-refinancing-and-nonmortgage-debts/)). Debt proceeds from a refinance used to pay cards are also not "qualified principal residence indebtedness" for tax purposes ([IRS Pub. 4681](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p4681.pdf)).

**The 401(k) loan** deserves particular attention when employment is uncertain. The IRS allows a loan of the lesser of 50% of the vested balance or $50,000 (up to $10,000 if 50% is lower, if the plan permits), repaid generally within five years with at least quarterly payments. On leaving the employer the plan may require full repayment, and an unpaid balance is treated as a distribution reported on Form 1099-R. Tax can be avoided by rolling the outstanding balance into an IRA or eligible plan by the due date, including extensions, of the federal return for the year of the deemed distribution; loans that miss the schedule are deemed distributions that may also draw the 10% early-distribution tax ([IRS Retirement Topics: Loans](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-loans)). The research did not verify how bankruptcy treats 401(k) loans or retirement balances, though South Carolina's exemption statute lists ERISA-qualified pensions and IRAs as exempt from creditors ([SC Code 15-41-30](https://scstatehouse.gov/code/t15c041.php)). Borrowing from a protected retirement account to pay dischargeable card debt converts a protected asset into spent cash, which is the same logic that applies to life-insurance loans (below).

Three other options are mostly traps. **Life-insurance policy loans** need no credit check and may reach 90% of cash value, but interest accrues, an unpaid loan cuts the death benefit, and a lapse can trigger a tax bill on policy gain even when little cash value remains ([Guardian](https://www.guardianlife.com/life-insurance/loans); [Kitces](https://www.kitces.com/blog/life-insurance-loan-taxation-rules-at-death-or-lapse/)). **Margin loans** let a broker sell your securities without consulting you and without a margin call ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/investor/alerts/ib_marginaccounts.pdf); [FINRA](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/margin-calls)). **Auto-title and payday loans** run about 300% to nearly 400% APR, and four in five payday loans are rolled over or reborrowed, per the CFPB's 2016-era research (the newest data retrieved) ([CFPB title lending](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/201605_cfpb_single-payment-vehicle-title-lending.pdf); [CFPB payday highlights](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/Payday_Loans_Highlights_From_CFPB_Research.pdf)). Buy-now-pay-later balances are small and easy to overlook in a debt inventory; the CFPB found BNPL users carry $871 more in open card balances on average ([CFPB BNPL report, Jan 2025](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_BNPL_Report_2025_01.pdf)).

## Nonprofit debt management plans: real rate relief with South Carolina price caps

The FTC describes a DMP as a counselor setting a payment schedule with creditors, who may lower interest rates or waive fees, while you make monthly deposits and the agency pays your unsecured debts; plans can run 48 months or more and are not for secured debts ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-get-out-debt)). The CFPB adds that counselors cannot erase debts, that agencies may charge fees, and that a reputable one never advises you to stop paying ([CFPB](https://consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-the-difference-between-credit-counseling-and-debt-settlement-debt-consolidation-or-credit-repair-en-1449)). GreenPath, a provider, reports its clients' average rate falls to 6.6% from about 28% and payments drop an average $199 per month; that is marketing data ([GreenPath](https://www.greenpath.com/debt-settlement-risks/)). NFCC says creditors may cut rates to 10% or less ([NFCC](https://www.nfcc.org/blog/debt-relief-programs-the-pros-and-cons-of-each-type/)). No published DMP completion rate was found; a 2020 CFPB report shows only that about 34 million accounts for 18 million consumers had been settled or managed through counseling through 2019 ([CFPB Trends, July 2020](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_quarterly-consumer-credit-trends_debt-settlement-credit-counseling_2020-07.pdf)).

South Carolina regulates this industry unusually tightly. Credit counseling organizations need an SCDCA license and a bond of at least $25,000; the agency must complete a written budget analysis and may not accept an account unless the debtor can meet the budget; the contract term may not exceed five years; consumers may cancel for any reason on ten days' written notice with a refund of unspent funds; and charging fees before they are earned is prohibited ([SC Code Title 37, Ch. 7](https://scstatehouse.gov/code/t37c007.php)). Faith-based nonprofits, attorneys, banks and credit unions are excluded from the licensing definition. A regulation excerpt caps fees at **$60 for an initial consultation, $50 for DMP setup, and $10 per creditor per month up to $70 monthly** ([SC Code of Regulations Ch. 28](https://scstatehouse.gov/coderegs/Chapter%2028.pdf)); the research could not confirm this is the current version, so verify with SCDCA (complaint line 800-922-1594) ([SCDCA](https://consumer.sc.gov/business-resourceslaws/licensing/credit-counseling)). By simple addition (self-computed), the most a first month could cost is about $120 plus a $60 consultation if charged. The suitability requirement is a gate: for someone with variable or reduced income, an agency may be unable to accept the account.

On credit reporting, myFICO says enrolling in a DMP does not directly affect your score and that a creditor notation is not scored as negative, but card closures can spike utilization and "temporarily negatively" affect scores; NFCC's framing ("without affecting your credit score") is rosier, and the two conflict ([myFICO](https://www.myfico.com/credit-education/blog/debt-management-score); [NFCC](https://www.nfcc.org/resources/debt-management-plans/)). Experian puts typical DMP length at three to five years ([Experian](https://www.experian.com/blogs/ask-experian/what-is-debt-management/)). Ramsey's side calls counseling a "last-ditch effort" with fees ([Ramsey](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/credit-counseling)).

## Debt settlement: legal fee rules, heavy collateral damage, and a tax bill

Settlement firms charge **15% to 25% of enrolled debt** over two to four years ([NFCC](https://www.nfcc.org/blog/which-debt-repayment-method-is-right-for-you-a-closer-look-at-debt-settlement/); [NerdWallet](https://www.nerdwallet.com/article/loans/personal-loans/is-debt-settlement-a-good-idea)). The FTC's rule is that a company cannot collect fees before settling your debt, and savings should sit in an account you own and can withdraw from ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-get-out-debt)). NFCC says for-profit settlement is not recommended "under any circumstances." Programs usually require stopping payments, so late fees accrue, creditors may sue, and a judgment could lead to wage garnishment or a lien where state law allows. NFCC estimates score drops "exceed 100 points in most cases," while GreenPath cites an NCLC average of 161 points; neither is a FICO or bureau figure. Two industry-linked success statistics cannot be reconciled: NFCC cites a study where clients settle 43% of accounts by month 36 and pay over 78% of the original balance, whereas a 2023 industry-commissioned report claims average net savings of 31.9% of what clients owed ([NerdWallet](https://www.nerdwallet.com/article/loans/personal-loans/is-debt-settlement-a-good-idea)). Ramsey calls settlement "a scam" and says firms typically charge 20% to 25% ([Ramsey](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/what-is-debt-relief)).

Recent enforcement shows the pattern to avoid. In July 2025 the FTC filed a complaint against a group operating as "Accelerated Debt," alleging false claims of cutting debt by up to 75% or 85%, impersonation of card issuers and agencies, and illegal advance fees; the FTC estimated about $100 million was taken. In April 2026 a court entered a temporary restraining order against an alleged student-loan relief scheme that had collected at least $8.8 million. These are allegations at the stage of the sources read ([Consumer Finance Monitor](https://www.consumerfinancemonitor.com/2025/07/28/ftc-takes-action-against-accelerated-debt-for-running-a-debt-relief-scam/); [Orrick](https://infobytes.orrick.com/2026-04-24/ftc-obtains-temporary-restraining-order-against-alleged-student-loan-debt-relief-scheme/)). The FTC's March 2026 alert lists the red flags: upfront fees, guaranteed results, and unsolicited calls or texts ([FTC](https://www.consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2026/03/looking-debt-relief-heres-how-avoid-scam)).

**Forgiven debt is generally taxable.** The IRS treats canceled debt as ordinary income for the year of cancellation, reportable even without a Form 1099-C. Debt discharged in a Title 11 bankruptcy case is excluded, and an insolvency exclusion applies "to the extent" liabilities exceeded the fair market value of assets immediately beforehand, claimed on Form 982 (IRS example: $10,000 liabilities, $7,000 assets, $5,000 canceled makes $3,000 excludable and $2,000 taxable) ([IRS Pub. 4681](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p4681); [IRS Topic 431](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc431)). The home-debt exclusion for qualified principal residence indebtedness applied only to discharges before January 1, 2026 (or written arrangements before that date), and the research found no evidence Congress extended it, though H.R. 917 is pending ([IRS Form 982 instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i982); [Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr917/BILLS-119hr917ih.htm)). The $600 reporting threshold comes from a secondary source only.

## Student loans, medical debt, and tax debt follow their own rules

Federal student loans are in transition. A court order ended the SAVE plan on March 10, 2026; the Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP) launched July 1, 2026 with payments of 1% to 10% of earnings and forgiveness after 30 years; loans disbursed on or after July 1, 2026 can use only RAP or the tiered standard plan; PAYE and ICR are scheduled to end by July 1, 2028 ([NerdWallet](https://www.nerdwallet.com/student-loans/learn/save-lawsuits); [Attorney Haskell](https://attorneyhaskell.com/blog/student-loan-repayment-changes-2026)). These come from secondary sources because studentaid.gov would not load. Consolidating can change which plans you can use (RAP excludes Parent PLUS and consolidation loans that repaid Parent PLUS debt), so check the servicer before consolidating. Private refinance rates run from just under 4% to roughly 11% to 14% depending on source ([Bankrate](https://www.bankrate.com/loans/student-loans/current-interest-rates/); [Credible](https://www.credible.com/refinance-student-loans)); refinancing federal loans into private ones forfeits federal protections, a point the research did not verify from a primary source.

Medical debt: the three credit bureaus voluntarily stopped reporting paid medical debts, those under a year old, and those under $500 in March 2022, and a judge vacated the CFPB's January 2025 rule to remove most medical debt from reports in July 2025; eleven states had their own laws as of June 2025 ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-08-29_IF12169_044474269126c7f04642e141e5ac7130f1beba16.html)). Tax debt has structured options: short-term plans up to 180 days, Simple Payment Plans for assessed balances of $50,000 or less, offers in compromise (not available during an open bankruptcy), and Currently Not Collectible status, which is temporary, with interest and penalties continuing ([IRS Topic 202](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc202)).

## What the research says happens after consolidation

The strongest evidence is mixed rather than damning. Di Maggio and Yao analyzed 3.79 million fintech loans to 1.88 million borrowers and found that borrowers who used the loans to pay down card debt "often ran their cards up again within months"; scores first improved then fell, and about a year later more than 5% were likely to default, roughly 25% higher risk than similar bank borrowers ([HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/the-dark-side-of-fintech-borrowing); [NBER WP 28021](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28021/w28021.pdf)). By contrast, Dore and Mach (Prosper marketplace borrowers, about three-quarters consolidating) found utilization fell to 44% versus 56% for non-borrowers, scores were about 13 points higher at application and 6.6 points higher four quarters later, total debt was about 27% higher at application and 13% higher twelve quarters later, and delinquency was *lower* by about 1.1 to 1.8 percentage points ([Fed FEDS 2019-022](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2019022pap.pdf)). The two samples differ by platform, years, and benchmark, so the honest reading is divergent findings, not consensus. Both agree on one mechanism: freed credit gets used again. Gathergood and coauthors found people split repayments in proportion to balances rather than targeting the highest-rate card ([AER 2019](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/aer.20180288)), and the 2009 CARD Act cut borrowing costs by an annualized 1.7% of balances ([NBER 19484](https://www.nber.org/papers/w19484)). No verified study gives a headline "percent who re-accumulate" figure.

**Consolidation fits** when income covers the new payment with margin, the all-in APR (fees included) is clearly below the blended current rate, the term is not stretched so far that total interest rises, the spending gap is closed, and the new debt stays unsecured. It fits poorly when income is unstable or falling, when the debt is already mostly unaffordable, or when the only approvable loans are high-APR or secured by a home or car (this is a synthesis, not a sourced rule).

## Credit scores: modest dents from consolidation, long shadows from default

myFICO publishes weights of payment history 35%, amounts owed 30%, length of history 15%, new credit 10%, credit mix 10%, and declines to publish point effects. A hard inquiry "can lower your score by a few points," counts for scoring for 12 months, and stays on the report two years. Closing a card is not helpful: in myFICO's example, closing an unused $3,000 line on $6,500 of total limits with $2,000 of balances lifts utilization from 30% to 57% ([myFICO weights](https://www.myfico.com/credit-education/whats-in-your-credit-score); [myFICO new credit](https://www.myfico.com/credit-education/credit-scores/new-credit); [myFICO FAQ](https://www.myfico.com/credit-education/faq/cards/impact-of-closing-credit-card-account)). Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act, bankruptcy may stay up to 10 years and collections or adverse items up to 7; Experian reports Chapter 13 for seven years and Chapter 7 for ten ([15 U.S.C. 1681c](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=(title:15%20section:1681c%20edition:prelim)); [Experian](https://www.experian.com/blogs/ask-experian/will-debt-relief-hurt-my-credit-score/)). A study of 90-plus-day delinquency found negative effects on scores, credit limits, homeownership and income lasting up to ten years ([arXiv 2306.00574](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00574)). Time-to-recover figures were not sourced.
