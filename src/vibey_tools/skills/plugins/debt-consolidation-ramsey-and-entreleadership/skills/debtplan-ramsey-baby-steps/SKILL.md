## Part 2. Dave Ramsey's 7 Baby Steps

*Method caveat: several Ramsey pages were read through a fetch tool that produced summaries rather than raw text, and some were later confirmed on the live page. Where a point rests on a summary only, it is flagged.*

## The seven steps, as currently published, are a fixed sequence with few exceptions

The sequence is: (1) a $1,000 starter emergency fund; (2) pay off all non-mortgage debt with the debt snowball; (3) a fully funded emergency fund of three to six months of expenses; (4) invest 15% of household income for retirement; (5) save for children's college; (6) pay off the home early; (7) build wealth and give. Ramsey's own pages recognize few exceptions: Step 2 excludes the mortgage, an optional Step 3b covers a home down payment, retirement saving may be paused up to about two years to build that down payment, and identity-theft coverage may be skipped temporarily if it slows the snowball ([Help Center](https://help.ramseysolutions.com/hc/en-us/articles/360046646012-What-are-the-7-Baby-Steps); [Save for a house](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/real-estate/how-to-save-money-to-buy-a-house); [Insurance](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/insurance/insurance-and-the-baby-steps)). A discrepancy remains unresolved: the main help-center article lists seven steps with no 3b, while the Step 3 page and EveryDollar help describe it.

## Step 1 is still $1,000, and the page defends that figure openly

The Step 1 page says to save $1,000 "as fast as possible," acknowledges the number has not changed in about 30 years, argues it was always meant as a starter, and suggests finishing in about 30 days. No variant exists for higher earners, and I found no dated change to $2,000; third-party claims of one are unsupported unless a dated Ramsey source appears. The page also says to give 10% of gross first, pay only minimums on debt, pause investing "even up to the company match," and cover the Four Walls (food, utilities, shelter, transportation), citing a 2026 Federal Reserve report that 37% of people could not cover a $400 expense ([Step 1](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-1)). The emergency test is unexpected, necessary, urgent, and not coverable from the budget. The page concedes $1,000 will not cover job loss or major surgery, which is Step 3's job. Critics such as one CFP quoted by FinanceBuzz accept a 3-to-6-month fund but dislike a fixed $1,000 and cutting all enjoyment ([FinanceBuzz](https://financebuzz.com/news/financial-advisors-dave-ramseys-advice-2)).

## Step 2: the snowball is smallest-to-largest, and tax debt jumps the line

Mechanics: list every debt except the mortgage, smallest balance to largest, ignoring interest rate; pay minimums on all but the smallest; throw everything extra at the smallest; when it is gone, roll its payment into the next. Covered debts include credit cards, student loans, auto loans, medical debt, personal loans, and IRS or back-tax debt. The page gives a typical timeline of 18 to 24 months for most families. The FAQ resolves an earlier ambiguity: **tax debt goes to the top of the snowball "regardless of the balance,"** so the accurate description is smallest-to-largest with tax debt first ([Step 2](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-2)). Other stated rules: sell unneeded items and put non-retirement savings above $1,000 toward debt; cut up cards; avoid balance transfers and personal loans; pause 401(k) contributions "even the match" because debt at 7%, 15%, or 20% outweighs assumed 10% to 12% returns; treat retirement funds as untouchable except when facing bankruptcy or foreclosure; pause the snowball if the emergency fund is spent; and, for collections, call creditors, ask about hardship programs, and protect the Four Walls. The FAQ also concedes that the avalanche "saves you a little more" in interest.

### Ramsey's position on every consolidation tool is no, except one

Ramsey Solutions' debt-consolidation article (by Jade Warshaw, published June 2, 2023, updated February 28, 2025) answers "No" for consolidation loans (citing longer payoff, fees, often higher interest, and collateral risk), "No" for balance transfers (fees and rate spikes), and "No" for HELOCs, which it calls a trap. The lone "Maybe" is federal student loan consolidation, "the only form of debt consolidation we recommend," case by case, for example to swap variable for fixed rates; the company notes that it does not lower the rate (the weighted average is rounded up), may forfeit credit toward income-driven plans or PSLF, and is generally irreversible ([Debt consolidation](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/debt-consolidation-truth); [Student loan consolidation](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/should-i-consolidate-my-student-loans)). It says forgiveness and income-driven plans are not to be banked on. The article also says consolidation hurts credit and that the author does not "give a rip" about credit. Credit scores are called an "I love debt" score; the pages advise cutting up cards and say a mortgage is available through manual underwriting ([Truth about debt](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/the-truth-about-debt); [Living without a credit score](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/living-without-credit-score)). A different quoted phrase attributed to Ramsey, "I don't care about your credit score," was not found in a primary source and should not be treated as verified.

### On counseling, settlement, and bankruptcy, the primary text was thin

Per summaries, Ramsey calls credit counseling a "last-ditch effort" (DMPs run three to five years with fees, and nonprofit status is not automatic proof of trustworthiness), settlement "a scam," and bankruptcy a last resort that is "not an easy out," lasts roughly 7 to 10 years on a report, and does not erase student loans, child support, or generally IRS debt. His alternatives to bankruptcy are covering the Four Walls, selling items, a bare-bones budget, a second job, and a coach ([Credit counseling](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/credit-counseling); [Debt relief](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/what-is-debt-relief); [Avoid bankruptcy](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/how-to-avoid-bankruptcy)). In a 2022 column he rejected an attorney's advice to file over a $3,000 debt as bad advice ([Dave Says](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/company/newsroom/ramsey-syndication/2022-12-27)). The research did not retrieve primary text for those pages beyond summaries and snippets, so the details above should be checked live. His own history is relevant context: Ramsey's biography says the bankruptcy was **September 23, 1988**, after real-estate success built on debt, and not the early 1990s that circulates in some accounts; his testimony page says only that he and his wife filed bankruptcy ([Dave bio](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey); [Testimony](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/dave-ramsey-personal-testimony)).

## When income stops, Ramsey's pages say to protect the Four Walls and slow the plan

For people out of work or in hardship, Ramsey's pages tell readers to cover the Four Walls first, call creditors about hardship options, treat Baby Step 1 budgeting as the priority, and pause the snowball if the emergency fund must be used ([Budgeting after job loss](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/budgeting/budgeting-after-job-loss); [When an emergency exceeds your fund](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/saving/what-to-do-if-emergency-is-more-than-emergency-fund); [If you get laid off](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/career-advice/what-to-do-if-you-get-laid-off); [When you can't pay your bills](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/what-to-do-when-you-cant-pay-your-bills)). The fixed-income article keeps standard budgeting, says saving the first $1,000 may take longer, and names income level as the main obstacle; it does not address disability benefits or Social Security asset rules ([Fixed income](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/debt-free-on-fixed-income)). No page was found on whether the steps formally change for someone out of work beyond this pause guidance. The July 2026 show segment reported by Yahoo (a caller proposing to cash out a 401(k) to clear $20,800) shows the same line: don't cash it out; stop contributing until debt-free ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/articles/dave-ramsey-says-early-401-205108168.html)). That is a secondary report, not a transcript.

## Steps 3 through 7 are less contested, with one major exception

**Step 3** is three to six months of essential expenses (three with a steady job, six with variable income), held liquid and uninvested, typically taking 6 to 12 months ([Step 3](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-3)). **Step 4** is 15% of gross household income, excluding the employer match, in tax-advantaged accounts; the order is match, then Roth IRA, then back to the 401(k) ("Match beats Roth beats traditional"), or for those without a match or who are self-employed, Roth IRA first, then SEP-IRA or solo 401(k). The funds are split "evenly" across four categories (growth and income, growth, aggressive growth, international); a 25%-each figure appears in a separate article, not on the Step 4 page ([Step 4](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-4); [Mutual funds](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/retirement/how-to-invest-in-mutual-funds)). **Step 5** covers college: an ESA up to $2,000 per year or a 529, with retirement ahead of college ([Step 5](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-5)). **Step 6** is extra principal-only payments on the mortgage, no cash-out refinance, no 401(k) withdrawals; an example shows a $300,000 mortgage at 5% over 15 years at about $2,373 per month ([Step 6](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-6)). The 25%-of-take-home and 15-year-fixed rules belong to the home-buying article, not the Step 6 page ([How much house](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/real-estate/how-much-house-can-i-afford)). **Step 7** treats 10% giving as a common starting point rather than a rule, and covers wills, trusts, and insurance ([Step 7](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-7)). Insurance is "not a Baby Step" but a protection for every step, and Ramsey recommends level term life insurance of 10 to 12 times income ([Insurance](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/insurance/insurance-and-the-baby-steps)), advice independent planners consider mainstream.

The SmartVestor/RamseyTrusted referral network is attached to Step 4. The program formerly called Endorsed Local Providers is now RamseyTrusted, with nearly 5,000 providers ([RamseyTrusted](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/retirement/how-does-elp-program-work)). Ramsey's own disclosure says SmartVestor Pros pay a $3,000 training fee, $400 per month, territory fees up to $1,050 per month, and, in select territories, up to 25% per year of gross fees from referred clients; Ramsey calls itself a "paid, non-client promoter," acknowledges a conflict of interest, and says it does not monitor Pros' advice ([SmartVestor disclosure](https://ramseysolutions.com/retirement/smartvestor-disclosure)). InvestmentNews reported that Missouri regulators found Lampo acted as an unregistered investment solicitor from 2010 to 2014 and in fall 2016 ordered $50,000 to an investor education fund plus $5,000 costs; Ramsey then restructured the program as advertising ([InvestmentNews](https://www.investmentnews.com/rias/advisers-profit-through-association-with-dave-ramsey-but-not-by-as-much-as-in-the-past/71056)). The article's date was not shown in the fetch and its fee figures predate the current disclosure.

## The evidence: strong on behavior, weak on investment math

### The snowball has modest behavioral support and a small interest penalty

The academic record supports a narrower claim than "snowball beats avalanche." Kettle, Trudel, Blanchard and Häubl (Journal of Consumer Research, 2016) found concentrating repayments on one account increases motivation to get out of debt, strongest when it is the smallest account, and people appear to judge progress by the largest proportional reduction in any single account ([JCR abstract](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/43/3/460/2630069); [St. Gallen record](https://www.alexandria.unisg.ch/entities/publication/fa49decd-c7bd-4c0c-813a-aa497db505fe)). Gal and McShane (2012) found closing out whole accounts predicts debt elimination ([JMR](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmr.11.0272)). But Amar and coauthors (2011) describe "debt account aversion" as potentially costly when it ignores interest rates ([JMR](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmkr.48.SPL.S38)), and Hamilton (2023) quantifies the snowball's extra interest ([Southern Economic Journal](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/soecon/v89y2023i3p830-859.html)). Ramsey's own FAQ concedes the avalanche costs a little less. Early Retirement Now argues avalanche is a "mathematical certainty" to be more efficient ([ERN](https://earlyretirementnow.com/2017/06/14/good-advice-vs-feel-good-advice/)); Kitces values the snowball's behavioral benefits but would target debts above roughly 8% earlier ([Kitces](https://www.kitces.com/blog/checklist-style-financial-planning-dave-ramsey-baby-steps-money-guy-show-order-of-operations/)).

A **self-computed** example from the research: four cards totaling $30,000 ($3,000 at 19.99%, $6,000 at 29.99%, $9,000 at 24.99%, $12,000 at 17.99%, minimums of $75, $180, $250 and $300) with a $1,500 monthly budget. Avalanche takes 25 months and $7,222 of interest; snowball takes 26 months and $7,571, a gap of about $349 and one month. The gap grows when the largest balance also carries the highest rate and the budget is tight. When the difference is a few hundred dollars, the method a person will actually sustain is the better one. Whatever one concludes, the Baby Steps' consolidation ban and snowball are bundled: nothing in the sources shows that rejecting a lower-rate consolidation loan beats using one with the same discipline.

### The "millionaire study" is real but not independent

Ramsey's National Study of Millionaires covers over **10,000** millionaires, fielded November 17, 2017 to January 31, 2018, on a page dated June 11, 2024; it reports 79% received no inheritance, 8 in 10 invested in a 401(k), and 94% live on less than they earn ([Ramsey study](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/retirement/the-national-study-of-millionaires-research); [PDF](https://cdn.ramseysolutions.net/media/company/pr/everyday-millionaires-research/national-study-of-millionaire-new.pdf)). A "2,000" figure circulating in summaries is not a separate study: book excerpts describe a random subsample of about 2,000 with no prior Ramsey connection, and one independent reader estimates the rest came from Ramsey's own audience, raising selection concerns ([Empathic Finance](https://empathicfinance.com/what-does-the-data-say-reading-the-national-study-of-millionaires/); the 2,000/8,000 split is that reader's inference, not Ramsey's document). The data are self-reported, nearly nine years old, and cannot show how many Baby Steps followers did not become millionaires (survivorship). No separate Baby Steps outcome study exists. The only academic work found on Financial Peace University is an Iowa State thesis of 32 couples, self-reported with no control group ([Iowa State](https://dr.lib.iastate.edu/handle/20.500.12876/25655)).

### The return assumptions are the weak leg

Ramsey told a caller in May 2025 that $100 per month at 12% for 40 years grows to about $1.176 million; at 8% it would be roughly $300,000 ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/dave-ramsey-calls-listeners-math-033116346.html)). Over 40-year S&P 500 periods from 1871 to 2012, the average was 9.27% nominal and 6.42% real, and 5.56% of periods exceeded 12% nominal ([DQYDJ](https://dqydj.com/dave-ramseys-investment-advice/)). US stocks from 1926 to 2014 averaged 12.14% arithmetically but 10.14% annualized ([White Coat Investor](https://whitecoatinvestor.com/dave-ramsey-doubles-down-on-his-bad-investment-advice)). The dispute is arithmetic versus compound and nominal versus real, not the raw data. The withdrawal claim is weaker: Ramsey defended an 8% safe withdrawal rate as 12% minus 4% inflation, and Early Retirement Now's 30-year simulations show roughly 56% to 61% failure at 8% ([ERN](https://earlyretirementnow.com/2023/11/12/dave-ramsey-8-percent-withdrawal-rate/)); Of Dollars and Data found about 42% of 30-year periods since 1935 ran out of money at 8% in all-equity portfolios ([Of Dollars and Data](https://ofdollarsanddata.com/how-safe-is-an-8-withdrawal-rate/)). Yahoo reported in April 2026 that Ramsey again suggested retirees withdraw around 8% ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/articles/dave-ramsey-says-withdraw-8-094500759.html)). These matter to anyone reading Step 4 as a retirement plan, but they are irrelevant to the debt-payoff sequence.

### Planner critiques cluster on a handful of rules

Pausing the employer match is the most-cited objection: Robert Johnson (CFA) calls forfeiting the match one of the worst mistakes, and CFPs quoted by Investopedia in November 2025 would contribute at least to the match while paying debt ([Nasdaq/Benzinga](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/dave-ramsey-urges-americans-pause-401k-contributions-should-you); [Investopedia](https://www.investopedia.com/why-financial-experts-say-dave-ramsey-is-wrong-about-pausing-401k-contributions-11852780)). Kitces' order of operations puts capturing the match second, ahead of non-high-interest debt. Ramsey's reply is the "power of focus" framing and the rate-versus-return comparison in the Step 2 FAQ; no direct reply to a named critic was found ([transcript](https://podcasts.happyscribe.com/the-dave-ramsey-show/my-dad-has-hoarded-500-000-in-cash)). Other objections: avoiding credit cards entirely (card-affiliate outlets making that case have an opposite conflict); front-end load funds, which Ramsey defends as a one-time cost ([White Coat Investor](https://whitecoatinvestor.com/dave-ramsey-doubles-down-on-his-bad-investment-advice)); 15-year-only mortgages; and student-loan advice for forgiveness-eligible borrowers ([White Coat Investor on PSLF](https://www.whitecoatinvestor.com/dave-ramseys-bad-advice-about-pslf/), a 2015 letter that predates RAP and SAVE's end).

### Where independent planners agree

Kitces praises the Baby Steps for clear, low-friction actions and behavioral momentum, and says it matters more that people act than that the assumptions be perfect ([Kitces](https://www.kitces.com/blog/checklist-style-financial-planning-dave-ramsey-baby-steps-money-guy-show-order-of-operations/)). Planners endorse 3-to-6-month emergency funds and minimizing debt, and even math critics accept that paying off a 14%-plus card is correct ([Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/2020/09/15/4-things-dave-ramsey-gets-wrong-about-investing)). A fair summary: a good behavioral scaffold with shaky technical assumptions.

## Controversies: allegations and procedural rulings, not findings

The following are plaintiffs' allegations or procedural rulings; none is a finding of liability in the sources read, and none bears on whether the snowball arithmetic works.

**O'Connor v. The Lampo Group (M.D. Tenn.):** the plaintiff alleged she was fired after revealing a pregnancy while unmarried; the company said it enforced a "righteous living" conduct rule and disclosed in a March 2021 filing that it had fired at least eight other employees in five years for premarital sex ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/business/business-news/dave-ramsey-s-company-fires-employees-over-premarital-sex-court-n1262498)). On June 12, 2025 the judge allowed her religious-discrimination claim to proceed ([Religion News Service](https://religionnews.com/2025/06/18/dave-ramseys-company-loses-again-in-court-over-discrimination/)). **The case was dismissed by agreement on December 11, 2025 (Doc. 120)**; settlement terms were not disclosed in the docket sources, and a settlement is an inference ([CourtListener](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/17372107/oconnor-v-the-lampo-group-llc/)).

**Amos v. Lampo Group (M.D. Tenn.):** a video editor alleged religious discrimination after his July 2020 firing; the company said the suit has "absolutely no merit" and that he was let go for performance. The district court dismissed in December 2023, and the Sixth Circuit reversed the discrimination claim on August 8, 2024 while affirming dismissal of a fraud claim ([MinistryWatch](https://ministrywatch.com/appeals-court-rules-against-dave-ramseys-company/)). Earlier reporting said trial was set for mid-July 2025. As of the docket, a November 10, 2026 trial was later set, and **Doc. 406 (September 18, 2026) cancelled the trial, pretrial conference and deadlines, to be rescheduled "as soon as feasible"**; the reason is not stated in the order read ([CourtListener docket](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/61614266/amos-v-lampo-group-llc-the/); [Doc. 406](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.tnmd.88710/gov.uscourts.tnmd.88710.406.0.pdf)). A trial before 2027 appears unlikely, but no document says so.

**Patrick v. Ramsey (W.D. Wash., 2:23-cv-00630):** 17 former listeners allege Ramsey was paid up to $30 million (2015 to 2021) to endorse Timeshare Exit Team without adequate disclosure and seek over $150 million; Ramsey stopped advertising the firm in 2021 and his lawyers said he was not responsible for details of the relationship ([Religion News Service, 2023](https://religionnews.com/2023/06/01/dave-ramsey-sued-for-150-million-by-former-fans-who-followed-his-timeshare-exit-advice/)). The Ninth Circuit denied his bid to compel arbitration on November 12, 2025; a 60-day extension was granted September 29, 2026, the latest filing seen was October 7, 2026, and no class has been certified ([CourtListener docket](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/67290865/)).

Two other items are thinly sourced: a former employee's suit alleging she was pushed out after coming out as gay, where the settlement report is single-source (Wikipedia) while a 2021 Nashville Post article reported no resolution ([Nashville Post](https://www.nashvillepost.com/business/legal/former-ramsey-employee-says-she-was-fired-for-coming-out/article_484bdd21-20d7-58b0-bdb8-9045862c482f.html)); and Inc. magazine's removal of the company from its 2020 Best Workplaces list over the conduct policy ([MinistryWatch](https://ministrywatch.com/dave-ramseys-company-dropped-from-best-workplaces-list-by-inc-magazine/)). Several other circulating claims were searched for and not found in any source, including any EEOC action, so none is repeated here. A mortgage-industry author's claim that Ramsey is a "paid lead provider" for a partner lender is an interested party's opinion ([HousingWire](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/reverse-mortgage-pro-fact-checks-product-claims-by-dave-ramsey/)).
