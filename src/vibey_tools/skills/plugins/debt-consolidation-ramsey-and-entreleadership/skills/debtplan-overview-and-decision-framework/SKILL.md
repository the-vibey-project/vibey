## Executive summary

**Debt consolidation is a payment-structure tool, not a debt-reduction tool, and it works only when income reliably covers the new payment.** Consolidation (a balance-transfer card, a personal loan, a home-secured loan, a 401(k) loan) replaces several debts with one new debt that must be repaid in full; only settlement and bankruptcy reduce what is owed. The best verified evidence shows a short-lived benefit: borrowers in a Federal Reserve study saw card utilization drop about 12 percentage points in the first quarter, but the gap shrank to about 1.7 points within a year and total debt ended up higher ([Dore and Mach, Fed 2019](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2019022pap.pdf)). Dave Ramsey's seven Baby Steps reject consolidation entirely (federal student loan consolidation, case by case, is the lone exception) in favor of a behavioral method, the debt snowball, that independent planners call a good motivational scaffold with shaky technical assumptions. Bankruptcy is the legal reset that Ramsey calls a last resort, and for South Carolina residents it is a mainstream option that the means test, state exemptions, and South Carolina's garnishment limits all shape. EntreLeadership, Ramsey's small-business arm, applies the same no-debt, cash-reserve logic to companies; its useful pieces for a one-person LLC are the money rules, not the team-management machinery.

The key takeaways, in the order they matter:

- **Cost math beats marketing.** On $30,000, a 12% loan over 60 months costs about $10,040 in interest, while the same rate over 84 months costs about $14,485; a lower payment from a longer term can cost more (self-computed amortization, shown in Part 1).
- **Secured consolidation changes who can collect.** Turning card debt into a HELOC, cash-out refinance, or title loan swaps lawsuit risk for foreclosure or repossession risk, and it can convert debt that South Carolina's exemption and garnishment rules make hard to collect into debt that is not.
- **A 401(k) loan becomes taxable if the job ends and the balance is not repaid or rolled over** by the tax filing deadline for that year ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-loans)).
- **South Carolina is unusually debtor-protective on wages and old debt.** State law bars garnishing earnings on consumer credit debts, the statute of limitations on contract debt is three years, and any part-payment on an old debt can revive it under section 15-3-120 ([SC Code 37-5-104](https://scstatehouse.gov/code/t37c005.php); [SC Code Title 15, Ch. 3](https://scstatehouse.gov/code/t15c003.php)).
- **The Chapter 7 means test looks backward six months and counts unemployment compensation but not Social Security**, so a recent income drop can matter a great deal; South Carolina's median for a one-person household is $64,808 for cases filed since April 1, 2026 ([USTP median income table](https://www.justice.gov/ust/eo/bapcpa/20260401/bci_data/median_income_table.htm); [Official Form 122A-1](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/form_b122a-1.pdf)).
- **Ramsey's Step 1 is still $1,000 and Step 2 is smallest-to-largest regardless of rate, with tax debt placed first**, and his organization says to pause retirement saving, even the employer match, until the debt is gone ([Step 1](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-1); [Step 2](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/dave-ramsey-7-baby-steps/step-2)).
- **No head-to-head outcome study compares the Baby Steps, consolidation, and bankruptcy.** Every ranking of them, including any in this report, is reasoning from partial evidence.
- **EntreLeadership has no "Business Baby Steps," "EntreLeadership Pyramid," or "Six Steps."** What exists is a 15-chapter 2011 book, a Five Stages of Business model, and Six Drivers of business growth, plus a no-debt rule and a six-month business reserve.
- **For a solo LLC owner, state unemployment rules matter more than most management advice.** South Carolina's Department of Employment and Workforce (DEW) treats self-employment as reportable and potentially disqualifying, and unreported earnings are treated as fraud ([DEW](https://www.dew.sc.gov/individuals/how-unemployment-insurance-works)).

## Part 4. Connecting the three: consolidation, Ramsey's method, and bankruptcy

## The three paths solve different problems

Consolidation changes the *price and shape* of debt you intend to repay in full. Ramsey's method changes the *behavior and sequence* of repaying in full, using the snowball and a cash-flow squeeze. Bankruptcy changes the *legal obligation itself*. They are therefore not strictly rivals: all three answer a different question, which is why a person can reasonably combine pieces (a DMP's lower rates with snowball-style focus, or a Chapter 13 plan with a Ramsey budget). What is contested is the sequencing and the cost of being wrong. The research found no head-to-head outcome data, so the comparison below is reasoned from the cited evidence and flagged where it extends beyond it.

| Dimension | Consolidation (loan, card, secured) | Ramsey's Baby Steps (Steps 1-2) | Bankruptcy (Chapter 7 / Chapter 13) |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it changes | Rate, term, number of payments | Order of payoff, spending, income push | Legal obligation; automatic court process |
| Principal repaid | Full | Full | Chapter 7: discharged; Chapter 13: per plan, at least Chapter 7 value to unsecured |
| Upfront cost | Fees: 3% to 5% (transfer card), up to 12% (online loan), points and closing costs (secured) | Free method; optional paid tools (EveryDollar $79.99/yr; FPU $79.99) | Court fee about $335 to $338 plus attorney, roughly $1,100 to $3,000 quoted (weak source); Chapter 13 fee $310 plus attorney |
| Requires income to cover | A new fixed payment | Minimums on all debts plus aggressive extra payment | Chapter 7 test: income vs. median; Chapter 13 plan payment |
| Credit effect | Inquiry, new account; utilization drop if cards stay at zero | Cutting cards; no new credit; Ramsey disregards scores | Up to 10 years (Chapter 7), 7 per Experian (Chapter 13) |
| Typical timeline | 15 to 21 months (0% card); 2 to 7 years (loans) | 18 to 24 months per Ramsey (company claim) | Chapter 7: roughly 3 to 4 months from filing (self-computed); Chapter 13: 3 to 5 years |
| Biggest failure mode | Re-accumulating debt; stretched term; secured collateral loss | Income too low or unstable for the squeeze; pausing the match | Losing non-exempt assets; some debts survive; credit shadow |
| Interaction with SC law | Secured loan bypasses exempt-income protections; part-payment can revive old debt | Part-payment on stale debt can revive it; Ramsey's "clean up old debts" advice is moral, not legal | Exemptions ($76,125 homestead etc.) and means test shape outcome |
| Ramsey's stance | No (except federal student loans, case by case) | The method | Last resort; "not an easy out" |
| Evidence base | Mixed academic findings; benefit decays | Behavioral lab/small studies; company surveys | US Courts data: over 99% discharge of non-dismissed Chapter 7 cases |

The Chapter 7 timeline figure derives from the meeting of creditors at 21 to 40 days plus discharge 60 to 90 days after that meeting, which is a self-computed range from US Courts data; actual cases vary.

## What Ramsey's approach gets right, and where reduced income changes the math

Ramsey's pages capture three things the academic evidence supports: concentration helps motivation, freed credit lines tempt re-borrowing (the Di Maggio and Yao pattern is exactly what the cut-up-the-cards rule targets), and an emergency buffer prevents debt from re-forming. Where the method is most exposed is stable income. The Step 2 timeline of 18 to 24 months assumes enough monthly slack to attack debt; Step 3 sets six months of reserve rather than three for variable income, the hardship pages tell readers to call creditors and protect the Four Walls, and his own fixed-income article concedes slower timelines, but none of this changes the steps themselves. Even Ramsey's most prominent exception, retirement money untouchable unless facing bankruptcy or foreclosure, shows that bankruptcy appears in the framework as a boundary condition rather than a banned word. Whether Dave's on-air statements go further was not checked (no podcast transcripts were searched beyond two cited).

The critique that applies most sharply to current conditions is not the return assumption but the *match* and *credit* rules. Pausing 401(k) contributions "even the match" is a stable core position (Step 1, Step 2, the 401(k) page, a 2020 show transcript, a July 2026 segment), and CFPs object to it. The credit-score position matters for people who may later need to rent, refinance, or finance a car; Ramsey's manual-underwriting answer applies to mortgages only in the sources read.

## A decision framework built from the evidence

This is a synthesis from the cited sources, not advice; a South Carolina bankruptcy attorney or a nonprofit credit counselor can apply it to actual numbers.

1. **Inventory everything first, including what is easy to miss.** List every debt with balance, APR, secured or unsecured, whether it is past the three-year South Carolina limitations period, and its collection status. Include BNPL, medical bills, tax debt, student loans, and any 401(k) loan. The CFPB data show BNPL users hold more card debt than non-users.
2. **Sort by who can reach what.** Secured debts (home, car) put collateral at stake; unsecured consumer-credit debts in South Carolina cannot reach wages by garnishment and cannot reach exempt benefits. Federal student loans, taxes and support have separate rules that this research did not fully cover.
3. **Separate the income stream from the debt.** Note which income is exempt (Social Security, unemployment compensation, veterans' and disability benefits under South Carolina's statute; federal benefits protected in bank accounts), which counts for the means test (the six-month look-back, unemployment included, Social Security excluded), and which is business income that DEW may require you to report.
4. **Protect the floor first.** All three frameworks agree on stabilizing basics: Ramsey's Four Walls, the CFPB's point that a reputable counselor never advises you to stop paying, and the FTC's warning against upfront fees. A $1,000 starter fund, a six-month business reserve for a solo operator, and a plan for taxes come before optimizing rates.
5. **Test consolidation against five conditions.** Income covers the new payment with margin; all-in APR is clearly below the blended current rate (use APR to include fees); the term does not stretch so far that total interest rises; the cards are left unused (and the spending gap is closed); the loan stays unsecured. If the only offers fail any of these, the evidence suggests consolidation is the wrong tool.
6. **Compare against a DMP before taking a loan.** SC-licensed agencies must show you can meet the plan, charge capped fees, and let you cancel on ten days' notice; NFCC says rates can fall to 10% or less, and one provider's marketing claims an average of 6.6% (neither is independently verified).
7. **If the debt is unaffordable on realistic income, price bankruptcy honestly.** Run the means test from a six-month income average, price the exemptions against what you own (homestead, vehicle, household goods, cash), and weigh the credit shadow (up to 10 years on the report, though effects on scores fade with time and the research did not source recovery times). Compare that with the settlement path (taxable forgiven debt unless insolvent, lawsuit risk) and the do-nothing path (judgments, liens, calls, credit damage, and the revival risk if you make small payments on old debt).
8. **Avoid the scams and the false economies.** No upfront fees, no guarantees, no unsolicited offers; do not borrow from a 401(k) if a job change is plausible; do not pull equity from a home you may need to protect; and do not make a small payment on an old debt without checking whether it restarts the clock.

## Questions to ask each professional

For a **South Carolina bankruptcy attorney**: Do I pass the means test with my last six months of income, and how does unemployment compensation count? Which of my assets are within the current (July 1, 2026) South Carolina exemption caps, and what are those caps? What happens to my car and home if I surrender versus keep them, and what does reaffirmation involve? Would paying a particular creditor, taking a new loan, or making a payment before filing create preference or fraud problems? Which of my debts will not be discharged? What is your fee, what does it include, and can it be paid in installments? Chapter 7 or Chapter 13, and why?

For a **nonprofit credit counselor** (check the DOJ approved list and SCDCA license): Are you SCDCA licensed, and what are the exact fees (setup, monthly) against the state caps? Will my creditors actually reduce rates, and what do I need to cancel? Does the plan require closing cards, and what happens to utilization? Can you provide a written budget analysis showing the plan fits my income?

For a **lender or consolidation offer**: What is the APR with fees included? Is there a prepayment penalty? Is the loan secured, and by what? What happens to the rate after the promotional period? Will it appear as a hard inquiry before I accept? Is the offer contingent on me stopping payments to my creditors?

For a **tax professional**: How would a settlement, bankruptcy discharge, or 401(k) distribution affect this year's return? Do I qualify for the insolvency exclusion and Form 982? How should I handle estimated payments on business profit while receiving unemployment?

For **DEW**: Does starting or operating my LLC, even without income, need to be reported? Are expenses counted? How are partial benefits computed against self-employment profit?

## How the business side (Part 3) ties back to the debt decision

A business can undermine a debt plan in three ways: personally guaranteed business debt, owner draws that outrun profit, and commingled accounts. Ramsey's EntreLeadership rules (no borrowing, separate accounts, a six-month reserve, a tax set-aside) are consistent with what personal-debt triage needs. Two cautions. First, any business income affects the means test calculation, exempt-income analysis, and unemployment eligibility, and the interaction is fact-specific. Second, the Kauffman evidence that founders routinely use personal credit cards and home equity means a solo LLC's personal and business debt can be hard to separate. For anyone weighing bankruptcy while running an LLC, how the LLC's debts, assets and income are treated is exactly the sort of question that a bankruptcy attorney, not this report, can answer.
