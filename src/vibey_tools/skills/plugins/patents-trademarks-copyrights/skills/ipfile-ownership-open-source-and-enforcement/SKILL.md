# US Patents, Trademarks and Copyrights: Filing and Maintenance: Part 4: Cross-Cutting Issues

> **Part 5 of 7** of the *US Patents, Trademarks and Copyrights: Filing and Maintenance* reference (plugin `patents-trademarks-copyrights`), covering Part 4 (§4.1–§4.7: ownership, open source, AI-assisted code, trade secrets, international, enforcement, licensing). Sibling skills: `ipfile-overview-and-decision-map` (the executive summary and the one-page decision map (Part 0)); `ipfile-trademarks` (Part 1 (§1.1–§1.13: clearance through maintenance)); `ipfile-copyrights` (Part 2 (§2.1–§2.14: registration portal, deposits, fees, DMCA)); `ipfile-patents` (Part 3 (§3.1–§3.13: documentation, search, provisional, nonprovisional, prosecution, maintenance)); `ipfile-budgets-deadlines-scams-and-free-help` (Part 5 (§5.1–§5.5: budget scenarios, a 12-month roadmap, the master deadline table, scam spotting, free and low-cost help)); `ipfile-open-questions-and-glossary` (Part 6 (§6.1–§6.4: unresolved source conflicts, unverified items, what to re-check on filing day) and the glossary).
>
> **Currency:** current as of **October 8, 2026**. Fee schedules, processing times and USPTO or Copyright Office procedures change; every dollar figure carries a source, and the official page to re-check before you pay is named where it matters (`ipfile-open-questions-and-glossary`, §6.4).

> **⚠️ Scope.** General information, **not legal advice**, and it creates no attorney-client relationship. A trademark filed by the wrong owner is void, a missed deadline is expensive to forgive, and publishing code before a patent filing starts a clock: **consult a trademark, copyright or patent attorney or agent before any filing that matters** (each Part says when one is worth paying).

> **The key ideas:**
> 1. **Ownership mistakes are the most expensive silent errors.** Copyright and patent rights in contractor work do not move to an LLC without a signed written assignment: fix ownership before any filing.
> 2. **Enforcement is where the money goes:** the secondary-reported AIPLA 2025 survey puts patent litigation medians at $600,000 to $1 million for mid-size cases.
> 3. **Trade secret needs no filing** but only protects non-public know-how kept under reasonable secrecy measures; anything you publish is out.

## Part 4: Cross-cutting issues (ownership, open source and AI, trade secrets, international, enforcement, licensing)

### 4.1 Ownership and assignments (do this before any filing)

| Situation | Default rule | What to sign |
|---|---|---|
| Employee writes code in the scope of employment | Copyright vests in the employer as a work made for hire | Employment agreement with confidentiality and invention-assignment clauses |
| Independent contractor writes code | Contractor owns the copyright unless the work falls in one of nine statutory categories and there is a signed writing; commissioned software rarely fits ([17 USC 101](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/101); *CCNV v. Reid*) | Present-tense written assignment ([17 USC 204](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/204)) |
| Founder wrote code or conceived an invention before the LLC existed | Founder owns it | Assignment from founder to LLC, signed and dated |
| Inventor and patent rights | The inventor owns the invention until assigned in writing | Present assignment ("hereby assigns"), recorded at the USPTO |
| Trademark | Belongs to whoever controls the nature and quality of the goods or services under the mark | File in the owner's name (Part 1, 1.4) |

- **"Hereby assigns" matters.** *Stanford v. Roche* (2011) is the notes' illustration that a promise to assign in the future can lose to a present assignment by someone else ([Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/publications/2011/06/inside-stanford-v-roche)). Use present-tense language in contractor and employee agreements.
- **Employers and moonlighting.** If you or a co-founder works for another company, read the invention-assignment clause before building. Broad clauses can reach side projects, and the notes did not verify how South Carolina courts treat every variant.
- **South Carolina law.** *Milliken & Co. v. Morin* (S.C. 2012) upheld a one-year holdover clause (inventions made within a year after employment that relate to the employer's business are assigned to the employer) and a confidentiality clause ([Parker Poe](https://www.parkerpoe.com/news/2012/09/south-carolina-supreme-court-upholds-confidentiality-and-inventions); [Maynard Nexsen](https://www.maynardnexsen.com/insights/sc-supreme-court-upholds-confidentiality-and-holdover-inventions-assignment-clauses-in-employment-agreement)). An earlier SC decision enforced an assignment agreement but refused part of a broader remedy ([Parker Poe 2009](https://www.parkerpoe.com/news/2009/12/south-carolina-enforces-invention-assignment-agreement-but-refuses)). Treat these as holdings on their own facts.
- **Checklist.** (1) LLC operating agreement stating who holds IP. (2) Founder IP assignment to the LLC. (3) Contractor agreements with present assignment, moral-rights waiver where relevant, third-party and open-source code disclosure, and confidentiality. (4) Employee agreements with an SC-enforceable holdover clause drafted by counsel. (5) Record trademark and patent assignments; recording a trademark assignment has a fee and rules ([Oppedahl](https://blog.oppedahl.com/why-it-costs-money-to-record-a-trademark-assignment-at-uspto/)).
- **Contributors to an open-source project.** Without a license or a contributor agreement, each contributor owns their code. The Developer Certificate of Origin (DCO 1.1, [developercertificate.org](https://www.developercertificate.org/)) is a lightweight sign-off certifying the right to submit; the notes flagged clause (c) wording as one to recheck. A Contributor License Agreement (CLA, individual ICLA or corporate CCLA) grants broader rights, including the ability to relicense or to grant patent licenses, which matters if you ever plan a license change or a dual-license model. Re-licensing a project later is only possible for code you own or have a CLA for, which is why the 2023 to 2026 relicensing wave (below) was easier for single-vendor projects.

### 4.2 Open source: licenses, patents, trademarks and enforcement

**Choosing a license.** Permissive licenses (MIT, BSD, Apache 2.0) maximize adoption. Copyleft licenses (GPL, AGPL, MPL) require sharing changes under defined conditions. Patent clauses differ: Apache 2.0, GPLv3 and MPL 2.0 include express patent grants from contributors (and terminate them if you sue); MIT and BSD do not state one ([Apache 2.0](https://www.apache.org/licenses/LICENSE-2.0.txt); [MPL 2.0](https://www.mozilla.org/en-US/MPL/2.0/); [swpat.org](https://swpat.org/wiki/Patent_clauses_in_software_licenses)). If you want patent protection for yourself, plan to file before you publish; the license does not give you a patent.

**The source-available wave (2023 to 2026).** The notes document companies moving away from OSI-approved licenses to Business Source License (BUSL, [SPDX](https://spdx.org/licenses/BUSL-1.1.html)), Server Side Public License, Functional Source License ([FSL](https://fsl.software/); [SPDX](https://spdx.github.io/license-list-data/FSL-1.1-ALv2.html)) and Elastic License 2.0, and some partially reversing: Elastic re-added AGPL ([Elastic](https://www.elastic.co/blog/elasticsearch-is-open-source-again)) and Redis announced AGPLv3 ([Redis](https://redis.io/blog/agplv3/)). Sentry moved to a "fair source" model ([Sentry](https://blog.sentry.io/sentry-is-now-fair-source/)). The practical lesson: license changes are a business decision with community cost, and they need the copyright ownership in 4.1.

**Defensive patent networks.** The Open Invention Network (OIN) offers a cross-license for Linux-system software, free for small companies under $10 million revenue per the notes ([OIN participation fees](https://openinventionnetwork.com/oin-2-0/participation-fees/)); the LOT Network is free for companies under $25 million in revenue ([LOT pricing](https://lotnet.com/lot-network-pricing/)); the Defensive Patent License ([DPL 1.1](https://defensivepatentlicense.org/s/DPL-11.pdf)) and Google's Open Patent Non-Assertion pledge ([Google OPN](https://www.google.com/patents/opnpledge/)) are alternatives. These are fit-for-purpose tools for a developer who will not enforce patents.

**Trademark policy for open-source projects.** Open-source licenses do not grant trademark rights. Mozilla, Rust and Docker publish trademark policies that separate the code license from the name ([Mozilla](https://www.mozilla.org/en-US/foundation/trademarks/policy/); [Rust Foundation](https://rustfoundation.org/policy/rust-trademark-policy/); [Docker](https://www.docker.com/legal/trademark-guidelines/)). GitHub, npm and PyPI have trademark or name-dispute processes ([GitHub trademark policy](https://docs.github.com/en/site-policy/content-removal-policies/github-trademark-policy); [npm disputes](https://github.com/npm/documentation/blob/main/content/policies/disputes.mdx); [PEP 541](https://peps.python.org/pep-0541/)). *WP Engine v. Automattic* is a reported dispute over use of WordPress marks, and the notes rely on a single outlet for a September 2026 ruling that Automattic was not an owner of the WordPress marks it asserted; treat that as reported, not verified ([Webhosting Today](https://webhosting.today/2026/09/29/automattic-is-not-an-owner-of-the-wordpress-marks-it-asserted-in-court-a-judge-rules/)).

**Enforcement cases.** *SFC v. Vizio* (consumer enforcement of GPL source-code obligations, trial delayed per SFC; [SFC](https://sfconservancy.org/copyleft-compliance/vizio.html)) and *Artifex v. Hancom* are the notes' examples that open-source license terms can be enforced in court. In *Doe v. GitHub* the Ninth Circuit ruled on September 16, 2026, in an opinion the notes summarize as affirming dismissal of DMCA 1202(b) claims about Copilot output ([opinion](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/24-7700/24-7700-2026-09-16.html)); read it before relying on that summary.

**EU Cyber Resilience Act.** Manufacturers' reporting obligations started September 11, 2026; the notes describe "open-source software steward" provisions that lightly regulate non-commercial stewards, but they leave commercial releases of open-source components in scope ([European Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/cra-reporting); [OpenSSF guide](https://openssf.org/blog/2026/09/11/a-community-guide-to-the-eu-cra-september-11-deadline-for-manufacturers/)). If your LLC sells into the EU, check this.

**Hygiene checklist.** SPDX license identifiers in every file header ([SPDX](https://spdx.org/ids)); REUSE compliance ([REUSE](https://reuse.software/tutorial/)); a CONTRIBUTING file with DCO or CLA; a trademark policy; a SECURITY contact; a record of third-party licenses; a fiscal host if you accept donations ([Open Collective fiscal hosts](https://documentation.opencollective.com/fiscal-hosts/fiscal-hosts); [SFC](https://sfconservancy.org/projects/apply/)).

### 4.3 AI-assisted code and training data

- Copyright in AI-assisted code covers only your human contribution (Part 2, 2.5). Prompts alone were found insufficient in the Office's Part 2 report ([IPWatchdog](https://ipwatchdog.com/2025/01/29/part-two-copyright-office-ai-report-says-creative-prompting-doesnt-constitute-authorship/)). Keep version-control history showing your modifications.
- Patent inventorship needs a human who conceived the claimed invention (Part 3, 3.2).
- The Office's Part 3 report on generative-AI training exists in a pre-publication version ([Part 3](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-3-Generative-AI-Training-Report-Pre-Publication-Version.pdf)); the notes also reference a dispute over the Register of Copyrights position (*Perlmutter*). Do not rely on either as settled law.
- If you use AI tools trained on others' code, license risk is unsettled; the notes record the *Doe v. GitHub* litigation as one example. A scan tool and attention to output that reproduces recognizable code are practical safeguards.

### 4.4 Trade secrets as an alternative or companion

- Federal law (the Defend Trade Secrets Act, [18 USC 1836](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1836)) and the South Carolina Trade Secrets Act (S.C. Code 39-8-10 et seq., [statute](https://scstatehouse.gov/code/t39c008.php); [39-8-30](https://law.justia.com/codes/south-carolina/title-39/chapter-8/section-39-8-30)) protect information that has independent economic value from secrecy and is subject to reasonable secrecy efforts. The SC Act has a three-year limitations period per the notes.
- For software, server-side code, model weights, training data, tuning parameters and unpublished algorithms fit trade-secret protection; anything shipped as open source or readable client code does not.
- Reasonable measures: NDAs, access controls, contractor assignment and confidentiality, exit procedures, and not putting the secret in a patent (a nonprovisional publishes at 18 months). A copyright deposit with redaction (Part 2, 2.6) can register code without exposing secrets.
- A trade secret has no registration, no term, and no protection against independent discovery or reverse engineering; the choice between patent and trade secret is largely a question of whether the invention can be reverse-engineered from the product.

### 4.6 Enforcement: options and realistic costs

1. **Start with the cheapest tool.** Platform notices (Etsy, eBay VeRO, Amazon Brand Registry and IP Accelerator, GitHub DMCA) cost nothing or little; the Amazon IP Accelerator vendor caps in the notes are $700/$650/$1,800 across packages ([Amazon IP Accelerator](https://sell.amazon.com/programs/ip-accelerator); [Etsy](https://www.etsy.com/legal/ip-dmca/)).
2. **Cease-and-desist letters carry risk.** A letter implying a patent threat can create standing for a declaratory-judgment action (*MedImmune*), letting the recipient pick the forum ([Finnegan](https://www.finnegan.com/en/insights/articles/walking-the-tightrope-of-cease-and-desist-letters.html)).
3. **Domain disputes.** UDRP filing fees are $1,500 (one panelist, WIPO) and $4,000 (three panelists), Forum about $1,330 for one to two domains and up ([WIPO AMC](https://www.wipo.int/amc/en/domains/fees/); [Forum](https://www.adrforum.com/assets/resources/domain/udrp/UDRP%20Fee%20Schedule%20Final.pdf)); the Anticybersquatting Consumer Protection Act is the court alternative ([15 USC 1125](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/1125)).
4. **Customs.** Record a registered trademark with CBP at $190 per class (copyrights similar), $80 per renewal ([CBP](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/priority-issues/ipr/protection)).
5. **Small claims.** The CCB (Part 2, 2.11) handles smaller copyright claims.
6. **Litigation.** The AIPLA 2025 survey, reported second-hand through IPWatchdog and Lumenci, puts median patent litigation costs at about $600,000 through trial for $1 million to $10 million at risk and about $1 million for bigger cases ([IPWatchdog](https://ipwatchdog.com/2026/10/01/the-true-cost-of-patent-infringement-litigation-enforcement-and-defense-in-a-complex-market/); [Lumenci](https://lumenci.com/blogs/patent-litigation-costs-2026/)). The original AIPLA report is paywalled, so these numbers are secondary. PTAB inter partes review fees alone are $23,750 plus $28,125 per the notes, and the median IPR total is about $355,000. A "litigation is not a viable revenue source for most inventors" commentary is available ([Hutter Group](https://thehuttergroup.com/a-patent-reality-check-litigation-not-a-viable-revenue-source-for-most-inventors/)).
7. **Fee shifting** in exceptional cases cuts both ways; the notes cite examples but treat them as case-specific.

### 4.7 Licensing and monetization

- Royalty benchmarks in the notes are broad and not specific to you: the LES 2021 survey median 4.75% (2017: 5%) ([LES 2021 summary](https://cdn.ymaws.com/members.lesusacanada.org/resource/collection/D8145BC5-DBCC-4F8F-8B68-593C0B0D4776/2021_Royalty_Rate_&_Deal_Terms_Executive_Summary.pdf)); an SEC-filings study median 5%, software 10% ([LES Nouvelles](https://lesi.org/les-nouvelles-articl/technology-royalty-rates-in-sec-filings/)). Trademark royalty rates were not verified.
- Inventor commercialization data is thin: one study cited in the notes found about 6.5% of independent inventors earn back costs ([Astebro](https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/entthe/v23y1998i2p41-48.html)). Treat patent income as unlikely for most solo developers.
- Trademark licensors must control quality or risk "naked licensing" abandonment (*Barcamerica v. Tuscany*, [Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/289/589/483677/)). Open-source trademark policies (4.2) are the practical expression of that duty.
- Patent assertion entities are a risk and not a revenue plan ([FTC PAE study](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2016/10/ftc-report-sheds-new-light-how-patent-assertion-entities-operate-recommends-patent-litigation)).
