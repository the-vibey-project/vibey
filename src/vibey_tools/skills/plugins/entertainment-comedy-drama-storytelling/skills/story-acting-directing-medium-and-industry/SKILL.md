## §20. Acting

```
STANISLAVSKI       ⚠️ the root system. Objectives, obstacles, given
   circumstances, emotional memory. ⚠️ "What do I want, what's in my way"
   — the same question as §5
METHOD (Strasberg)  ⚠️ emotional recall. Influential, controversial, and
   ⚠️ the popular "stayed in character for months" version is a distortion
   of the technique and is not what it requires
MEISNER            ⚠️ repetition; "living truthfully under imaginary
   circumstances." Focus OUTWARD on the scene partner
ADLER              imagination over personal memory
CHEKHOV            psychological gesture; physical approach
PRACTICAL AESTHETICS  action-based, anti-emotionalist (Mamet)
BRECHT             ⚠️ deliberately ANTI-immersive; the alienation effect
```
**⚠️ The common thread across all of them: playing an ACTION ("to persuade," "to
humiliate") works better than playing an EMOTION ("be sad").** ⚠️ **Emotion is a
by-product of pursuing something; playing it directly produces indicating.**
**⚠️ Screen vs stage acting differs mechanically**: ⚠️ **the camera reads thought, so screen
performance is smaller and more interior; stage must reach the back row, so it's larger
and more sustained.** **⚠️ Continuity of performance across out-of-order shooting is a
screen-specific technical demand with no stage equivalent.**

## §21. Directing

**⚠️ The director's actual job is making choices coherent across every department** —
**performance, camera, design, sound, edit** — **so that they express one interpretation.**
**⚠️ Blocking is storytelling**: ⚠️ **where people stand and move expresses relationship
and power before a word is spoken**, **and this is the most under-taught directing skill.**
**⚠️ Camera as POV**: **shot size controls intimacy, height controls power, movement
controls attention, lens controls the relationship between subject and space.**
**⚠️ Coverage vs designed shooting**: **shooting everything from every angle buys safety in
the edit and costs time and intentionality; ⚠️ knowing precisely what you need is faster
and riskier.**
**⚠️ Working with actors is mostly ASKING rather than telling** — ⚠️ **result-oriented
direction ("be angrier") is generally less effective than actionable direction ("try it
as though you're trying not to let her see you care").**

## §22. Medium Differences

```
⚠️ STAGE      live, present tense, ⚠️ language-forward. The audience chooses
   where to look. Unity of space is a constraint AND a resource
⚠️ FILM       ⚠️ the camera controls attention. Visual, compressed, single
   sitting. ⚠️ Interiority must be externalized (§8)
⚠️ TELEVISION ⚠️ character-forward and duration-forward. Serialized TV can
   develop character over dozens of hours — an advantage film cannot match.
   ⚠️ WRITER-led, unlike film's director-led tradition
⚠️ PROSE      ⚠️ the only form with direct access to consciousness. Can do
   psychic distance (§8), free indirect discourse, and interiority natively
⚠️ GAMES      ⚠️ agency changes everything. The player acts, so authored
   arc conflicts with freedom — the "ludonarrative dissonance" problem.
   Environmental storytelling and systemic narrative are native strengths
⚠️ AUDIO/PODCAST  ⚠️ intimate, imagination-driven; ⚠️ must carry everything
   through voice and sound design, with no visual disambiguation
SHORT FORM    ⚠️ vertical video demands the hook in seconds; structure is
   compressed to setup-payoff with minimal development
```
**⚠️ Adaptation is mostly about what the new medium can and cannot do** — ⚠️ **the common
failure is transposing plot faithfully while losing the mechanism that made it work in the
original form.** **Interior novels adapt badly by default; plot-driven ones adapt well.**

## §23. Editing and Rhythm

**⚠️ The edit is where screen storytelling is finally authored, and it can rescue or
destroy everything upstream.**
**⚠️ Continuity conventions** (⚠️ **180-degree rule, eyeline match, match on action,
30-degree rule**) **exist to make cuts invisible — and ⚠️ they're conventions, deliberately
broken for effect.**
**Montage and the Kuleshov effect** — ⚠️ **meaning generated by juxtaposition rather than
by shot content, which is one of the genuinely foundational discoveries about the medium.**
**⚠️ Pace and rhythm**: ⚠️ **cutting rate is an expressive tool, not a default** — **and
average shot length has fallen substantially across decades of mainstream film, which is a
measurable stylistic shift rather than a myth.**
**⚠️ Sound is half of picture and gets a fraction of the attention** — **⚠️ audiences
tolerate poor image far better than poor sound, which is the single most useful thing for
a low-budget filmmaker to know.**

## §24. Development and Economics

```
⚠️ THE FUNNEL  idea → pitch → option/deal → script → development →
   greenlight → production → post → distribution
   ⚠️ Attrition is brutal at every stage; most projects die in development
COVERAGE       ⚠️ readers' reports (recommend/consider/pass). The first gate
PACKAGING      ⚠️ script + writer + talent attached. Reduces perceived risk
NOTES          ⚠️ the recurring truth: notes usually identify a REAL problem
   and usually propose the WRONG solution. Diagnose, don't just comply
```
**⚠️ The economics are power-law, and this drives everything else:**
- ⚠️ **A small number of titles produce most of the revenue; most lose money.**
  **Which is why studios pursue IP, franchises and stars — they're risk-reduction
  instruments, not creative preferences.**
- ⚠️ **"Nobody knows anything" (Goldman) remains the honest summary**: **outcome
  prediction is genuinely poor, and everyone acts to be defensible when wrong.**
- ⚠️ **Marketing spend is frequently comparable to production budget** for wide releases.
- **⚠️ Backend participation is notoriously unreliable** — **"Hollywood accounting" and the
  disappearance of net profits is well documented** — **and ⚠️ the streaming model largely
  replaced backend with higher upfront fees, which changed writer and performer economics
  structurally** (§25.1 → `story-reference`).
