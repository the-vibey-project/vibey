## §7. Dialogue

**⚠️ Good dialogue is not how people actually talk** — ⚠️ **real speech is repetitive,
digressive and full of filler.** **Good dialogue is compressed speech that reads as
natural.**
**⚠️ What it does simultaneously**: **reveals character, advances plot, carries subtext,
and establishes voice.** ⚠️ **Dialogue doing only one of those is usually cuttable.**
**⚠️ Subtext is the main technique**: **characters rarely say what they want directly.**
⚠️ **A scene where everyone states their feelings accurately is called "on the nose" and
it's the most common dialogue failure.**
**⚠️ Voice differentiation**: ⚠️ **cover the character names and see whether you can still
tell who's speaking.** **If not, everyone is speaking in the author's voice.**
**⚠️ Practical**: **characters interrupt, evade, change subject, and answer questions with
questions.** **⚠️ Silence and refusal to answer are dialogue.** **And ⚠️ dialect rendered
phonetically is generally a mistake — rhythm and word choice convey it better than
misspelling does.**

## §8. Point of View and Narration

```
FIRST PERSON      intimacy; ⚠️ limited to what the narrator knows —
                  and enables the UNRELIABLE NARRATOR
THIRD LIMITED     the modern default in prose
THIRD OMNISCIENT  ⚠️ out of fashion, and capable of things nothing else does
SECOND PERSON     rare; ⚠️ standard in interactive fiction and games
⚠️ CAMERA (screen)  external by default. ⚠️ Interiority must be EXTERNALIZED —
                  which is the fundamental adaptation problem (§22)
```
**⚠️ Psychic distance** (Gardner) — **how close the narration sits to the character's
consciousness, and it can slide within a paragraph.** ⚠️ **Controlling it deliberately is
one of the more advanced prose skills.**
**⚠️ Free indirect discourse** — **third person that takes on the character's idiom and
judgements without quotation** — ⚠️ **is the workhorse of literary fiction and is nearly
invisible when done well.**

## §9. Theme

**⚠️ Theme is what the story is ABOUT underneath what happens, and it is discovered more
often than planned.**
⚠️ **Stated thematically-explicit dialogue ("this is a story about forgiveness") is almost
always worse than dramatizing it.**
**⚠️ The practical construction: theme emerges from the ARGUMENT between characters who
embody different answers to the same question.** ⚠️ **If every character agrees with the
theme, you have a sermon rather than a drama** — **and the antagonist should have the best
available counter-argument** (§3 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`).
**⚠️ Motif and image systems** carry theme non-verbally, and **⚠️ the ending is where theme
resolves** (§11) — **what the story finally endorses is what it lets the protagonist get
away with.**

## §10. ⚠️ Exposition

**⚠️ The hardest technical problem in dramatic writing: the audience needs information,
and delivering it is inherently undramatic.**
```
⚠️ WORST      "As you know, Bob..." — characters telling each other things
              they both already know
⚠️ BETTER     deliver it during CONFLICT, when the audience is distracted
              by wanting to know what happens
⚠️ BETTER     make someone WANT the information — an outsider who must ask
⚠️ BETTER     show rather than state
⚠️ BEST       withhold it. ⚠️ Most exposition is unnecessary; audiences
              tolerate confusion far longer than writers believe, and
              catching up is pleasurable
```
**⚠️ The core principle: give the audience information at the LATEST possible moment they
still need it.** ⚠️ **Front-loaded exposition is the most common cause of slow openings**,
**and the usual fix is to cut the first scene or two entirely** (§5 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`'s "enter late").

## §11. Endings

**⚠️ The ending is what the whole thing was for, and it retroactively determines the
meaning of everything before it.**
**⚠️ "Surprising yet inevitable" is the standard** — ⚠️ **and the inevitability comes from
SETUP, not from predictability.** **Whatever pays off must have been planted, or the
ending feels arbitrary; whatever is planted too obviously makes the ending feel
telegraphed.** **⚠️ Deus ex machina fails because the resolution doesn't come from the
protagonist's agency** (§3 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`).
**⚠️ The ending should answer the DRAMATIC QUESTION posed at the start** — **and the
answer can be "no" or "yes, but at a cost."**
**⚠️ Ambiguity is legitimate and is not the same as being unresolved**: ⚠️ **an ambiguous
ending answers the emotional question and leaves the factual one open.** **An unresolved
ending answers neither and reads as a failure of nerve.**
**⚠️ Denouement length is genre-dependent**: **⚠️ modern screen drama typically ends fast
after the climax; prose and epic fantasy tolerate much more.**
