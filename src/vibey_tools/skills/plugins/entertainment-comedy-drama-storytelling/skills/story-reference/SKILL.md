# Storytelling and Performance: What's Live, Misconceptions, Books, and Numbers

> **Part 5 of 5** of the *Entertainment, Comedy, Drama and Storytelling* reference (plugin `entertainment-comedy-drama-storytelling`), covering §25–§30. Sibling skills: `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene` (§0–§6), `story-dialogue-point-of-view-theme-exposition-and-endings` (§7–§11), `story-comedy-jokes-improv-standup-and-timing` (§12–§19), `story-acting-directing-medium-and-industry` (§20–§24). Section numbers are shared across the set; a reference written as §N → `skill` points into that sibling skill.
>
> **Currency:** The craft is ancient and stable. Two areas are live. See §25 for the production contraction, and AI in film and television.

> **⚠️ A craft domain with an unusually loud rules industry.** **The techniques are real
> and largely teachable; the "rules" sold alongside them are mostly heuristics with a
> decent hit rate, presented as laws.**
>
> **Complements a prose/writing-craft reference (sentence-level mechanics), an art theory
> reference (§1's register distinction), and a business reference (§24's economics).**
>
> **⚠️ GOTCHA** boxes mark rules that are actually conventions, and claims the evidence
> or the practice contradicts.
>
> **The three ideas that organize this document:**
> 1. **⚠️ Structure models are DESCRIPTIONS of what has worked, retrofitted** (§1 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`, §4 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`).
>    **They're useful diagnostic tools and terrible generative recipes, and the industry
>    routinely reverses that.**
> 2. **⚠️ Drama is desire meeting obstacle; comedy is expectation meeting violation**
>    (§3 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`, §12 → `story-comedy-jokes-improv-standup-and-timing`). **Almost every technique in both halves descends from one of those two
>    sentences.**
> 3. **⚠️ The unit of craft is the SCENE, not the outline** (§5 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`). **Most story problems
>    presented as structure problems are scene problems — nobody wants anything
>    specific enough.**

## §25. What's Live — verified August 2026

### 25.1 ⚠️ The contraction: Peak TV ended and did not come back
**⚠️ This is the structural fact anyone entering or working in the industry needs, and it
is measured rather than vibes.**

- **⚠️ Scripted series orders by US distributors went from a peak of 759 in 2021 to roughly
  478–493 per year across 2023, 2024 and 2025** — ⚠️ **about a 36% decline, described as
  content commissioners finding "a new magic number for stability."** **FX's John Landgraf,
  who coined "Peak TV," dated its end to 2023 and noted the decline began before the
  strikes accelerated it.**
- **⚠️ The labour impact was severe and disproportionate.** **The WGA reported TV writer
  jobs falling 42% in the 2023–24 season — 1,319 positions — to 1,819 total**, ⚠️ **with
  declines of roughly 40–46% at every level from staff writer to co-executive producer.**
- **⚠️ The budget distribution barbelled, which matters more than the headline count.**
  **$20M+ per-episode budgets — once unheard of in television — became standard for
  franchise and genre series.** ⚠️ **Meanwhile sub-$5M-per-episode series fell from 82% of
  US scripted releases to under two-thirds by 2025, and shows under $1M per episode "have
  all but disappeared."**
- **⚠️ Geographic concentration shifted**: **40% of US scripted series released in Q1 2019
  filmed in Los Angeles, versus under 25% in Q1 2026** — **driven both by relocation and by
  the overall contraction.** ⚠️ **US production share showed signs of stabilizing in Q1
  2026 following California's expanded tax credit.**

> **⚠️ GOTCHA — the craft consequence, which is the part that belongs in a storytelling
> reference.** ⚠️ **The middle disappeared.** **The lower-cost shows that filled slates
> during Peak TV were where writers learned, where unusual premises got made, and where
> steady between-seasons employment lived.** **⚠️ What remains is expensive franchise work
> and very little beneath it.**
> ⚠️ **One executive's description of the buyer environment: roughly four buyers for a
> given show, more hyperspecific parameters, and windows for a particular kind of material
> that "come and go in just weeks, or even days."** **⚠️ Consolidation compounds it — when
> the buyer is also the producer and the distributor, small strategy shifts propagate.**

**⚠️ Worth noting against the doom framing**: **an analysis of IMDb ratings suggests series
acclaim has continued rising** — ⚠️ **the drop in "buzzy" shows appears to stem from
production VOLUME rather than declining quality.** **And Ampere's analyst view is that the
cutback has bottomed, pending further consolidation.**

### 25.2 ⚠️ AI: the guardrails landed, and they differ sharply by guild
**⚠️ 2026 was the year the labour framework actually got written, and the union-by-union
divergence is the instructive part.**

- **⚠️ SAG-AFTRA ratified a four-year TV/Theatrical Agreement in June 2026** (**91.4% in
  favour, 19.3% turnout; effective 1 July 2026 through 30 June 2030**), **with over $1
  billion in new compensation and benefits funding and a pension plan merger.**
- **⚠️ The AI provisions are specific.** **Producers must have an "articulable business
  reason" to scan a performer; ⚠️ producers may not use synthetic performers without first
  bargaining with SAG-AFTRA, under a strict notice-and-bargaining schedule, with
  arbitration for damages on violation.** ⚠️ **Producers may use AI performers only where
  they bring "significant additional value" compared to a live actor.** **Voice work gained
  separate written-consent requirements, and the Interactive agreement now defines "voice
  actor" as a HUMAN performer.**
- **⚠️ The WGA's April 2026 deal was notably weaker on AI**: **studios agreed to notify the
  guild if they license writers' work for AI training and to hold consultation meetings,**
  ⚠️ **but with no requirement to pay writers when scripts are used to train models** —
  **reportedly trading AI enforcement for a $321M health plan infusion.** **⚠️ Compare the
  2023 WGA precedent that AI training on creative work is compensable; the 2026 outcome
  did not extend it.**
- **⚠️ The DGA's 2023 contract was the first to bar studios from delegating directors'
  creative work to generative AI**, **and its renewal was in negotiation with AI central.**

> **⚠️ GOTCHA — read the criticism as well as the terms, because it's substantive.**
> ⚠️ **Critics inside SAG-AFTRA argue studios still face little real constraint on AI
> performers**, **and note that the union gets notice and an opportunity to bargain but
> ⚠️ cannot strike over synthetic performers until 2030** — **a four-year term instead of
> the usual three, agreed at a moment when AI capability is moving fast.** **The AMPTP's
> stated priority across this cycle was securing longer "labour peace."**
> ⚠️ **Note also that much protection comes not from contract language but from the union
> reserving the ability to establish protections as actual use cases arise** — **which is
> a bet on future bargaining rather than a settled rule.**

**⚠️ Where AI is actually being used, as distinct from the discourse**: ⚠️ **VFX and
animation pipelines, previsualization, frame interpolation, editing assistance and
background generation** — **the unglamorous end.** **⚠️ Reported effects on employment are
material in VFX and animation and more modest elsewhere so far.**
**⚠️ Legislative activity is running in parallel**: **a New York law requiring advertisements
to be labelled when synthetics are used, a NY bill requiring disclaimers for AI in
published news, and over 16,000 signatures on a SAG-AFTRA letter urging passage of the
federal NO FAKES Act.**

## §26. Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| Structure models tell you how to write | ⚠️ **They're retrofitted descriptions. Use for diagnosis** (§1 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`, §4 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`) |
| The hero's journey is universal | ⚠️ **Campbell was doing comparative mythology, fitted loosely** (§4 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`) |
| All drama is conflict | ⚠️ **Kishōtenketsu disproves it** (§4 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`) |
| Three-act structure is a rule | ⚠️ **So general it's nearly unfalsifiable** (§4 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`) |
| Story problems are structure problems | ⚠️ **Usually scene problems — nobody wants anything** (§5 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`) |
| Bigger stakes mean more tension | ⚠️ **Specific and personal beats large** (§6 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`) |
| Surprise creates tension | ⚠️ **Dramatic irony does. The bomb under the table** (§6 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`) |
| Good dialogue sounds like real speech | ⚠️ **Real speech is unusable. It's compressed** (§7 → `story-dialogue-point-of-view-theme-exposition-and-endings`) |
| Characters should say what they feel | ⚠️ **That's "on the nose." Subtext** (§7 → `story-dialogue-point-of-view-theme-exposition-and-endings`) |
| The audience needs the backstory up front | ⚠️ **Withhold. Late as possible** (§10 → `story-dialogue-point-of-view-theme-exposition-and-endings`) |
| Protagonists must be likeable | ⚠️ **They must be interesting and want something** (§3 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`) |
| Every protagonist needs a change arc | ⚠️ **Flat arcs transform the world instead** (§3 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`) |
| Comedy can't be taught | ⚠️ **It's the most empirically iterated craft there is** (§12 → `story-comedy-jokes-improv-standup-and-timing`, §17 → `story-comedy-jokes-improv-standup-and-timing`) |
| Random is funny | ⚠️ **No pattern to violate. Comedy needs internal logic** (§14 → `story-comedy-jokes-improv-standup-and-timing`) |
| Just be funny in the scene | ⚠️ **Find the game and play it** (§14 → `story-comedy-jokes-improv-standup-and-timing`, §16 → `story-comedy-jokes-improv-standup-and-timing`) |
| "Yes, and" means agree with everything | ⚠️ **Accept the established reality, then add** (§16 → `story-comedy-jokes-improv-standup-and-timing`) |
| Timing is innate | ⚠️ **It's rhythm, and it's learnable — and made in the edit** (§18 → `story-comedy-jokes-improv-standup-and-timing`, §23 → `story-acting-directing-medium-and-industry`) |
| The K sound is funnier | ⚠️ **Weakly evidenced folklore; rhythm is the real effect** (§13 → `story-comedy-jokes-improv-standup-and-timing`) |
| Method acting means staying in character | ⚠️ **A distortion of the technique** (§20 → `story-acting-directing-medium-and-industry`) |
| Direct actors by describing the emotion | ⚠️ **Give an action, not a result** (§20 → `story-acting-directing-medium-and-industry`, §21 → `story-acting-directing-medium-and-industry`) |
| Audiences forgive bad sound like bad picture | ⚠️ **They don't. Sound is half of picture** (§23 → `story-acting-directing-medium-and-industry`) |
| Notes from executives are wrong | ⚠️ **Usually the right problem, wrong solution** (§24 → `story-acting-directing-medium-and-industry`) |
| Someone knows what will succeed | ⚠️ **"Nobody knows anything." Power-law outcomes** (§24 → `story-acting-directing-medium-and-industry`) |
| Peak TV will come back | ⚠️ **~36% below the 2021 peak and stabilized there** (§25.1) |
| TV got worse | ⚠️ **Ratings suggest volume fell, not quality** (§25.1) |
| The guilds solved AI | ⚠️ **SAG-AFTRA got real terms; WGA got notification** (§25.2) |

## §29. Quick Reference

### 29.1 Picker
| Problem | Where |
|---|---|
| The story feels like a list of events | ⚠️ **"And then" vs "therefore/but"** (§2 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`) |
| The scene is flat | ⚠️ **What does each person want IN THIS SCENE?** (§5 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`) |
| The protagonist is boring | ⚠️ **They're passive. Give them agency** (§3 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`) |
| The opening is slow | ⚠️ **Front-loaded exposition. Cut the first scenes** (§10 → `story-dialogue-point-of-view-theme-exposition-and-endings`) |
| No tension | ⚠️ **Let the audience know something the characters don't** (§6 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`) |
| Dialogue is on the nose | ⚠️ **Subtext — nobody says what they want** (§7 → `story-dialogue-point-of-view-theme-exposition-and-endings`) |
| Everyone sounds the same | ⚠️ **Cover the names and test** (§7 → `story-dialogue-point-of-view-theme-exposition-and-endings`) |
| The ending doesn't land | ⚠️ **Setup, and protagonist agency in the resolution** (§11 → `story-dialogue-point-of-view-theme-exposition-and-endings`) |
| The joke doesn't work | ⚠️ **Funny word last; check the misdirection is honest** (§13 → `story-comedy-jokes-improv-standup-and-timing`) |
| The comic scene is just weird | ⚠️ **Find the game. Random isn't funny** (§14 → `story-comedy-jokes-improv-standup-and-timing`) |
| The comedy feels mean or misfires | ⚠️ **Check the target and the distance** (§12 → `story-comedy-jokes-improv-standup-and-timing`, §19 → `story-comedy-jokes-improv-standup-and-timing`) |
| The performance is indicating | ⚠️ **Play an action, not an emotion** (§20 → `story-acting-directing-medium-and-industry`) |
| The cut feels wrong | ⚠️ **Comedy timing is frames. Try trims either side** (§18 → `story-comedy-jokes-improv-standup-and-timing`, §23 → `story-acting-directing-medium-and-industry`) |
| Low budget — where to spend? | ⚠️ **Sound** (§23 → `story-acting-directing-medium-and-industry`) |
| Got contradictory notes | ⚠️ **Find the shared problem, ignore the proposed fixes** (§24 → `story-acting-directing-medium-and-industry`) |

### 29.2 Scene diagnostic
- [ ] ⚠️ **Someone wants something SPECIFIC in this scene** (§5 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`)
- [ ] ⚠️ **Something concrete is in the way** (§5 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`)
- [ ] ⚠️ **The scene TURNS — it ends somewhere different** (§5 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`)
- [ ] Entered as late as possible, left as early as possible (§5 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`)
- [ ] ⚠️ **What's said ≠ what's wanted** (§7 → `story-dialogue-point-of-view-theme-exposition-and-endings`)
- [ ] The audience knows something useful the characters don't (§6 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`)
- [ ] ⚠️ **Nothing here is explaining what the audience could infer** (§10 → `story-dialogue-point-of-view-theme-exposition-and-endings`)
- [ ] ⚠️ **Cut it and see if anything breaks** (§5 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`)

## §30. Method

**§1–§24 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`, `story-dialogue-point-of-view-theme-exposition-and-endings`, `story-comedy-jokes-improv-standup-and-timing`, `story-acting-directing-medium-and-industry` rest on craft tradition** — **Aristotle through the modern structure literature,
comedy theory, acting systems, and standard production practice** — sourced from §27.
⚠️ **None of it needed verification; *Poetics* is not going to move.**

**Two searches were run in August 2026**, on **the production contraction** and **AI in
film and television** — ⚠️ **the two things that materially changed the conditions under
which this craft is practised and paid for.**

**Confidence.** **High** in §1 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`, §5 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene` and §12 → `story-comedy-jokes-improv-standup-and-timing`, which are the load-bearing sections.
⚠️ **The "structure models are retrofitted descriptions" point is the one I'd defend
hardest** — **it's historically accurate, it explains the homogeneity complaint about
studio film, and it reframes the models from recipes into diagnostics, which is how
working writers actually use them.** **§5 → `story-rules-character-structure-and-scene`'s scene diagnostic is the highest-yield practical
tool here.**

**High** in §12 → `story-comedy-jokes-improv-standup-and-timing`'s benign violation framing. ⚠️ **It's the most predictive available theory
of humour, it explains failures in both directions, and the psychological-distance
mechanism accounts for why the same joke lands differently by audience and by year** —
**which is also what makes §19 → `story-comedy-jokes-improv-standup-and-timing` tractable as a craft question rather than only a political
one.**

**High** in §25.1's figures, which come from Ampere Analysis, Luminate and the WGA's own
reporting and recur consistently across trade coverage. ⚠️ **The interpretive emphasis is
mine: the disappearance of the sub-$5M-per-episode tier matters more for the craft than
the headline order count, because that tier was where writers trained and where unusual
material got made.** **I've also included the counterweight — that ratings analysis suggests
volume fell rather than quality — because the declinist framing is easy and partly wrong.**

**High** in §25.2's contract terms, which come from SAG-AFTRA's own materials, Variety and
The Hollywood Reporter. ⚠️ **The comparison is the useful part: SAG-AFTRA secured
bargaining rights, an "articulable business reason" standard for scanning, and a
"significant additional value" test for synthetic performers, while the WGA's April 2026
deal reportedly secured notification without compensation for AI training.** **I've
included the internal criticism — no strike right over synthetics until 2030, and much
protection resting on future bargaining rather than present language — because it's a
substantive objection made by members, not an outside complaint.**

⚠️ **§19 → `story-comedy-jokes-improv-standup-and-timing` is deliberately structured as competing positions rather than a verdict.** **The
craft mechanics (target, distance, ambiguity) are stateable; the normative question isn't
mine to settle, and I've given each side its strongest version including the complication
that "punching up" presupposes an agreed hierarchy.**
