> **⚠️ A craft domain with an unusually loud rules industry.** **The techniques are real
> and largely teachable; the "rules" sold alongside them are mostly heuristics with a
> decent hit rate, presented as laws.**
>
> **Complements a prose/writing-craft reference (sentence-level mechanics), an art theory

>
> **⚠️ GOTCHA** boxes mark rules that are actually conventions, and claims the evidence
> or the practice contradicts.
>
> **The three ideas that organize this document:**
> 1. **⚠️ Structure models are DESCRIPTIONS of what has worked, retrofitted** (§1, §4).
>    **They're useful diagnostic tools and terrible generative recipes, and the industry
>    routinely reverses that.**
> 2. **⚠️ Drama is desire meeting obstacle; comedy is expectation meeting violation**
>    (§3, §12 → `story-comedy-jokes-improv-standup-and-timing`). **Almost every technique in both halves descends from one of those two
>    sentences.**
> 3. **⚠️ The unit of craft is the SCENE, not the outline** (§5). **Most story problems
>    presented as structure problems are scene problems — nobody wants anything
>    specific enough.**

## §1. ⚠️ Rules, Heuristics and Descriptions

```
⚠️ CRAFT MECHANICS — reliable, teachable, near-objective in their effects
   ⚠️ Unclear stakes reduce tension. A scene with no opposition goes slack.
   Setups pay off better than surprises from nowhere. ⚠️ You can be WRONG here
⚠️ STRUCTURAL MODELS — descriptions retrofitted to successful work
   ⚠️ Three-act, hero's journey, Save the Cat beats. Useful for DIAGNOSIS.
   ⚠️ Dangerous as GENERATION — they produce competent, familiar, dead work
⚠️ TASTE — preference, not adjudicable
```
> **⚠️ GOTCHA — the survivorship problem in screenwriting advice is severe and rarely
> acknowledged.** ⚠️ **Structure models were derived by examining films that got made and
> succeeded.** **They cannot tell you what would have worked and wasn't made, and they
> systematically encode the conventions of a particular era and market.**
> **⚠️ The honest use: write the thing, then use the model to find where it sags.**
> **The dishonest use: fill in the beat sheet and expect a story.** **⚠️ The second
> produces the specific deadness that people describe as "feels like every other
> movie" — because it structurally is.**

**⚠️ A second caution: most screenwriting gurus have modest produced credits.** ⚠️ **The
teaching industry selects for people who can articulate a system, which is a different
skill from making things.** **The exceptions — working writers who also teach — are worth
weighting more heavily** (§27 → `story-reference`).

# PART I — STORY

## §2. What a Story Is

**⚠️ The minimum viable definition: somebody wants something, something stands in the way,
and by the end something has irreversibly changed.**
```
⚠️ CAUSALITY   "and then" is a list; "THEREFORE / BUT" is a story.
   ⚠️ The single most useful diagnostic there is — read your outline and
   insert the connective; if "and then" fits everywhere, you have episodes
   rather than a plot
⚠️ CHANGE      the state at the end differs irreversibly from the start
⚠️ MEANING     the change signifies something (§9)
```
**⚠️ Story vs plot** (Forster's distinction): **"the king died and then the queen died" is
story; "the king died and then the queen died OF GRIEF" is plot** — ⚠️ **causality is what
converts one into the other.**
**⚠️ Fabula and syuzhet** — **the events as they happened versus the order in which they're
told.** ⚠️ **Nearly all narrative sophistication lives in the gap between them:
flashback, dramatic irony, unreliable narration, mystery.**

## §3. Character and Desire

**⚠️ Character is revealed through CHOICE UNDER PRESSURE, not through description.**
⚠️ **Backstory, adjectives and quirks are not characterization; what someone does when it
costs them something is.**
```
⚠️ WANT (external, conscious)   the goal that drives plot
⚠️ NEED (internal, often unconscious)   what would actually make them whole
   ⚠️ The tension between them IS the character arc. When they conflict,
   the climax is usually the choice between them
⚠️ AGENCY   the character must ACT, not merely have things happen to them.
   ⚠️ The single most common failure in amateur work is a passive protagonist
STAKES      what they lose by failing (§6)
FLAW        the thing preventing them getting the need
```
**⚠️ Arc types**: **change arc (the character transforms), flat/steadfast arc (⚠️ the
character transforms the WORLD instead — Atticus Finch, most series leads), and negative
arc (they degrade).** ⚠️ **Not every protagonist needs a change arc, and forcing one onto
a steadfast character is a common note-driven mistake.**
**⚠️ Sympathy vs interest**: ⚠️ **audiences do not need to LIKE a protagonist; they need to
be INTERESTED and to understand the want.** **Competence, wit, being treated unjustly, and
caring about something all buy engagement without likeability.**
**⚠️ Antagonists**: ⚠️ **the strongest ones are right from their own perspective, and the
weakest are evil because the plot requires it.** **A good test: can you write the story
from the antagonist's POV and have it make sense as a story?**

## §4. ⚠️ Structure Models, Assessed

```
⚠️ THREE-ACT           setup / confrontation / resolution. ⚠️ So general it's
   nearly unfalsifiable — which is why it's both universally true and
   nearly useless as guidance
⚠️ FREYTAG'S PYRAMID   exposition, rising action, climax, falling action,
   dénouement. ⚠️ Derived from Greek and Shakespearean TRAGEDY; fits
   modern narrative poorly, and the long falling action is rare now
⚠️ HERO'S JOURNEY      Campbell via Vogler. ⚠️ Campbell was doing comparative
   MYTHOLOGY, not writing instruction. ⚠️ Criticized for being fitted so
   loosely it matches anything, and for a male-coded, Western-weighted
   reading of a global corpus
⚠️ SAVE THE CAT        Snyder's 15 beats with page numbers. ⚠️ Genuinely
   useful as a diagnostic; ⚠️ its dominance is plausibly a real cause of
   structural homogeneity in studio film
SEQUENCE METHOD        ⚠️ 8 sequences of ~15 min. Underrated — descends from
   reel-length practicalities and maps well to how scripts actually build
STORY CIRCLE           ⚠️ Harmon's 8 steps, a compressed hero's journey.
   Excellent for episodic television specifically
KISHŌTENKETSU          ⚠️ 4-act East Asian structure that does NOT require
   conflict — ki/shō/ten/ketsu, with the TWIST being juxtaposition rather
   than opposition. ⚠️ Important because it disproves "all story is conflict"
```
> **⚠️ GOTCHA — "all drama is conflict" is a Western convention, not a universal.**
> ⚠️ **Kishōtenketsu structures produce satisfying narrative through juxtaposition and
> recontextualization rather than through opposition**, **and a great deal of successful
> work — much anime and manga, many literary short stories, some of the best comedy — runs
> on it.** **⚠️ If your story feels forced, the problem may be that you're manufacturing
> conflict a structure demands rather than one the material wants.**

## §5. ⚠️ The Scene

**⚠️ The scene is the actual unit of craft, and most "structure problems" are scene
problems.**
```
⚠️ Every scene needs: someone who WANTS something specific IN THIS SCENE,
   an OBSTACLE, and a TURN — the state changes by the end
⚠️ VALUE CHARGE  the scene should move from + to − or − to + on some value
   (safety, love, hope, power). ⚠️ A scene that ends where it started is cut
⚠️ ENTER LATE, LEAVE EARLY  start as close to the turn as possible
⚠️ SUBTEXT  what characters say ≠ what they want (§7)
```
**⚠️ The diagnostic that fixes most flat scenes**: ⚠️ **ask what each character wants IN
THIS SCENE, specifically, and what they'll do to get it.** **If the answer is vague —
"to explain the plan," "to bond" — the scene will be flat regardless of the dialogue.**
**⚠️ Scene vs sequel** (Bickham/Swain): **scene = goal, conflict, disaster; sequel =
reaction, dilemma, decision.** ⚠️ **Prose needs the sequel beats; screen drama compresses
or externalizes them, which is one of the real differences between the forms** (§22 → `story-acting-directing-medium-and-industry`).

## §6. Conflict, Tension and Stakes

**⚠️ Tension ≠ surprise, and Hitchcock's bomb-under-the-table illustration remains the
clearest statement of it**: ⚠️ **a bomb that explodes unexpectedly buys a few seconds of
shock; a bomb the AUDIENCE knows about and the characters don't buys a whole scene of
tension.** **⚠️ Dramatic irony — the audience knowing more than the character — is the
single most reliable tension generator available.**
**⚠️ Stakes must be specific and personal to work.** ⚠️ **"The world will end" is weaker
than "her brother will die," because scale is not the same as weight** — **and the
common note "raise the stakes" usually means "make me care," not "make it bigger."**
**⚠️ Types of conflict**: **interpersonal, internal, societal, environmental** — ⚠️ **and
the strongest work usually runs an external conflict that FORCES the internal one.**
**⚠️ Escalation and complication**: ⚠️ **each attempt should make things worse in a way
that follows from the attempt.** **"Yes, but / No, and" is the practical tool: the
character succeeds but a new problem arises, or fails and something else goes wrong.**
