Military science is **not a science in the physics sense**. The object of study is a reactive,
adapting adversary, which means no principle holds unconditionally and every advantage is temporary.
The defining feature is the interactive, two-sided nature of the problem: engineering has
constraints; war has an opponent trying to invalidate your assumptions.

The material is pitched at the **war-college survey level** — strategy, doctrine, institutions, and
policy — what an informed citizen, policy analyst, or historian needs in order to reason about
defence. It contains no operational, tactical, or technical instruction for causing harm.

## §1 What military science is, and the theoretical canon

> **THREE IDEAS THAT ORGANIZE EVERYTHING.** First, **war is a political instrument or it is
> nothing** — Clausewitz's central claim is not a slogan but a normative claim that military force
> detached from a political objective it can achieve is the recurring cause of strategic failure.
> Second, **defence is an economic and industrial problem before it is a military one** — logistics,
> industrial capacity, and the ability to replace losses decide protracted wars; amateurs discuss
> tactics, the professional literature discusses sustainment. Third, **the hardest capability to copy
> is institutional learning** — any state can buy equipment, but what distinguishes effective
> militaries is the speed at which they absorb, adapt, and redistribute what they learn, which is
> organizational, not technological.

The first idea governs §3 below; the second and third are developed at length in §4 →
`endeavour-logistics-doctrine-modern-conflict-and-the-law`.

### Clausewitz

Carl von Clausewitz's *On War* is the single most important body of military theory and the most
commonly misquoted. His central claim — **"war is the continuation of policy by other means"** — is
not a cynical observation but a **normative** claim: military force only makes sense as an instrument
of a political purpose. War pursued for its own logic, or with objectives the military instrument
cannot deliver, is the recurring source of catastrophe.

The **paradoxical trinity** is his most useful analytical tool. War is unstable between three
elements, and a strategy that ignores any one of them fails:

| Element of the trinity | Associated with | What ignoring it produces |
|---|---|---|
| Primordial violence and hatred | The people | Wars that lose domestic support |
| Chance and probability | The army and its commander | Plans that dissolve on contact |
| Rational subordination to policy | The government | Purely political direction that ignores military reality — and disasters |

The other four concepts carry most of the analytical weight. **Friction** — *"everything in war is
simple, but the simplest thing is difficult"* — is the accumulation of small difficulties that
separates the plan from the execution; **the fog of war** is decision under irreducible uncertainty.
**The centre of gravity** is the source of an opponent's strength and cohesion, frequently
**misapplied as "the important thing"**, which drains the concept of its content. **The culminating
point** is where an offensive, weakening as it advances (extending supply lines, absorbing losses,
moving away from its base), becomes weaker than the defender; recognizing it is one of the hardest
judgements in operational art, and in practice it is usually a logistics phenomenon — that reading is
developed in §4 → `endeavour-logistics-doctrine-modern-conflict-and-the-law`.

Clausewitz also argued that **defence is the inherently stronger form of war**, though with a
**negative object** — you can preserve but not gain.

### Sun Tzu and the other traditions

Sun Tzu's *Art of War* emphasizes winning without fighting, deception, knowing both yourself and the
enemy, attacking strategy rather than forces, and the primacy of positioning and intelligence.

The common reading of Sun Tzu as the "smart" alternative to Clausewitz's "bloodthirsty" realism is a
**misreading of both**: Clausewitz also emphasized that war should serve policy at least cost, and
Sun Tzu also assumed decisive battle when necessary. The genuine differences are **contextual** —
Sun Tzu writes for a world of shifting alliances between comparable states, Clausewitz after
Napoleon's wars of national mobilization.

Other traditions worth knowing by name and claim: **Thucydides** on fear, honour, and interest as
motives for war; **Kautilya's *Arthashastra*** on statecraft; **Machiavelli**; and **Jomini**, whose
geometric, prescriptive approach was **more influential than Clausewitz on nineteenth-century staff
practice**. The **Russian and Soviet tradition** contributed **operational art** as a distinct level,
**deep battle** theory, and **correlation of forces** analysis — genuinely original contributions
that Western militaries later absorbed.

### Sea and air power

**Mahan** argued that command of the sea through decisive fleet action determines national power.
Influential — and it shaped naval building programmes before 1914 in ways that arguably contributed
to the war it was meant to win.

**Corbett** is the more subtle counterpoint: the sea is a **means of communication**, not territory;
command is usually **disputed** rather than absolute; and naval power matters for what it **enables
on land**. Corbett has worn better.

**Douhet, Mitchell, and Trenchard** on air power promised that strategic bombing would break enemy
will and make land campaigns unnecessary. The **strategic bombing surveys after 1945** found the
effects on morale and production **far more limited and far more costly than predicted**. The claim
has been made repeatedly since and has repeatedly disappointed. **Warden's five rings** and
**effects-based operations** are the modern descendants.

The honest assessment: precision air power has been **decisive in some campaigns and insufficient
alone in most**.

## §2 The levels of war, the principles, and decision cycles

The three levels are distinct, and **conflating them is the most common error in public discussion
of war**.

| Level | What it concerns | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic | National objectives, resource allocation, what the war is for | Where the political objective is set — and therefore where the war is decided |
| Operational | Campaigns — the sequencing of engagements to achieve strategic aims | Formalized as a distinct level by Soviet theorists; adopted in the West only in the **1980s** |
| Tactical | Individual engagements | Winning these does not by itself decide the war (see the callout) |

> **TACTICAL SUCCESS DOES NOT AGGREGATE INTO STRATEGIC SUCCESS.** This is the single most important
> thing about the levels of war. A military can win every engagement and lose the war, because the
> war is decided by whether the political objective is achieved. **Vietnam** is the canonical Western
> example; the point generalizes. The corresponding error runs **upward** too: a strategic objective
> the military instrument cannot deliver — "defeat an ideology," "create a stable democracy" — is not
> made achievable by tactical excellence.

### The principles are heuristics, not laws

The classic lists — **objective, offensive, mass, economy of force, manoeuvre, unity of command,
security, surprise, simplicity** — are useful mnemonics but heuristics, not laws. Several contradict
each other outright:

| Principle | Contradicts | The tension |
|---|---|---|
| Mass | Economy of force | Force concentrated at the decisive point is force not economized elsewhere |
| Surprise | Security | The measures that preserve surprise and those that preserve security pull opposite ways |
| Simplicity | Sophistication | A plan simple enough to execute reliably is not the most sophisticated one available |

Judgement lies in knowing **which principle governs in a given situation** — which is exactly what
the list cannot tell you, and why it is a memory aid rather than a method.

### Manoeuvre, attrition, and decision cycles

**The manoeuvre-versus-attrition distinction:** attrition seeks to **destroy the opponent's
forces**; manoeuvre seeks to **break their cohesion and decision-making** so that their remaining
strength becomes irrelevant. **Boyd's OODA loop** (observe, **orient**, decide, act) argues that
operating inside an opponent's decision cycle causes disorientation and collapse — but **ORIENT is
the part Boyd considered decisive, and the part popular versions omit**, so a version that is only
"decide faster" has thrown away the argument. **Mission command** (*Auftragstaktik*) specifies the
**intent and the task** and delegates the **method**; it requires trust, shared doctrine, and
tolerance of subordinate error, which is why it is **easy to declare and hard to practise**. The
doctrine and training machinery it depends on is §4 →
`endeavour-logistics-doctrine-modern-conflict-and-the-law`.

## §3 Deterrence, nuclear strategy, escalation, alliances, and grand strategy

### Deterrence is psychological, not physical

Deterrence exists **in the mind of the opponent**. That is why it can fail while capability is
entirely adequate, and why **you cannot verify that it is working**.

| Form | Mechanism | Credibility |
|---|---|---|
| Deterrence by **punishment** | Threaten unacceptable costs | Harder — it requires the defender to accept costs of its own |
| Deterrence by **denial** | Convince the opponent the objective cannot be achieved | **Generally the more credible**, because it does not |

The **three requirements — capability, credibility, and communication — must all hold**. Credibility
is the hard one, and it is why the credibility of **extended deterrence** (defending someone else) is
the central problem of alliance politics.

**Compellence is harder than deterrence (Schelling).** Deterrence asks someone **not to act** — they
can comply invisibly and save face. Compellence asks them **to act, visibly**, which requires
accepting humiliation. This asymmetry explains many failed coercive campaigns.

**The stability-instability paradox:** stability at the nuclear level can *enable* conflict at lower
levels, because both sides know it will not escalate.

> **THE MEASUREMENT PROBLEM.** You cannot observe deterrence succeeding. The absence of attack is
> consistent both with successful deterrence **and** with the opponent never having intended to
> attack. Every claim that a posture "has deterred" someone is an inference from a null result.

### Nuclear strategy: survivability, the triad, and extended deterrence

Nuclear weapons made the costs of great-power war potentially unlimited **while leaving the political
disputes intact**. The concepts that matter at this level:

- **First and second strike**, with **survivability the key requirement** — a force that cannot be
  destroyed preemptively removes the incentive to strike first.
- **The triad** (land, sea, air) for survivability and redundancy, **ballistic missile submarines the
  most survivable leg**; **MAD** (mutual assured destruction) as a **condition rather than a policy**; **counterforce versus
  countervalue** targeting; and **launch on warning** with its risks.
- **Extended deterrence is the hard problem**: will A risk its own cities for B? Forward deployment,
  nuclear sharing, and alliance integration exist to make the answer credible, and **the question
  never fully closes**.

**The near-miss record is the strongest argument for caution** — multiple documented incidents where
escalation was averted by individual human judgement.

> **DETERRENCE "WORKING" FOR DECADES IS COMPATIBLE WITH HAVING BEEN REPEATEDLY LUCKY.** A record of
> non-use is evidence about outcomes, not about the reliability of the mechanism that produced them.
> Read together with the measurement problem above and the near-miss record, that is the whole
> epistemic position on nuclear deterrence: the strongest available evidence is consistent with
> several different explanations, and the confident ones are the ones to distrust.

**Scope.** This is deterrence theory and force posture — survivability, targeting doctrine, alliance
credibility. It is **not** weapon design, yield, device physics, or targeting specifics, and none of
that appears anywhere in this reference. The nuclear material this reference does carry is **fusion
energy** plus the radiation and dose background: §23–§25 →
`endeavour-fusion-physics-confinement-and-engineering`.

> **THE LAW GOVERNING THE USE OF FORCE APPLIES TO EVERYTHING ABOVE, AND A READER WHO LOADS ONLY THIS
> SKILL STILL MEETS IT.** Deterrence, targeting doctrine and escalation are reasoned about inside
> the law of armed conflict, not alongside it. **Jus ad bellum** governs *when* force may lawfully
> be used; **jus in bello** (international humanitarian law) governs *how*, and **it applies equally
> to all parties regardless of who started it or whose cause is just** — the better *ad bellum* case
> grants no latitude whatsoever in conduct. Its four core principles:
>
> - **Distinction** — between combatants and civilians, and between military and civilian objects.
>   The foundational rule.
> - **Proportionality** — expected incidental civilian harm must not be excessive relative to the
>   **concrete and direct military advantage anticipated**. It is **not a casualty comparison**
>   between sides, and that is how the principle is almost always misused in public argument.
> - **Military necessity** — permits only what a legitimate military purpose requires, and **does
>   not excuse violations of the other rules**.
> - **Humanity** — avoidance of unnecessary suffering.
>
> The full treatment, with enforcement, why weak enforcement does not make the law meaningless, and
> command responsibility, is **§6 → `endeavour-logistics-doctrine-modern-conflict-and-the-law`**.

### Escalation

**Kahn's** escalation ladders and thresholds: thresholds are **socially constructed and
asymmetrically perceived** — what one side reads as proportionate, the other may read as a major
escalation.

**Salami tactics and grey-zone action exploit this**: each individual step is too small to justify a
response, and the cumulative effect is large. The grey-zone repertoire itself — deniable forces,
economic coercion, lawfare, proxies — is §5 → `endeavour-logistics-doctrine-modern-conflict-and-the-law`.

Escalation management depends on **signalling** (making intent legible to an opponent who reads
thresholds differently), **off-ramps that let an opponent stop without humiliation** — the
compellence asymmetry above is exactly why this matters — and **communication channels that survive a
crisis**, since the moment they are needed is the moment ordinary channels are least likely to work.

> **THE UNCOMFORTABLE WARGAMING FINDING.** Participants frequently escalate **faster than they
> predict they will**. Stated thresholds and revealed thresholds are different things, including
> one's own.

### Alliances

Alliances exist to **aggregate capability, deter jointly, and constrain each other**. The two failure
modes are opposite and **both real**:

| Failure mode | What happens | Why it persists |
|---|---|---|
| **Abandonment** | The ally does not come | The extended-deterrence question never fully closes |
| **Entrapment** | The ally drags you into their war | The commitments that make deterrence credible also bind |

**Burden sharing is a permanent dispute** because collective defence is a **public good with
free-rider incentives**. This is **structural, not a moral failing** — an analysis that treats it as
bad faith will keep being surprised by it.

### Grand strategy

Grand strategy is the integration of all instruments — **diplomatic, informational, military, and
economic** — toward national objectives, with **military force usually the most expensive and least
controllable** of them.

The core discipline is **matching ends, ways, and means**. The classic failure is **ends that exceed
means**, and it is the same failure the trinity and the levels of war both predict: an objective the
instrument cannot deliver is not rescued by executing well.
