# Speaking, Influence and Community: What This Field Actually Knows, Speech Anxiety, Audience and Purpose, Structure, and Openings and Closings

> **Part 1 of 6** of the *Public Speaking, Influence and Community* reference (plugin `speaking-influence-and-community`), covering §0–§5. Sibling skills: `speaking-language-delivery-visual-aids-and-practice` (§6–§11), `speaking-listening-rapport-difficult-conversations-and-negotiation` (§12–§17), `speaking-persuasion-and-the-ethics-of-influence` (§18–§19), `speaking-groups-community-facilitation-organizing-and-conflict` (§20–§26), `speaking-reference` (§27–§32). Section numbers are shared across the set; a reference written as §N → `skill` points into that sibling skill.
>
> **Currency:** The craft is old and stable. Two areas have moved. See §27 → `speaking-reference` for the status of the famous body-language claims, and what the persuasion evidence now shows.

> **⚠️ A domain with an unusually bad ratio of confident advice to actual evidence.** ⚠️ **A
> great deal of what is taught in communication training traces to small 1960s studies,
> misread, or to findings that failed to replicate — and is still being repeated in
> material published this year** (§27.1 → `speaking-reference`).
>
> **⚠️ This file tries to separate three things: what is well supported, what is craft
> wisdom that experienced practitioners agree on without formal evidence, and what is
> folklore.**
>
> **⚠️ GOTCHA** boxes mark where the standard advice is wrong or overstated.
>
> **The three ideas that organize this document:**
> 1. **⚠️ SUBSTANCE IS THE TECHNIQUE** (§4, §18 → `speaking-persuasion-and-the-ethics-of-influence`, §27.2 → `speaking-reference`). **The persuasion evidence points
>    consistently at content — relevant facts, specific evidence, real engagement with the
>    other person's actual position — rather than at delivery tricks. This is both the most
>    reassuring finding for nervous speakers and the least commercially attractive one for
>    the training industry.**
> 2. **⚠️ INFLUENCE WITHOUT A LEGITIMACY CHECK IS MANIPULATION** (§19 → `speaking-persuasion-and-the-ethics-of-influence`). **The techniques in
>    this file work on people who don't want them used. The distinction between persuasion
>    and manipulation is not a matter of degree or intent — it has a testable structure, and
>    it belongs at the front, not in an appendix.**
> 3. **⚠️ COMMUNITIES ARE MAINTAINED, NOT FOUNDED** (§21 → `speaking-groups-community-facilitation-organizing-and-conflict`, §24 → `speaking-groups-community-facilitation-organizing-and-conflict`). **Almost all the failure
>    modes are in sustaining: the founder bottleneck, volunteer burnout, unhandled conflict,
>    and the transition from a group that knows each other to one that cannot.**

## §1. What This Field Actually Knows

```
⚠️ THREE TIERS, and honest advice distinguishes them
   ⚠️ WELL SUPPORTED  ⚠️ exposure reduces speech anxiety ·
      preparation reduces it · ⚠️ structure aids comprehension
      and recall · ⚠️ specific evidence persuades more than
      assertion · ⚠️ listening-first conversation changes minds
      more durably than argument
   ⚠️ CRAFT CONSENSUS (no strong evidence, wide practitioner
      agreement)  ⚠️ open with something concrete · cut ruthlessly ·
      rehearse aloud · know your first and last lines cold
   ⚠️ ⚠️ FOLKLORE  ⚠️ the 7-38-55 rule · power posing as a
      confidence hack · reading deception from body language ·
      most "learning styles" claims · the idea that speech
      anxiety outranks death in surveys (§27.1, §29)
⚠️ ⚠️ WHY THE FOLKLORE PERSISTS  ⚠️ it is memorable, sells
   training, and flatters the idea that communication is a set of
   tricks rather than a matter of having something worth saying
   and understanding who you are saying it to
```

# PART I — PUBLIC SPEAKING

## §2. ⚠️ Speech Anxiety

> **⚠️ The first obstacle for most people, and the area where the honest advice differs most
> from the popular advice.**
```
⚠️ WHAT IT IS  ⚠️ an ordinary threat response — adrenaline,
   heart rate, shaking, dry mouth, narrowed attention.
   ⚠️ NOT a character defect and not rare
⚠️ ⚠️ WHAT ACTUALLY HELPS, roughly in order of evidence
   ⚠️ 1. EXPOSURE  ⚠️ repeated, graduated speaking. This is the
      one with the strongest support, and it is why speaking
      clubs work — the mechanism is habituation, not tuition
   ⚠️ 2. PREPARATION and REHEARSAL  ⚠️ reduces the genuine
      uncertainty rather than managing the feeling
   ⚠️ 3. ⚠️ REAPPRAISAL  ⚠️ interpreting arousal as readiness
      rather than fear. ⚠️ The physiology is nearly identical;
      the label is what differs, and relabelling is easier than
      suppressing
   ⚠️ 4. Slow exhalation to steady the breath
   ⚠️ 5. ⚠️ ATTENTION SHIFT  ⚠️ from self-monitoring to the
      audience and the message. ⚠️ Much of the distress is
      self-focus, and having a task outside yourself displaces it
   ⚠️ 6. Cognitive behavioural approaches for severe cases
⚠️ ⚠️ WHAT HELPS LESS THAN CLAIMED  ⚠️ "picture them in their
   underwear" (⚠️ absurd and distracting) · ⚠️ power posing
   (§27.1) · suppressing the feeling · ⚠️ trying to be
   relaxed — the goal is FUNCTIONING while activated, not calm
⚠️ ⚠️ THE AUDIENCE CANNOT SEE MOST OF IT. ⚠️ Internal
   experience far exceeds visible signs — a genuinely useful
   thing to know
```

## §3. Audience and Purpose

**⚠️ Answer these before writing a word**, ⚠️ **because almost every bad talk is a talk that
skipped them:**
```
⚠️ WHO  ⚠️ what do they already know? · what do they CARE about? ·
   what do they believe now? · ⚠️ why are they here, and did
   they choose to be?
⚠️ WHAT CHANGE  ⚠️ "inform" is too vague. ⚠️ What should be
   DIFFERENT afterwards — known, believed, felt, or DONE?
⚠️ ⚠️ THE ONE-SENTENCE TEST  ⚠️ if you cannot state the point in
   one sentence, the talk does not have one yet
⚠️ THE CONSTRAINTS  time (⚠️ and going over is a discourtesy to
   everyone) · room · time of day · what precedes and follows
⚠️ ⚠️ THE CURSE OF KNOWLEDGE  ⚠️ the single most common failure in
   expert speaking: you cannot un-know what you know, so you
   systematically overestimate what is obvious. ⚠️ The fix is
   testing on a real non-expert, not introspection
```

## §4. ⚠️ Structure

> **⚠️ The highest-leverage part of preparation, and the part most often skipped in favour
> of slides.**
```
⚠️ ⚠️ THE CORE PRINCIPLE: STRUCTURE IS FOR THE LISTENER, NOT THE
   SPEAKER. ⚠️ A reader can re-read; a listener cannot. ⚠️ They
   need signposting, repetition and clear transitions that would
   feel excessive on the page
⚠️ PATTERNS THAT WORK
   ⚠️ PROBLEM → SOLUTION  · ⚠️ SITUATION-COMPLICATION-RESOLUTION
   · chronological · comparison · ⚠️ CLAIM-EVIDENCE-IMPLICATION
   · ⚠️ MONROE'S MOTIVATED SEQUENCE for persuasive talks
   (attention, need, satisfaction, visualization, action)
⚠️ ⚠️ THREE MAIN POINTS is a cliché because working memory is
   limited. ⚠️ Five is usually too many for a listener to hold
⚠️ SIGNPOSTING  ⚠️ tell them the shape, mark transitions
   explicitly, summarize at the end of sections. ⚠️ "Tell them
   what you'll tell them" is mocked and it works
⚠️ ⚠️ CUT FIRST, POLISH SECOND. ⚠️ Nearly every draft talk is too
   long, and the fix is removing a whole point rather than
   speaking faster
⚠️ STORIES  ⚠️ genuinely more memorable than abstraction, and
   ⚠️ the honest caveat is that a vivid anecdote is weak
   evidence — use narrative to make a point LAND, not to
   substitute for the point being true (§19)
```

## §5. Openings and Closings

**⚠️ The two moments people remember**, ⚠️ **and the two worth writing out verbatim and
knowing cold — because they carry you through the worst of the anxiety.**
**⚠️ Openings that work**: ⚠️ **a concrete specific (a scene, a number, an object), a real
question, a surprising fact, a short story — anything that begins IN something rather than
approaching it.**
**⚠️ Openings that don't**: ⚠️ **"thank you for having me," an apology, throat-clearing about
your credentials, a joke you are not sure of, and a dictionary definition.**
**⚠️ Closings**: ⚠️ **land the one sentence from §3, then stop.** ⚠️ **A call to action if
there is one; a return to the opening image if you have one.**
> **⚠️ GOTCHA — do not end on the Q&A.** ⚠️ **The last thing said should be yours, not
> whatever the final questioner raised.** **⚠️ Take questions, then close with a prepared
> two sentences.**
