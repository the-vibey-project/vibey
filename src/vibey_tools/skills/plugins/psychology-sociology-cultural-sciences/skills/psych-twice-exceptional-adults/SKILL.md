## §30. Routing, Scope and How to Read This Part

**⚠️ The 2e literature has a shape you need to know before you read any of it.** **Most of
it is about children in schools, much of it is written by advocates and practitioners, and
the adult evidence is thin.** **Large, preregistered, adult-focused studies of the
giftedness × autism × ADHD intersection essentially do not exist.** What follows therefore
mixes three kinds of material, and keeping them apart is the whole job:

1. **Research findings** about intelligence, autism and ADHD **separately** — some of it
   among the most robust in psychology (§14 → `psych-development-personality-social-clinical-and-neurodiversity`).
2. **Small or self-selected studies** of gifted samples — Mensa members, profoundly gifted
   cohorts of a few dozen, single-site clinical samples.
3. **Clinical and educational lore** — frameworks, observations and advocacy positions
   that are influential and often useful, and that have **not been tested in the way their
   confident phrasing implies**.

### 30.1 The labels used in this part
These extend the reliability gradient of §1.3 → `psych-epistemic-situation-and-replication-reform`:

| Label | Meaning here |
|---|---|
| **[ROBUST]** | Replicated widely, large samples, often decades of data |
| **[MODERATE]** | Real but smaller, narrower or more context-dependent than usually presented; or one strong study not yet independently replicated |
| **[WEAK]** | Small, self-selected or uncontrolled samples; self-report against population base rates; single unreplicated study |
| **[CONTESTED]** | Serious researchers disagree, or the construct itself is disputed |
| **[CLINICAL LORE]** | Practitioner observation, theory or advocacy position — not tested in the way the claim implies. **Not the same as false** |
| **[INTERPRETATION]** | An inference the source draws beyond what its cited data show |
| **[VERSIONED]** | A figure that changes with policy, funding or editions; recheck before use |

## §31. Defining Giftedness — Competing Definitions, and Why It Matters

**⚠️ Giftedness has never had a single agreed definition, and the choice of definition
decides who counts.** The main positions, as the source presents them:

| Framework | Claim | Standing |
|---|---|---|
| **Terman**, *Genetic Studies of Genius* (from 1921) | Psychometric: IQ above ~135. Tracked 1,528 Californian children across their lives; as a group they were healthier, better adjusted and more educated than peers (≈70% held bachelor's degrees by midlife against a national rate of ≈8%) | **[ROBUST] as a longitudinal record; [WEAK] as a general statement about gifted people** — the sample was ~95–99% white and middle-class, Terman intervened in participants' lives, and two children he screened out later won Nobel Prizes (Shockley, Alvarez) |
| **Hollingworth** (contemporary with Terman) | Studied the profoundly gifted (IQ 160+); proposed IQ ~125–155 as "socially optimal" and warned of severe social difficulty above ~160, from lack of peers rather than disturbance | **[CLINICAL LORE]** — case observations on very small samples (§38) |
| **Renzulli**, Three-Ring Conception (1978) | Giftedness emerges where above-average ability, creativity and task commitment meet | **[CLINICAL LORE]** — influential in identification policy; a conceptual model, not a measured structure |
| **Sternberg**, Triarchic Theory (1985) → WICS | Analytical, creative and practical intelligence; later Wisdom, Intelligence, Creativity, Synthesized | **[CONTESTED]** — the separability of "practical intelligence" from *g* is disputed |
| **Gardner**, Multiple Intelligences (1983) | Several independent intelligences | **⚠️ [WEAK] as a psychometric model** — popular in education; the positive manifold (all abilities correlate) contradicts the independence claim (§14 → `psych-development-personality-social-clinical-and-neurodiversity`) |
| **Gagné**, Differentiated Model (1985) | Gifts (untrained ability) need catalysts — environment, motivation, practice — to become talents (developed skill) | **[CLINICAL LORE]**, conceptually useful: it explains why high ability and high achievement come apart |
| **Columbus Group** (1991; Silverman, Piechowski, Tolan) | Giftedness is **asynchronous development** — advanced cognition plus heightened intensity producing qualitatively different inner experience, increasing with ability | **[CONTESTED]** — a **definitional stance**, not a finding. It is the foundation of most 2e advocacy, and "qualitatively different experience" has not been operationalized or measured |

### 31.1 Thresholds, tiers and ceilings
- **The common school threshold is IQ 130** (2 SD above the mean, ≈2.3% of the population)
  **[ROBUST as arithmetic; the cut-off itself is a convention]**.
- **Gross's tiers** — 145–159 "highly", 160–179 "exceptionally", 180+ "profoundly" gifted,
  with rarities of roughly 1 in 1,000, 1 in 10,000 and under 1 in a million
  **[CLINICAL LORE]**: the rarities are normal-curve arithmetic, and ⚠️ **the normal curve is
  least trustworthy exactly in its extreme tails**, where norming samples are thinnest.
- **⚠️ Ceilings.** Modern deviation-IQ tests (WISC-V, WAIS-IV) top out around 160; a person
  at the ceiling has demonstrated *at least* that score. The old ratio-IQ Stanford-Binet L-M
  produced scores above 200 that are **not comparable** to deviation IQs **[ROBUST]**.

> **⚠️ GOTCHA — very high IQ figures are the least precise numbers in psychometrics.**
> Measurement error, thin norms at the extremes, ceiling effects, and regression to the mean
> all bite hardest above ~145. **A reported score of 165 or "200+" is a wide interval, not a
> point**, and historical ratio-IQ figures cannot be compared with modern scores at all.
> Treat tier labels as rough bands.

## §32. What IQ Scores Show — and Hide — Under Adversity

**⚠️ For a 2e adult the question is not "is IQ valid" (it is, at the population level, §14 →
`psych-development-personality-social-clinical-and-neurodiversity`) but "what does THIS score,
taken in THIS state, tell you".**

### 32.1 State effects on measured performance
- **Depression** depresses Performance IQ and Processing Speed more than Verbal IQ
  (Sackeim et al., 1992) **[MODERATE]**.
- **Chronic stress** impairs cognitive flexibility, working memory and attentional shifting,
  with sustained HPA-axis activation as the proposed pathway (Stenfors et al., 2013)
  **[MODERATE]** — the association is well supported; the mechanism less so.
- **Childhood PTSD** is associated with lower measured IQ, with deficits that appear to
  follow trauma rather than precede it (De Bellis et al., 2009) **[MODERATE]**.

### 32.2 Index scatter and the General Ability Index
- **In gifted children with ADHD or autism, Verbal Comprehension and Fluid Reasoning run
  well above Processing Speed** — in one 2026 sample (Gilman, Peters, Silverman et al.,
  N=390), mean VCI ≈132 against PSI ≈106, a gap of nearly 26 points **[MODERATE]** — a
  clinical sample, recently published, not yet independently replicated.
- **The General Ability Index (GAI)** omits Processing Speed and Working Memory. In the same
  study mean FSIQ was 126.6 and mean GAI 128.9. Among gifted children with ADHD, GAI–PSI
  discrepancies were roughly twice those of ADHD-only children (Cornoldi et al., 2023)
  **[MODERATE]**.
- **Practice has moved accordingly.** WISC-V Technical Report #5 (Raiford, Silverman, Gilman
  & Courville, 2019) set out an Expanded General Ability Index, and NAGC's 2018 position
  statement said the Full Scale score may disadvantage such students **[VERSIONED — a
  publisher report and an advocacy position, not an independent validation]**.

> **⚠️ GOTCHA — the scatter is real and it is not a diagnostic signature.**
> **Large gaps between a person's highest and lowest index scores are common in the general
> norming samples too**, and they grow with overall ability. A 25-point spread is evidence
> that a composite hides structure — a sound reason to report index scores and the GAI —
> ⚠️ **not evidence of 2e status, and not a measurement of "true" ability.** Note also that
> the group-level FSIQ–GAI difference in the study above is about two points; the large
> discrepancies are at index level.

### 32.3 Claims in the source that outrun the data
- **"Masking and executive dysfunction can suppress measured IQ by 15–25 points"** —
  **[WEAK]**: no study is cited for the figure. State effects are real (§32.1); this
  magnitude for this population is unestablished.
- **"A score taken during burnout should be read as a floor, not a ceiling"** —
  **[INTERPRETATION]**. Reasonable caution about *one* direction of error, but ⚠️ **measurement
  error runs both ways**, and for very high scorers **regression to the mean** predicts that a
  retest will usually come out *lower*, not higher. The defensible version: **a score obtained
  in a depleted state is a lower-confidence estimate; note the state in the report, prefer
  index-level interpretation, and retest when circumstances change.**
- **"True" or "trait" IQ** as a fixed hidden number **[INTERPRETATION]** — a score is an
  estimate with an error band, under specific conditions; there is no directly observable
  true value to compare it with.

## §33. The Neuroscience of High Ability — What It Shows and How Far It Reaches

**⚠️ The headline in popular 2e material — "gifted brains are wired differently at every
level" — is a group-level correlational literature restated as a categorical difference.**
The individual findings, labelled:

| Finding | Source | Label |
|---|---|---|
| **Cortical trajectory, not thickness, tracks IQ**: superior-IQ children (121–149) started with thinner prefrontal cortex, thickened until ~age 11 (vs 7–8), then thinned faster | Shaw et al. (2006), *Nature*, ~300 children scanned repeatedly at NIMH | **[MODERATE]** — a landmark longitudinal cohort; group averages from one programme; not a marker usable for individuals |
| **White-matter integrity (FA) correlates with intelligence** | Navas-Sánchez et al. (2014), mathematically gifted sample; a 2020 study of 57 children | **[MODERATE]** for the general correlation across DTI studies; **[WEAK]** for the specific gifted samples (small N). "Literally faster neural communication" is an **[INTERPRETATION]** of FA |
| **P-FIT**: intelligence relies on a parieto-frontal network | Jung & Haier (2007) | **[MODERATE]** — a widely used integrative framework |
| **Network Neuroscience Theory**: *g* reflects flexible transitions between network states | Barbey (2018); Barbey & Wilcox (2026, *Nature Communications*, N=976) | **[MODERATE]** — theory with growing support; the 2026 study is large but recent |
| **Neural efficiency**: higher-IQ people use less activation on easy-to-moderate tasks and more on hard ones | Haier et al. (1988, PET); Neubauer & Fink (2009) review of 54 studies | **[MODERATE]** — holds with the boundary conditions stated |
| **Shorter P300 latency; distinctive gamma-band patterns; fronto-parietal phase-locking in math-gifted adolescents** | Zhang et al. (2014, 2015) | **[WEAK–MODERATE]** — small samples, specific tasks |
| **Inverted-U dopamine–performance relation** in prefrontal cortex | broad pharmacological literature | **[ROBUST]** in animal and pharmacological work; relevant to ADHD stimulant response |

> **⚠️ GOTCHA — three over-readings to strike.**
> 1. **"The gifted brain is organized differently, producing qualitatively different
>    experience."** The data are **continuous correlations with IQ across the whole range**;
>    "qualitatively different experience" is the Columbus Group's definition (§31), not an
>    imaging result **[INTERPRETATION]**.
> 2. **No brain measure identifies a gifted or 2e individual.** Brain–IQ correlations are
>    modest; imaging is a research tool here, not an assessment.
> 3. **"The gifted-ADHD brain has the architecture but lacks the neurochemistry"** joins two
>    separate literatures **[INTERPRETATION]** — a metaphor, not a finding. And §15 →
>    `psych-development-personality-social-clinical-and-neurodiversity` applies: the imaging
>    literature is heterogeneous and has its own replication problems.

## §34. The 2e Concept and the Giftedness–Autism–ADHD Intersection

- **Origin.** Twice-exceptionality was formalized in the 1980s by Baum and Owen, studying
  high-ability students with learning disabilities who combined high creative productivity
  with processing deficits (Baum & Owen, 1988) **[CLINICAL LORE — a sound descriptive
  category, born in education]**.
- **Prevalence.** NAGC estimates ~360,000 2e students in US schools **[WEAK / VERSIONED]** —
  method unspecified, almost certainly an undercount, and no comparable adult figure exists.
- **⚠️ Autism and ADHD co-occur often** — 22–83% of autistic children also meet ADHD
  criteria across studies, and twin work estimates that 50–72% of contributing genetic
  factors are shared (Sokolova et al., 2017) **[ROBUST that co-occurrence is common; the
  wide range reflects sampling and method]**. Co-diagnosis has been permitted since DSM-5
  (2013).
- **"AuDHD is a distinct profile, not simple addition"** — one 2025 neuroimaging study found
  distinct structure and connectivity patterns **[WEAK — single study; plausible, unreplicated]**.
- **Baron-Cohen's hypersystemizing account (2017).** Autistic people score higher on the
  Systemizing Quotient; in 23andMe data on ~56,000 genotyped people, variants associated with
  systemizing overlapped with those associated with autism **[MODERATE as a genetic
  correlation of a self-report scale]**. The reading that autism genetics "includes genes for
  talent in pattern recognition", and that systemizing plus high *g* produces exceptional
  domain mastery, is **[CONTESTED]** — the empathizing–systemizing framework has been
  criticized for its measures and its sex-difference framing.
- **The "living contradictions" description** — autism's preference for routine against
  ADHD's novelty-seeking, predictability against impulsivity; deep expertise alongside
  failure at mundane tasks **[CLINICAL LORE]** — widely reported by clinicians and
  self-advocates, consistent with what is known of each condition, not measured as a
  combined profile.

> **⚠️ Each ingredient is well studied separately; the combination, in adults, is described
> far more than it is measured** — "the 2e AuDHD adult" as a type generalizes from clinical
> experience and child samples.

## §35. Invisibility — Masking, Compensation and Missed Diagnosis

**⚠️ This is the part of the 2e literature with the most practical value and some of the
better evidence, because it draws on the general autism literature on camouflaging.**

### 35.1 The three patterns of under-identification (NAGC)
1. **Giftedness seen, disability hidden** — the person compensates and reads as an
   "underachiever".
2. **Disability seen, giftedness hidden** — support targets the deficit and offers no
   intellectual challenge.
3. **⚠️ Each masks the other** — strengths and weaknesses cancel into "average" performance
   that triggers no referral either way. New Zealand guidance notes 2e learners often score
   in the 40th–60th percentile range on standardized tests.

**[CLINICAL LORE]** — a useful educational framework, not an empirically derived taxonomy.
The third pattern is the important one and is plausible on psychometric grounds.

### 35.2 Compensation and camouflaging
- **Livingston and Happé's compensation model (2017)** — behaviour looks improved while
  cognitive and neurobiological differences persist; higher (especially verbal) IQ
  facilitates compensation through learned rules and scripts; compensation is effortful and
  breaks down under depletion; high compensators report more anxiety than low compensators
  at equal verbal IQ **[MODERATE]** — a theoretical framework with supporting studies.
- **Hull et al. (2017)** — three dimensions of camouflaging: compensation, masking and
  assimilation **[MODERATE]** — from a qualitative study; later formalized in a measure.
- **Masking ability correlates positively with intelligence** (Fusar-Poli et al., 2022)
  **[MODERATE]** — the direction is consistent; the size varies by measure.
- **Diagnostic instruments under-detect higher-IQ autistic females**: autistic girls who
  meet criteria are more likely to have lower IQ (Dworzynski et al., 2012) **[MODERATE]**.
- **"Nearly 80% of autistic females undiagnosed at 18"** (attributed to UCLA Health)
  **[WEAK]** — a model-based estimate reported via an institutional summary; directionally
  consistent with the underdiagnosis literature, precise figure unreliable.
- **Autistic women are three to four times more likely to receive psychiatric
  misdiagnoses before autism is considered** (attributed to Lai & Baron-Cohen, 2015)
  **[MODERATE for the pattern; check the primary source for the multiplier]**.

> **⚠️ The structural point holds up better than any single figure**: **the same verbal
> ability that enables effective masking reduces the chance of referral**, so 2e adults are
> systematically late-identified. That is the "historical criteria built on a narrow sample"
> problem of §15 → `psych-development-personality-social-clinical-and-neurodiversity`,
> sharpened by high ability.

## §36. Dabrowski's Overexcitabilities and the Overlap Problem

**Kazimierz Dabrowski's Theory of Positive Disintegration (1964)** holds that psychological
tension and anxiety can be necessary for advanced personality development rather than signs
of pathology. Its five **overexcitabilities (OEs)** — psychomotor, sensual, intellectual,
imaginational and emotional — are heightened responses to stimuli read as "developmental
potential".

**The mapping onto neurodivergence the source draws:**

| OE | Overlaps with |
|---|---|
| Psychomotor (surplus energy, restlessness, rapid speech) | ADHD hyperactivity |
| Sensual (intensified sensory experience) | Autistic sensory-processing differences |
| Intellectual (relentless drive to understand) | Autistic deep interests; gifted curiosity |
| Imaginational | — |
| Emotional (intense feeling, deep caring) | Autistic emotional intensity; ADHD emotional dysregulation |

> **⚠️ GOTCHA — the OE construct is weaker than its standing in gifted education suggests.**
> - **[CONTESTED]** Positive Disintegration is a **grand developmental theory** that sits
>   outside mainstream personality science and has had little predictive testing.
> - **[WEAK]** The OEs are measured mainly by self-report questionnaires (the OEQ family)
>   with debated validity. **A 2016 meta-analysis (Winkler & Voight, *Gifted Child
>   Quarterly*) found gifted–nongifted differences that were modest and uneven across the
>   five OEs, largest for intellectual OE** *(wider literature, not the source)* — far from
>   the "defining trait of giftedness" presented in popular material.
> - **The overlap cuts in a direction the advocacy reading tends to skip.** At the 2023 NAGC
>   conference, Chris Wells of the Dabrowski Center argued OEs **cannot be reliably
>   distinguished** from ADHD and autism by behavioural observation **[CLINICAL LORE — a
>   conference position]**. If so, OEs may partly be **re-descriptions of neurodivergent
>   traits** under a non-clinical name. The source's resolution — "both may be
>   simultaneously true", a de-pathologized and a clinical description of the same
>   characteristics — is a reasonable **[INTERPRETATION]**, and ⚠️ **it means an OE framing
>   must never substitute for an autism or ADHD assessment.**

## §37. The Psychological Landscape — Existential Distress, Perfectionism, Impostor Feelings, Burnout

### 37.1 Existential depression
**James T. Webb** (founder of SENG) argued that gifted people meet Yalom's four "ultimate
concerns" — death, freedom, isolation, meaninglessness — earlier and harder, an "existential
depression" distinct from (though able to co-occur with) clinical depression and sharpened
by multipotentiality **[CLINICAL LORE]**: no validated measure, no controlled comparison.
⚠️ **The label must not delay assessment of a depression that meets clinical criteria.**

### 37.2 Perfectionism and the knowing–doing gap
- **Hamachek (1978)** distinguished normal perfectionism (high, achievable standards;
  satisfaction from effort) from neurotic perfectionism (fear of failure; worth tied to
  achievement). **Speirs Neumeister (2004, *Gifted Child Quarterly*)** found self-oriented
  perfectionists build work ethic and seek challenge, while socially prescribed
  perfectionists minimize success and internalize failure **[MODERATE]** — consistent with
  the wider perfectionistic-strivings vs perfectionistic-concerns distinction.
- **⚠️ Gifted people are not, on average, more maladaptively perfectionistic.** A 2020
  meta-analysis (Stricker et al., *Educational Psychology Review*) found gifted samples
  slightly higher on perfectionistic strivings and **no higher on perfectionistic
  concerns** *(wider literature, not the source)* **[MODERATE]**.
- **The 2e paralysis cycle** — the gifted mind sees what excellent work requires, the ADHD
  executive system cannot reliably deliver it, and the result is avoidance; procrastination
  as **self-handicapping** that protects an able identity **[CLINICAL LORE]**.
  Self-handicapping is an established construct; its application here is clinical
  description, not a tested model.

### 37.3 The impostor reframe
Clance and Imes (1978) described the **impostor phenomenon** in a clinical sample of
high-achieving women — a phenomenon, not a diagnosis **[MODERATE as a construct]**. The
source argues that **for 2e adults the self-doubt has a rational basis**: the evidence of
both exceptional competence and repeated failure on routine tasks is real, so the
"brilliant/broken" oscillation reflects an uneven profile rather than distortion
**[INTERPRETATION]** — a clinically helpful reframe, not a finding. Paula Prober's remark that
gifted people are more likely to underrate than overrate themselves is **[CLINICAL LORE]**.

### 37.4 Burnout — three constructs of very different standing
| Construct | Standing |
|---|---|
| **Autistic burnout** — pervasive long-term exhaustion, loss of function and reduced stimulus tolerance from chronic masking and expectation–ability mismatch; distinct from occupational burnout and depression (Raymaker et al., 2020, *Autism in Adulthood*) | **[MODERATE, emerging]** — defined through community-based participatory research; a fast-growing literature (the source counts at least 48 studies); 69% of autistic adults reporting at least one episode (Mantzalas et al., 2021) is **self-report survey data** |
| **"ADHD burnout"** — depletion from prolonged unstimulating demands | **[CLINICAL LORE]** — a community and clinical term, not a defined research construct |
| **"Gifted burnout"** — from multipotentiality and drive for excellence | **[CLINICAL LORE]** |

The source's **compound-burnout model** — the three mechanisms interacting, recovery needing
lower demand that ADHD resists and autism requires, each episode making the next recovery
harder — is **[INTERPRETATION]**; the escalation claim in particular is **[WEAK]**
(self-report).

### 37.5 The hyper-brain/hyper-body hypothesis
> **⚠️ GOTCHA — the "high IQ makes you sick" claim rests on a weak design.**
> **Karpinski et al. (2018, *Intelligence*)** surveyed 3,715 American Mensa members and
> reported higher self-reported rates of mood disorders, ADHD, autism, autoimmune disease
> and allergies than population averages, proposing that high ability brings
> hyperreactivity, chronic HPA activation and immune weakening.
> **[WEAK]**: self-report, a self-selected membership organization compared against
> population base rates, no control group. The mechanism is **speculative**. And it runs
> **against** cognitive epidemiology, where large population cohorts generally find higher
> measured intelligence associated with **better** health and lower mortality — consistent
> with Terman's findings. The source's suggestion of a curvilinear relation (protective at
> moderately high IQ, risky at the extremes) is a **hypothesis, not a result**.

## §38. The Interpersonal Cost of Cognitive Outliers

### 38.1 Hollingworth's "communication range"
> **⚠️ GOTCHA — the communication range is not a replicated finding.**
> Hollingworth observed that children above IQ ~160 played little with other children,
> and proposed that mutual understanding deteriorates beyond **~30 IQ points** of
> difference. The source says this "has been replicated repeatedly". ⚠️ **It has not.** It
> rests on case observations of a very small number of children **[CLINICAL LORE]**. The
> derived claims — that an IQ-140 person communicates well with the 110–170 range, or that
> only **1 in 44** people are potential intellectual peers for someone in the top 2% — are
> **arithmetic on an untested premise** **[INTERPRETATION]**. Leadership-perception data
> (§39.4) are sometimes cited as support; they measure something else.

### 38.2 Peers, acceleration and the forced-choice dilemma
- **Gross (2006, *Journal for the Education of the Gifted*)** followed 60 Australian children
  with IQ above 160 for ~20 years: drives toward peer intimacy and achievement conflicted;
  most reported isolation and frustration in inclusion classrooms; those **radically
  accelerated** reported high life satisfaction, while those held back often chose to
  underachieve for acceptance **[WEAK as a sample — small, self-selected — but the
  acceleration result agrees with a much stronger literature]**.
- **⚠️ Acceleration is among the better-supported interventions in gifted education:**
  meta-analyses find academic benefits and no average social-emotional harm *(wider
  literature, not the source)* **[ROBUST–MODERATE]**. Most of that evidence is about
  school-age students, not adults.

### 38.3 Communication mismatch
- **The curse of knowledge** (Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber, 1989) — difficulty reconstructing
  not-knowing — is **[MODERATE]** as a general effect; its specific application to gifted
  communication (faster leaps, greater abstraction, being heard as condescending) is
  **[CLINICAL LORE]**.
- **For autistic adults, first impressions are measurably less favourable**, and the
  **double empathy problem** frames autistic–non-autistic misunderstanding as mutual rather
  than an autistic deficit; information transfer is better within autistic pairs than
  across neurotypes *(wider literature: Sasson et al., 2017; Milton, 2012; Crompton et al.,
  2020)* **[MODERATE]**. This grounds the source's description of autistic directness plus
  expertise being read as arrogance — "too perceptive and too socially unaware" at once
  **[CLINICAL LORE]**.

### 38.4 Partners and friends
- **Dijkstra et al. (2012)** — gifted people prioritize intelligence in partner choice
  (similarity-attraction) **[MODERATE]**.
- **Nauta's Mensa Intimate Relationships Study (2016, *Marriage & Family Review*)** — similar
  relationship quality to controls, but less constructive conflict handling, more fearful
  attachment and lower friendship intimacy **[WEAK — Mensa sample]**.
- **Friendship scarcity** — craving depth, finding small talk unsustainable; for 2e adults
  compatibility spans intellect, neurotype and emotional style **[CLINICAL LORE]**.

## §39. Societal Context

### 39.1 SMPY and the threshold hypothesis
**The Study of Mathematically Precocious Youth** (Stanley, 1971; later Lubinski and Benbow)
tracked 5,000+ people identified before age 13. In the 40-year follow-up the top 1% earned
doctorates at ~25× the base rate and the top 0.01% at ~50×; participants collectively
produced 85 books, 7,572 refereed articles, 681 patents and $358 million in grants
(Lubinski & Benbow, 2006). **Even within the top 1%, the top quartile out-produced the
bottom quartile — evidence against the "threshold hypothesis" that ability above ~120 stops
mattering** **[ROBUST–MODERATE]**. ⚠️ Selection was on early test scores in a specific
programme, so the results speak to **predictive validity of early ability**, not to the
social worth of gifted people — the source's "disproportionate contributors to
civilization" is advocacy phrasing.

### 39.2 Policy — VERSIONED
- **US federal funding**: the Javits Gifted and Talented Students Education Act (1988) peaked
  around $11.2 million in its early era and was zeroed in the FY2011 budget. ⚠️ **The source
  stops there; Javits was restored from FY2014 at modest levels** *(wider record, not the
  source)* — check current appropriations **[VERSIONED]**.
- **State mandates**: "26 states require gifted programs and only 6 fund them" — date of the
  data not given **[VERSIONED]**.
- **International comparisons** — Singapore's universal Grade 3 screening for its Gifted
  Education Programme (announced in 2023 to be restructured; verify current form), South
  Korea's Gifted Education Promotion Act (2000) and gifted science high schools, Israel's
  testing of all students with the top ~1.5% enrolled **[VERSIONED]**. ⚠️ **Linking
  Singapore's PISA ranking to gifted screening is [INTERPRETATION]** — national test results
  have many causes.

### 39.3 Anti-intellectualism and egalitarian norms
- **Hofstadter, *Anti-Intellectualism in American Life* (1963)** — resentment and suspicion of
  the life of the mind, traced to evangelical Protestant, populist and utilitarian strands
  **[historical interpretation, not psychology]**.
- **Pew**: US trust in scientists fell from 87% to 73% between 2020 and 2023; **Motta (2024)**
  models distrust of experts feeding opposition to their policy role **[MODERATE]**. ⚠️ These
  are attitudes to **scientists**, not to gifted individuals; the bridge is **[INTERPRETATION]**.
- **Janteloven** (Sandemose, 1933), originally satire; Gullestad (2002) on the
  "inegalitarian subtext" of Nordic egalitarianism **[sociological interpretation]**.

### 39.4 The Flynn effect and its reversal
- **Flynn effect**: ~3 IQ points per decade across the 20th century in 30+ countries
  (Pietschnig & Voracek, 2015 meta-analysis) **[ROBUST]**; Flynn read it as gains in abstract
  problem-solving rather than in underlying *g*. See §14 →
  `psych-development-personality-social-clinical-and-neurodiversity`.
- **Reversal**: Bratsberg and Rogeberg (2018, *PNAS*; 736,808 Norwegian conscripts born
  1962–1991) found a decline of ~0.33 points per year, **including within families**,
  ruling out dysgenic fertility and immigration as primary causes; declines are reported in
  several other countries since the mid-1990s (Dutton et al., 2016) **[ROBUST for Norway,
  MODERATE elsewhere]**.
- **Candidate causes** — digital technology and less deep reading, environmental toxins,
  curriculum change **[WEAK / speculative]**. The specific "phthalates cost 6–8 IQ points"
  figure is **[WEAK]**, and animal findings on microplastics crossing the blood–brain barrier
  say nothing yet about human IQ.

> **⚠️ GOTCHA — "the reversal makes gifted people rarer" is a measurement error.**
> IQ is **re-normed** against the current population. By construction, roughly 2.3% score
> above 130 on any current test; the reversal changes raw performance, not the proportion
> above a norm-referenced cut-off. The source's claim that intellectual peers grow scarcer
> over time is **[INTERPRETATION]** and does not follow.

### 39.5 The social penalty for visible intelligence
- **Leadership perception — Antonakis, House and Simonton (2017, *Journal of Applied
  Psychology*)**: 379 mid-level leaders rated by 2,905 colleagues; perceived effectiveness
  followed an inverted U peaking around IQ 120 and declining above ~128 **[MODERATE]** —
  one study, **perceived** effectiveness only. The mechanism the source states (a gap beyond
  an ~18-point communication range) is Simonton's **theoretical prediction**, consistent
  with the data, not demonstrated by it.
- **Jordan v. City of New London (2000)** — an applicant rejected for a police post because
  his Wonderlic score exceeded the department's preferred range; the Second Circuit upheld
  the policy as rational **[legal fact]**.
- **Ambivalence toward intelligent targets** — a 2020 study reporting smarter targets rated
  less favourably **[WEAK — citation too vague in the source to check]**; Feather (1989) on
  tall-poppy schadenfreude toward fallen high achievers **[MODERATE]**.
- **Gender** — by age 6, girls associate "brilliance" more with males (Bian, Cimpian &
  Leslie, 2017, *Science*) **[MODERATE]**; the "38.3% fewer recommendations when a job
  mentions intellect" figure appears to come from a **separate** paper by the same group
  **[check the primary source]**. The source's "stacked penalties" for a gifted autistic
  ADHD woman is a plausible **[INTERPRETATION]**, not a measured interaction.

## §40. Families, Workplaces — and What the Evidence Supports Doing

### 40.1 Families
- **Parentification** of gifted children into problem-solver or regulator roles, with
  Hooper (2007) linking parentification generally to later complex-trauma symptoms
  **[CLINICAL LORE for gifted children]** — that literature is not about gifted samples.
  Sibling effects of the label (Colangelo & Brower, 1987) **[WEAK — old, small]**.
- **"But you're so smart"** — the assumption of effortless success that reads executive
  failure as unwillingness **[CLINICAL LORE]**: often reported, never measured.

### 40.2 Workplaces
- **Persson (2009, *Gifted and Talented International*)**, 287 Mensa members: highest job
  satisfaction among the self-employed or those with substantial autonomy; dissatisfaction
  from unchallenging tasks, unsuitable supervisors and colleague friction **[WEAK — Mensa
  sample]**.
- **Nauta and Corten (2002)** — a dual-perception chart: where the environment sees
  "arrogant" or "hard to manage", the employee experiences seeing the answer clearly and
  needing autonomy **[CLINICAL LORE — a practitioner framework]**.
- **A 2022 *Frontiers in Psychology* systematic review (40 studies)** — gifted adults have
  higher occupational status and income but report less enjoyment and meaning in work
  **[MODERATE]**.
- **The 2e inconsistency puzzle** — complex work done brilliantly, routine administration
  missed, the gap read as a moral failing **[CLINICAL LORE]**.

### 40.3 What the evidence supports doing
**⚠️ Ordered by the strength of what stands behind each item, not by appeal.**

1. **Assess the separate conditions properly** — by a clinician experienced with
   high-ability adults; ask for **index scores and the GAI**, not only a Full Scale IQ, with
   the testing **state** (sleep, depression, burnout) recorded (§32) **[ROBUST basis]**.
2. **Treat co-occurring depression and anxiety as such** — §13 →
   `psych-development-personality-social-clinical-and-neurodiversity`; "existential
   depression" or "positive disintegration" framings are not substitutes **[ROBUST]**.
3. **Change the environment before demanding more willpower** — structure, external cues,
   reduced sensory load, fewer task switches; §15's finding applied **[MODERATE–ROBUST]**.
4. **Seek autonomy and challenge at work; negotiate the routine load** **[WEAK–MODERATE]**.
5. **Seek peers who match on interest and neurotype** — supported by §38.2–§38.3; that peer
   access "transforms outcomes" for adults is **[CLINICAL LORE]**.
6. **Treat communication mismatch as two-way** — name directness as a style, check
   comprehension explicitly (double empathy) **[MODERATE]**.

**Method.** Each claim was sorted into research finding, small-sample result, clinical lore,
