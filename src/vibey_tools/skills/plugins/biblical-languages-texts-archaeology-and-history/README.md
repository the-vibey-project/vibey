# Biblical Languages, Translation, Textual Criticism, Archaeology and History Plugin

From autograph to the Bible on your shelf: the languages it was written and translated in, how
translators actually work, how textual critics decide which words are there, how archaeology and
history test what the text describes, and where — in the source's words — textual criticism,
archaeology and history all fail together when discipline slips.

One reference, split into 9 skills, so a task loads only the part it needs.

**The thesis the reference opens on**, and the reason it is one reference rather than nine essays:

> This is built as one connected system, not nine separate essays, because that is how the
> disciplines actually work. A translator cannot choose a word until a textual critic has decided
> which words are there; a textual critic weighs manuscripts that archaeologists dug up and
> palaeographers dated; historians use all of it and then argue about what it means.

- **Headings.** Section headings are written `§N Title` (the source writes `N. Title`) so pointers
  can name them. The source's `PART` heading lines are carried in each skill's banner, re-cased from
  the source's capitals, and kept as a heading only where the Part has introductory text of its own

- **The title and compiled-on note** are carried as a quotation under *About the source* in

- **Pointers.** A cross-part pointer such as "(Part III)" now also names the section and the skill
  that holds it, and §21's "(above)" — which pointed at §13, now in another skill — was replaced by
  that pointer.
- **One pack note**, in bold brackets and labelled as the pack's, at the Wycliffe figures (below).

And what the pack adds around the source's text, all of it labelled or structural: the frontmatter

## ⚠️ What this reference is and is not

**Where the source marks a claim, the pack keeps the marker exactly where the source put it;
claims the source leaves unmarked are unmarked here too.** **\[settled\]** means broad consensus across confessional and secular scholarship; **\[majority\]**
the mainstream position with a live minority dissent worth knowing; **\[contested\]** genuinely
disputed among competent specialists; **\[as of 2026\]** a status fact likely to change. Where the
source marks a question contested — the Hebrew verbal system, linguistic dating, verbal aspect, the
CBGM's details, the Iron Age chronology, Khirbet Qeiyafa, the Quirinius census, the criteria of
authenticity, the resurrection claim at the boundary of historical method — the pack presents it
as contested and does not resolve it.

**Dated facts are dated.** Edition status (BHQ fascicles, the Göttingen Septuagint, NA29, the ECM
target of 2030), translation statistics, the ESV and Catholic American Bible updates, AI drafting in
field translation, and the 2026 archaeological finds were checked against live sources on
September 30, 2026 by the source's author. They have **not** been re-checked for this pack. §38 in
`bible-reference` lists what remains open.

**Scope.** This is a survey of the disciplines — languages, translation, textual criticism,
archaeology, history and their ethics — as the source reports the state of each field. It reports
positions across confessional and secular scholarship; where it says what historians broadly agree
on (§27.5) it also says why the list is short and where agreement on facts does not settle
conclusions.

## One source figure flagged rather than silently repaired

§7.1's Wycliffe New Zealand statistics count 805 languages with a full Bible, 1,842 with only a New
Testament and 1,520 with only portions, "out of 7,393 known living languages — with 3,229 languages
having no known Scripture." The four figures sum to 7,396 — which is the Wycliffe Global Alliance
total quoted in the bullet above them — not 7,393. The source does not say which figure differs, and
the pack does not guess: all are carried exactly as printed, a pack note beside them says so, and
the source's own caution that such counts are approximate applies.
