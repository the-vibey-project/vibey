## §3 Biblical Latin

### §3.1 The Vetus Latina ("Old Latin")

Christians in the Latin-speaking West (North Africa first, then Italy, Gaul, Spain) began translating the Greek Bible into Latin in the 2nd century. The result was not one translation but a **family of independent, uneven translations**, collectively called the **Vetus Latina**. Scholars distinguish an "African" type (seen in Cyprian's quotations) and "European" types. The Old Testament Vetus Latina was translated from the **Septuagint**, not the Hebrew. **\[settled\]** It survives in manuscripts and in the quotations of Latin church fathers; the Vetus Latina Institute at Beuron (Germany) has been producing a comprehensive edition since 1949.

### §3.2 Jerome and the "Vulgate"

In 382 Pope Damasus I commissioned **Jerome** (c. 347–420) to revise the Latin Gospels against the Greek. Jerome then, on his own initiative, went much further — eventually translating most of the Old Testament **directly from the Hebrew** (*iuxta Hebraeos*), learning Hebrew from Jewish teachers in Bethlehem. That was controversial: Augustine worried Jerome was undermining the church's inspired Septuagint.

**The "Vulgate" is a composite, not Jerome's single work** **\[settled\]**:

- The **Gospels** are Jerome's revision of the Old Latin.
- The **rest of the New Testament** was revised by someone else (candidates include Rufinus the Syrian or Pelagian circles); nobody knows for sure.
- Several books were never touched by Jerome and entered the Vulgate in Old Latin form (1–2 Maccabees, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch).
- The **Psalms** are the weirdest case: Jerome made three Psalters. The *Romanum* (light revision), the *Gallicanum* (revised from Origen's Hexaplaric Greek), and the *iuxta Hebraeos* (from Hebrew). The medieval church used the **Gallican** — i.e., a Latin translation of a Greek translation of the Hebrew — because liturgy had already made it familiar. Jerome's better Hebrew-based Psalter lost.

The word *Vulgata* ("common, commonly used") only became the standard label for this Bible late; Roger Bacon in the 13th century is the earliest known to apply it this way.

### §3.3 Medieval and early-modern Vulgate history

- **Codex Amiatinus** (early 8th century, Wearmouth-Jarrow, England) — the oldest surviving complete Latin Bible and a key witness.
- **Alcuin's** revision under Charlemagne (c. 800) and Theodulf's rival edition tried to clean up corrupted texts.
- The 13th-century **Paris Bible** standardized book order and adopted the **chapter divisions** attributed to **Stephen Langton** (c. 1200s) — the chapters we still use.
- The **Gutenberg Bible** (c. 1454–55) was a Vulgate.
- The **Council of Trent** (1546) declared the Vulgate "authentic" for public doctrine — meaning doctrinally reliable, not that the original languages were superseded (a nuance often misreported).
- The **Sixtine Vulgate** (1590) was such a mess — Sixtus V reportedly edited it personally — that after his death the cardinals halted sales and later ordered copies destroyed. Its replacement, the **Clementine Vulgate** (1592), became the Catholic standard for nearly four centuries.
- **Modern critical editions:** the Oxford Vulgate (Wordsworth-White, NT), the Benedictine San Girolamo edition (OT, 1926–1995), and the one-volume **Stuttgart Vulgate** (Weber-Gryson; 5th ed. 2007) — the scholars' Vulgate.
- **Nova Vulgata** (1979; 2nd ed. 1986): the Holy See's official Latin Bible, not a Jerome edition but a revision of Jerome's Latin brought into line with modern critical Hebrew and Greek texts. The Clementine Vulgate remains in use for the 1962 Missal.

### §3.4 What makes "biblical Latin" distinctive

**Christian Latin** as a register was created partly by Bible translation:

- **Greek loanwords:** *ecclesia*, *baptizare*, *apostolus*, *evangelium*, *angelus*, *episcopus*, *presbyter*.
- **Hebraisms via Greek:** *et factum est* ("and it came to pass"), *in saecula saeculorum* ("unto ages of ages").
- **New meanings for old words:** *fides* (from "reliability/contract good faith" to "faith"), *gratia*, *salus*, *sacramentum* (originally a soldier's oath).

### §3.5 Latin renderings that changed Western history

| Passage | Vulgate | What happened |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Genesis 3:15 | *ipsa conteret caput tuum* ("**she** shall crush your head") | Hebrew has a masculine/common pronoun ("he/it"); the Latin feminine fed Marian interpretation and art |
| Exodus 34:29 | *cornuta esset facies sua* ("his face was **horned**") | Hebrew *qaran* ("shone/sent out rays") shares a root with *qeren* ("horn"); hence Michelangelo's horned Moses |
| Matthew 4:17 | *paenitentiam agite* ("**do penance**") | Erasmus pointed out the Greek *metanoeite* means "repent/change your mind"; Luther's first of the 95 Theses (1517) turned on this distinction |
| Luke 1:28 | *gratia plena* ("full of grace") | Greek *kecharitōmenē* ("favored one"); a key text in Catholic/Protestant debate |
| 1 John 5:7–8 | The *Comma Johanneum* ("three that bear witness in heaven...") | A Latin addition absent from early Greek manuscripts; entered the Textus Receptus and the KJV (Part III — §11.7 → `bible-textual-criticism`) |

### §3.6 The Latin legacy in modern Catholic translation policy

The Latin church's relationship to translation is governed by documents worth knowing: *Divino Afflante Spiritu* (1943) encouraged translation from the original languages; Vatican II's *Dei Verbum* (1965) urged translations "especially from the original texts"; *Liturgiam Authenticam* (2001) demanded close, formal translation for liturgy (requiring conformity with the Nova Vulgata in certain respects); and *Magnum Principium* (2017) returned more authority over liturgical translation to bishops' conferences.

## §4 Biblical German

### §4.1 Before German: Gothic

**Wulfila** (Ulfilas, c. 311–383), bishop to the Goths, translated the Bible into **Gothic**, inventing an alphabet to do it. The luxurious **Codex Argenteus** (6th century, silver ink on purple parchment, now in Uppsala) preserves much of the Gospels. Gothic is an East Germanic language — a cousin of German, not an ancestor — but it is the oldest substantial Germanic text of any kind, and it is a Bible.

### §4.2 Old High German and Old Saxon

The earliest German biblical texts are glosses, fragments, and adaptations:

- **Monsee Fragments** (c. 800, Matthew in Old High German).
- The Old High German **Tatian** (c. 830, Fulda): a translation of Tatian's Gospel harmony.
- **Otfrid of Weissenburg's** *Evangelienbuch* (c. 860s): a rhymed Gospel poem.
- The **Heliand** (c. 830, Old Saxon): an epic retelling of the Gospel in alliterative verse, casting Christ as a Germanic lord with his thanes.
- **Notker the German** (d. 1022): translated and commented on the Psalms.

### §4.3 Printed German Bibles before Luther

Luther was not the first — this is one of the most common misconceptions. **\[settled\]** The **Mentelin Bible** (Strasbourg, 1466) was the first printed Bible in any vernacular language. Between 1466 and 1522 there were fourteen High German and four Low German printed Bibles, alongside many German Gospel books and Psalters. Their weakness: they were translated from the Latin Vulgate in a stiff, word-for-word style, and their German was already dated — which is exactly why Luther's version swept them away.

### §4.4 Luther's Bible (the pivot point)

- **The Wartburg:** Hidden at the Wartburg castle after the Diet of Worms (1521), Luther translated the New Testament from Erasmus' Greek edition in about **eleven weeks**.
- **The "September Testament"** (Wittenberg, September 1522): a first printing of 3,000–5,000 copies sold out quickly; a second edition followed in December; pirated reprints proliferated. Before the full Bible appeared, the Deutsche Bibelgesellschaft counts 87 printings.
- **The full Bible** (1534), produced with a team — Melanchthon (Greek), Aurogallus (Hebrew), Cruciger, Bugenhagen, Jonas — who worked through the Old Testament from Hebrew.
- **The "Ausgabe letzter Hand"** (1545): the final edition Luther supervised, a year before his death; the standard for centuries.

**His translation philosophy** — the most influential statement of functional translation before the 20th century — is in his *Sendbrief vom Dolmetschen* ("Open Letter on Translating," 1530). Its famous line: you must *dem Volk aufs Maul schauen* — "look at the mouths" of the mother in the house, children in the street, the common man in the market, and translate the way they talk. The same letter defends his most notorious choice: adding *allein* ("alone") in Romans 3:28 ("justified by faith **alone**"), which is not a word in the Greek. Luther argued German idiom required it to express the sense; Catholic critics called it doctrinal tampering. It remains the classic case study in the line between translation and interpretation.

**Linguistic legacy:** Luther wrote in a form of East Central German (close to the Saxon chancery language), and the enormous circulation of his Bible helped make that variety the basis of standard written **New High German**. Many German idioms come from his phrasings (*Perlen vor die Säue werfen*, "cast pearls before swine"; *sein Licht unter den Scheffel stellen*, "hide one's light under a bushel"). The comparison usually drawn is with the KJV's influence on English.

**Official revisions \[as of 2026\]:** revisions in 1892, 1912, 1984, and 2017, with a failed 1975 New Testament revision that was criticized for drifting too far from Luther's language (the 1984 revision partly restored it). The 2017 revision ("Lutherbibel 2017") involved about 70 theologians and linguists over five years, re-checking the 1984 text against the original languages — and, notably, restoring many Luther wordings.

### §4.5 The Reformed and Catholic German traditions

- **Zürich Bible** (Zwingli and Leo Jud; complete 1531 — actually before Luther's complete Bible). Current edition: **Zürcher Bibel 2007**, a respected, fairly literal new translation.
- **Catholic:** Johann Dietenberger (1534) and Johann Eck produced Catholic responses (Dietenberger's leaned heavily on Luther's text!). Joseph Franz von Allioli's 19th-century version was long standard. The **Einheitsübersetzung** ("Unity Translation," 1980) became the official Catholic Bible for the German-speaking dioceses after Vatican II; a thorough revision appeared in 2016 (approved by Rome in April 2016), moving it closer to the original languages.

## §5 Biblical English

### §5.1 Old English (c. 700–1100)

No complete Old English Bible existed, but a great deal of Scripture was rendered:

- **Cædmon's** biblical poetry (7th century, per Bede).
- **Bede** reportedly translating John's Gospel on his deathbed (735).
- **Interlinear glosses:** Aldred's 10th-century Old English gloss written between the lines of the Latin **Lindisfarne Gospels**.
- The **Wessex Gospels** (late 10th century): the first free-standing translation of the four Gospels into English.
- **Ælfric of Eynsham** (c. 1000): translated parts of the Old Testament (the "Old English Hexateuch").

### §5.2 Middle English: the Wycliffite Bibles

The first complete English Bible is associated with **John Wycliffe** (d. 1384) and his circle. Two versions exist: an **Early Version** (c. 1380s), extremely literal from the Vulgate, and a **Later Version** (c. 1388–1395), more idiomatic, often credited to John Purvey. Both were translated from **Latin**. The church's response — Archbishop Arundel's **Constitutions of Oxford** (1409) — forbade unlicensed English translations; the Wycliffite Bible nonetheless survives in about 250 manuscripts, an extraordinary number for its time.

### §5.3 The printed Reformation Bibles

| Year | Bible | Significance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1526** | **Tyndale New Testament** (Worms; a 1525 Cologne printing was interrupted) | First English NT translated from **Greek** (Erasmus' edition) and first printed. Tyndale also translated the Pentateuch (1530), Jonah, and historical books. Executed near Brussels in 1536. |
| 1535 | **Coverdale Bible** | First complete printed English Bible (from Latin and German sources plus Tyndale). Coverdale gave us "lovingkindness" and much of the Psalter still in the Book of Common Prayer. |
| 1537 | "Matthew" Bible | John Rogers' compilation of Tyndale's full work plus Coverdale |
| 1539 | **Great Bible** | First "authorized" Bible for reading in English churches, under Henry VIII / Cromwell |
| **1560** | **Geneva Bible** | Made by Protestant exiles in Geneva; roman type, **verse numbers throughout** (using Robert Estienne's NT verse divisions of 1551), extensive (often anti-monarchical) study notes. The Bible of Shakespeare, the Puritans, and the Mayflower. |
| 1568 | Bishops' Bible | The Church of England's answer to Geneva; widely judged weak |
| 1582/1609–10 | **Douay-Rheims** | Catholic, from the Vulgate; Latinate vocabulary ("supersubstantial bread") |
| **1611** | **King James Version** (Authorized Version) | See below |

### §5.4 The King James Version: how it was actually made

- **Origin:** the **Hampton Court Conference** (1604), where the Puritan John Rainolds proposed a new translation; James I agreed, partly because he disliked the Geneva notes.
- **Organization:** Richard Bancroft, Bishop of London, recruited the scholars and issued **15 rules**. About 50 translators (54 were named; perhaps 47 worked) were divided into **six companies** — two each at Westminster, Oxford, and Cambridge — three on the Old Testament, two on the New, one on the Apocrypha.
- **Key rules:** follow the **Bishops' Bible** and alter it as little as "the truth of the original will permit"; keep traditional ecclesiastical words ("church," not Tyndale's "congregation"); **no doctrinal marginal notes** (a direct strike at Geneva); consult Tyndale, Matthew, Coverdale, the Great Bible, and Geneva where they agree better with the original.
- **Process:** each translator drafted a section; the company reviewed; drafts circulated to other companies; a final general committee resolved disputes. A few working documents survive (e.g., an annotated Bishops' Bible and John Bois' notes of the final revision meetings).
- **Tyndale's ghost:** the 1611 preface says the aim was not to make a new translation but to "make a good one better." Jon Nielson and Royal Skousen's 1998 quantitative study estimated that about **84% of the KJV New Testament and 76% of the Old Testament portions Tyndale translated** retain Tyndale's wording. **\[majority\]** (Other estimates range from two-thirds to 90% depending on method.)
- **Texts used:** for the NT, Greek editions in the Erasmus–Stephanus–Beza tradition (later called the *Textus Receptus*, Part III — §11 → `bible-textual-criticism`); for the OT, the Masoretic Hebrew of the Bomberg Rabbinic Bibles.
- **The KJV you buy is not the 1611.** Nearly every KJV printed today follows Benjamin Blayney's **1769 Oxford** standardization of spelling, punctuation, and italics. The 1611 had printing errors (the 1631 "Wicked Bible" famously omitted "not" from "Thou shalt not commit adultery").
- **Reception:** slow at first; the Geneva Bible stayed more popular for decades. The KJV's cultural dominance came in the late 17th–19th centuries.

### §5.5 The modern English translations

**The revision line (formal, KJV → RSV tradition):**

- **English Revised Version** (NT 1881, OT 1885): first official KJV revision; NT based on the new critical text of Westcott and Hort. Its American counterpart: **American Standard Version** (1901), which printed "Jehovah."
- **Revised Standard Version** (NT 1946, OT 1952): mainline Protestant; the Isaiah 7:14 "young woman" controversy made it a culture-war object.
- **New Revised Standard Version** (1989) → **NRSV Updated Edition** (NRSVue, 2021): the academic standard in most university biblical studies.
- **English Standard Version** (2001): an evangelical revision of the 1971 RSV. Crossway released text updates in 2007, 2011, 2016, and announced a limited update rolling out from spring 2025, expected to reach nearly all editions by fall 2026 — including returning Genesis 3:16 from the 2016 "contrary to" back to the 2001 rendering "for." **\[as of 2026\]**
- **New King James Version** (1982): modernized KJV, retaining the Textus Receptus.

**Independent formal-equivalence:**

- **NASB** (1971; 1995; 2020 update) — often called the most literal major English translation.
- **Legacy Standard Bible** (2021): a revision of NASB95, rendering the divine name as "Yahweh."

**Balanced / "optimal equivalence":**

- **NIV** (NT 1973, full 1978, revised 1984, 2011) — the best-selling modern English Bible; an intermediate TNIV (2005) sparked the gender-language controversy.
- **HCSB → CSB** (Christian Standard Bible, 2017; minor update 2020), produced by a trans-denominational team of 21 conservative evangelical scholars, based on BHS and NA28/UBS5.
- **NET Bible** (2005; 2nd ed. 2019): famous for about 60,000 translators' notes explaining choices — probably the best free window into how translators think.

**Functional / dynamic:**

- **Good News Bible / Today's English Version** (1966–76), Eugene Nida's school in action.
- **New Living Translation** (1996; revised 2004, 2015): a true translation (not a paraphrase like the old *Living Bible*).
- **The Message** (Eugene Peterson, 1993–2002): an idiomatic paraphrase.

**Catholic:** Jerusalem Bible (1966) → New Jerusalem (1985) → Revised New Jerusalem (2019); **New American Bible** (1970) → NABRE (2011). The US bishops have announced a revised version to be published in 2027 under a new name, the **Catholic American Bible**, intended to serve prayer, catechesis, and liturgy in one text. **\[as of 2026\]**

**Jewish:** the Jewish Publication Society's 1917 version; the **NJPS Tanakh** (1985; revised 2023 as the *JPS Tanakh: Gender-Sensitive Edition*); Everett Fox's *Schocken Bible* (Buber-Rosenzweig-style foreignizing); **Robert Alter's** *The Hebrew Bible* (2018) — a single scholar's literary translation, influential on how people think about Hebrew style.

**British/ecumenical:** New English Bible (1961/1970, a fully fresh translation) → Revised English Bible (1989).

### §5.6 The spectrum, simplified

```
MORE FORMAL ←——————————————————————————————————————————→ MORE FUNCTIONAL
Interlinear  YLT  NASB/LSB  ESV/NKJV/KJV  NRSVue  CSB  NIV  NET  NABRE/REB  NLT  GNT  CEV  Message
```

Warning: this line hides real complexity. A "literal" translation can be less *accurate* when Greek or Hebrew idiom is rendered word for word (e.g., "bowels of mercies"), and a "dynamic" one can over-interpret. Every translation sits at different points on the spectrum in different books. The honest summary: **formal translations preserve more of the source's form and ambiguity; functional translations make more decisions for you.** Serious readers use one of each.
