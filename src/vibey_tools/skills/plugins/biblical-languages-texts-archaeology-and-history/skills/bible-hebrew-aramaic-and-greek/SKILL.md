## Part I — The Languages

A note on the phrase "biblical X." Only Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek are *original* biblical languages. "Biblical Latin," "Biblical German," and "Biblical English" mean something different: the specific language registers created by translating the Bible into those tongues — registers that then reshaped those languages themselves. Treating them together is legitimate as long as you keep that distinction.

## §1 Biblical Hebrew (and Biblical Aramaic)

### §1.1 What it is

Biblical Hebrew (BH) is the Hebrew of the Hebrew Bible (the Tanakh / Christian Old Testament). It is a Northwest Semitic language, a close cousin of Phoenician, Moabite, Ammonite, and Edomite, and more distantly of Aramaic and Ugaritic. **\[settled\]**

Important framing: BH is not "ancient Hebrew" in full. It is a *literary corpus* of roughly 300,000 words, written over many centuries, then transmitted and eventually vocalized by later scribes. We only see Hebrew through this keyhole plus a modest set of inscriptions (ostraca, seals, tomb and building inscriptions) and, later, the Dead Sea Scrolls, Ben Sira, and Mishnaic Hebrew.

### §1.2 The three layers you are actually reading on a page of a Hebrew Bible

When you open a printed Hebrew Bible you are looking at three chronologically distinct layers stacked on top of each other:

1. **The consonantal text** (Hebrew *ketiv*, "what is written"). This is the old layer. Hebrew was written with consonants only, later supplemented by a few letters doubling as vowel indicators (*matres lectionis*: ה, ו, י). The "proto-Masoretic" consonantal text had stabilized by roughly the 1st century CE.
2. **The vowel points** (*niqqud*). Added by the **Masoretes** (from *masorah*, "tradition") between roughly the 7th and 10th centuries CE. Several systems existed (Babylonian, Palestinian, Tiberian); the **Tiberian** system, associated with the Ben Asher family, won.
3. **The accents** (*te'amim*, cantillation marks). These mark stress, the melody for liturgical chanting, and — crucially for translators — **syntax**: they function like a hierarchical punctuation system, telling you where the major and minor breaks in a verse fall.

Around these sit the **Masorah**: marginal notes (*Masorah parva* in the side margins, *Masorah magna* at top/bottom) counting letters and word forms, flagging unusual spellings, and warning copyists. A special category is **ketiv/qere** ("written/read"): places where the tradition preserved a consonantal form but instructed readers to pronounce something else. The most frequent is the divine name יהוה, which is not pronounced but read as *Adonai* ("Lord") — the source of English "the LORD" in small capitals.

**Why this matters for you:** vowels are interpretation. The same consonants can yield different words. A translator who departs from the Masoretic vowels (as translators sometimes do, often following the Septuagint) is not "changing the Hebrew text" in the consonantal sense — but they are overriding a millennium-old reading tradition.

### §1.3 The great codices

| Manuscript | Date | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Aleppo Codex** | c. 930 CE, vocalized in the Ben Asher tradition | The most prestigious Tiberian codex; badly damaged in the 1947 Aleppo riots — roughly 40% is now missing, including most of the Torah |
| **Leningrad Codex** (St. Petersburg, EBP. I B 19a) | 1008/1009 CE | The oldest *complete* Hebrew Bible manuscript; base text of BHS and BHQ |
| Cairo Codex of the Prophets | traditionally 895 CE (date debated) | Prophets only |
| Dead Sea Scrolls (Qumran and other Judean Desert sites) | c. 3rd century BCE – 2nd century CE | Roughly 900+ manuscripts, of which about 230 are biblical; many fragmentary. Push the attested text back about a thousand years. |

### §1.4 How the language changed over time — and the dating fight

Standard scholarship divides BH into periods: **Archaic BH** (some old poems, e.g., Exodus 15, Judges 5), **Standard or Classical BH** (much of Genesis–Kings prose), and **Late BH** (Chronicles, Ezra–Nehemiah, Esther, Daniel, Qohelet), with some scholars adding a transitional phase. The broad division is widely accepted, with the caveat that languages change gradually rather than in clean breaks. **\[majority\]**

The live controversy is whether you can **date texts by their language**. The traditional school (Avi Hurvitz, Jan Joosten, and others) says yes: Late BH features (Aramaisms, Persian loanwords, specific syntactic shifts) cluster in demonstrably post-exilic books, so a text lacking them is likely earlier. A skeptical school (Ian Young, Robert Rezetko, Martin Ehrensvärd, *Linguistic Dating of Biblical Texts*, 2008) argues that "early" and "late" features may reflect **style, dialect, or scribal choice** rather than chronology, and that textual transmission means we cannot be sure the linguistic profile of our text is the author's. Responses (Pat-El, Wilson-Wright, Dong-Hyuk Kim's sociolinguistic study, and others) have tried to rescue diachronic analysis with better methodology. **\[contested\]** This matters because linguistic dating is one of the main independent checks on literary theories about when biblical books were written.

### §1.5 Grammar essentials a serious reader should grasp

- **The root system.** Most words derive from three-consonant roots (e.g., כ־ת־ב *k-t-b*, "write"). Nouns and verbs are built by inserting vowel patterns and affixes. This makes etymological reasoning tempting — and dangerous (see "root fallacy," Part II — §6.4(b) → `bible-how-translation-is-done`).
- **Verb stems (*binyanim*).** Seven major stems modify the root meaning: Qal (simple), Niphal (often passive/reflexive), Piel (often intensive/factitive), Pual, Hiphil (causative), Hophal, Hitpael (reflexive/reciprocal). "Intensive" for the Piel is now considered an oversimplification.
- **The verbal system — a genuine unsolved problem.** BH has two basic conjugations (*qatal*, suffix; *yiqtol*, prefix) plus the narrative forms *wayyiqtol* ("and he did," the backbone of past narrative) and *weqatal*. Whether these encode **tense, aspect, mood, or discourse function** — and how much of each — is debated. Older grammars said "perfect/imperfect" (aspect); many recent approaches blend tense-aspect-mood with text-linguistic (discourse) analysis. **\[contested\]** This is not academic: it affects how you translate prophetic "prophetic perfects," poetry's tense shifts, and legal texts.
- **Construct chains.** "The word of the LORD" is expressed by juxtaposition (*d'var YHWH*), with ambiguity between "of," "belonging to," "from," "about," and adjectival senses.
- **Poetry.** Hebrew poetry runs on **parallelism** (paired lines that echo, contrast, or develop), terseness, and wordplay rather than rhyme or meter in the Greek/Latin sense. Robert Lowth (1753) named the pattern; James Kugel and Robert Alter redefined it as "A, and what's more, B" — the second line intensifies rather than repeats.
- **Hapax legomena.** Well over a thousand Hebrew words occur only once in the Bible, many of them with meanings recoverable only by comparative Semitics (Akkadian, Ugaritic, Arabic), ancient translations, or context. Every translation's footnotes that read "meaning of Hebrew uncertain" are pointing at this.

### §1.6 Biblical Aramaic

Portions of the Hebrew Bible are in **Aramaic**, the lingua franca of the Persian Empire: Daniel 2:4b–7:28, Ezra 4:8–6:18 and 7:12–26, Jeremiah 10:11, and two words in Genesis 31:47. **\[settled\]** Jesus' everyday language was almost certainly a Galilean dialect of Aramaic, which surfaces in Gospel transliterations (*talitha koum*, *ephphatha*, *Eloi Eloi lema sabachthani*). Aramaic Targums (interpretive translations of the Hebrew Bible) and the Syriac Peshitta are also key witnesses in textual criticism.

### §1.7 The scholarly toolkit

- **Critical editions:** *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia* (BHS, 1967/77, 5th corrected ed. 1997) and its successor-in-progress *Biblia Hebraica Quinta* (BHQ), which uses a diplomatic Leningrad text, an English-language apparatus, and a commentary on difficult cases. **\[as of 2026\]** BHQ is being issued book by book; nine fascicles are currently available in major software (Genesis, Leviticus, Deuteronomy, Judges, the Twelve, Job, Proverbs, the Megilloth, Ezra–Nehemiah). Two other major projects exist: the Hebrew University Bible Project (diplomatic, based on Aleppo) and *The Hebrew Bible: A Critical Edition* (HBCE, eclectic). See Part III (§12.3 → `bible-textual-criticism`).
- **Lexicons:** BDB (Brown-Driver-Briggs, 1906; dated but still used); **HALOT** (Koehler-Baumgartner); **DCH** (Clines, *Dictionary of Classical Hebrew*, includes non-biblical Hebrew).
- **Grammars:** Gesenius-Kautzsch-Cowley (GKC, 1910; classic); Joüon-Muraoka (standard reference); Waltke & O'Connor, *Introduction to Biblical Hebrew Syntax* (1990); van der Merwe, Naudé & Kroeze, *Biblical Hebrew Reference Grammar* (2nd ed. 2017; linguistically current).
- **Digital:** ETCBC/BHSA database (Amsterdam), Accordance, Logos, the Leon Levy Dead Sea Scrolls Digital Library, STEPBible.

## §2 Biblical Greek

### §2.1 Koine: the "common" Greek

Biblical Greek is **Koine** (κοινή, "common"), the international Greek of the Hellenistic and Roman periods, roughly 300 BCE–300 CE, spread by Alexander's conquests. It is simpler and more analytic than Classical Attic: fewer optatives, dual number nearly gone, more prepositions doing work case endings used to do, a flattening of certain distinctions. **\[settled\]**

The 19th-century idea that New Testament Greek was a special "Holy Ghost Greek" collapsed when Adolf Deissmann and others (c. 1890s–1900s) showed from newly found **Egyptian papyri** — letters, receipts, contracts — that the NT's vocabulary and grammar were ordinary Koine. **\[settled\]** This is a foundational example of archaeology reshaping philology.

### §2.2 Registers and Semitic influence

There isn't one "biblical Greek." There are at least two very different corpora:

- **Septuagint Greek (LXX):** translation Greek, often mirroring Hebrew word order and idiom (e.g., "and it came to pass," *kai egeneto*), sometimes very literal, sometimes free depending on the book and translator.
- **New Testament Greek:** composed mostly in Greek, but by authors steeped in the Septuagint and (for some) Aramaic. Register varies enormously: Hebrews and Luke's prologue approach literary Koine; Mark is plain and vivid; Revelation contains grammatical "irregularities" that some explain as deliberate Semitic style, others as a non-native writer.

**Semitisms** — Hebrew or Aramaic influence on NT Greek — are a major research topic: idioms like "sons of disobedience," the paratactic "and... and... and," or the heavy use of *idou* ("behold").

### §2.3 Live debates in NT Greek grammar

- **Verbal aspect.** Since Stanley Porter (1989) and Buist Fanning (1990), most scholars accept that the Greek verb's tense-forms primarily encode **aspect** (how the action is viewed: whole/perfective vs in-progress/imperfective) rather than time, with time conveyed mostly by context — at least outside the indicative. How far to push this (Porter: time is not grammaticalized even in the indicative; Fanning, Campbell, and others: more nuanced) remains **\[contested\]**. Practical fallout: the old preacher's claim that the aorist means "once for all" action is now considered a fallacy.
- **Middle voice and "deponency."** Traditional grammars labeled verbs "deponent" (middle/passive form, active meaning). A wave of work (Carl Conrad, Jonathan Pennington, Rachel Aubrey and others) argues deponency is a misreading imported from Latin grammar and the middle voice has a coherent meaning (subject-affectedness). The trend is toward abandoning the label. **\[majority, trending\]**
- **Discourse grammar.** Stephen Levinsohn, Steven Runge and others apply linguistic discourse analysis (information structure, prominence, connectives like *de* and *kai*) — now influential in translation handbooks.

### §2.4 The Septuagint (LXX)

**The legend vs the reality.** The *Letter of Aristeas* claims 72 Jewish elders translated the Torah in 72 days in 3rd-century BCE Alexandria under Ptolemy II. The Göttingen Septuagint project's own summary puts it plainly: in reality the translation took more than three centuries, running into the first century CE, and was revised repeatedly by Jews and Christians. **\[settled\]** The Torah was probably translated in the 3rd century BCE; other books followed piecemeal.

**Why the LXX matters so much:**

1. It was **the Bible of the early church** — most NT quotations of the Old Testament follow the Greek, not the Hebrew.
2. Its Hebrew **Vorlage** (the Hebrew text the translators had) sometimes differed from the Masoretic Text — e.g., the LXX Jeremiah is about one-eighth shorter and arranged differently, and Qumran fragments (4QJer^b,d) show a Hebrew text matching that shorter form. So the LXX is a witness to an alternative Hebrew text, not just a loose translation.
3. It determined the **Christian Old Testament canon** in Catholic and Orthodox traditions (books like Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, 1–2 Maccabees).

**Recensions:** The Greek text was repeatedly revised toward the proto-Masoretic Hebrew: the **kaige** revision (1st century BCE/CE, found in the Nahal Hever Minor Prophets scroll), Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion, **Origen's Hexapla** (3rd century CE, a six-column comparison), and the Antiochian/"Lucianic" text.

**Critical editions \[as of 2026\]:** Rahlfs' semi-critical *Handausgabe* (1935; revised Rahlfs-Hanhart 2006) is the everyday text. The full critical **Göttingen Septuagint** (founded by Alfred Rahlfs in 1908) is still in progress — as of early 2025, 26 of 36 planned volumes had appeared, roughly three-quarters of the project. The Cambridge "Larger Septuagint" was diplomatic (based on Codex Vaticanus) and lapsed in 1940.
