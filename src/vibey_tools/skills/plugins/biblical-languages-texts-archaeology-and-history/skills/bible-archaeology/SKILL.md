## §14 What archaeology is (and isn't)

Archaeology is the study of the human past through **material remains** — architecture, artifacts, bones, seeds, soils, residues, landscapes — and the **contexts** in which they are found. Context is everything: an object's scientific value comes mainly from knowing exactly where it lay, what was above and below it, and what was found with it. A looted object on the market has lost most of that information permanently.

**What archaeology does not do well:** "prove" or "disprove" the Bible directly. Material remains rarely speak about specific individuals, and they almost never speak about theology. What archaeology does superbly is reconstruct **settlement patterns, economies, technology, diet, trade, destruction events, religious practice, literacy, and chronology** — the world in which biblical texts were written and which they describe. Inscriptions are the exception that can name names.

## §15 A short history of "biblical archaeology"

| Period | Figures | Character |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 19th c. exploration | **Edward Robinson** (1838 survey identifying biblical sites by Arabic place names); the **Palestine Exploration Fund** (1865) | Mapping, topography, antiquarian digging |
| Scientific beginnings | **Flinders Petrie** at Tell el-Hesi (1890): recognized that a **tell** is layered and that **pottery changes over time** (seriation) | The two pillars of Levantine chronology |
| "Biblical archaeology" era | **William F. Albright** (Johns Hopkins; Tell Beit Mirsim), G. Ernest Wright, Nelson Glueck; in Israel, Yigael Yadin (Hazor, Masada) | Archaeology often framed as confirming the Bible's historical outline |
| Stratigraphic revolution | **Kathleen Kenyon** (Jericho 1952–58; Jerusalem) | Rigorous stratigraphic excavation (Wheeler-Kenyon); famously redated Jericho's walls |
| Professionalization and critique | **William Dever** ("Syro-Palestinian archaeology"), processual/"New Archaeology" influences, interdisciplinary science | Archaeology as an independent discipline with its own questions |
| Current | Israel Finkelstein, Amihai Mazar, Yosef Garfinkel, Aren Maeir, Oded Lipschits, Erez Ben-Yosef, and many others; heavy use of radiocarbon, microarchaeology, and data science | Debates now often turn on dating precision and interpretation of data |

## §16 The fieldwork cycle

### §16.1 Before digging

- **Research design:** questions, hypotheses, sampling strategy.
- **Permits and law:** in Israel, the Israel Antiquities Authority (IAA) licenses excavations; Jordan's Department of Antiquities, Egypt's Supreme Council of Antiquities, etc. Excavations in occupied territories raise specific legal and ethical issues (Part VI — §31 → `bible-forgeries-looting-and-provenance`).
- **Survey:** surface collection of pottery (to estimate periods of occupation), mapping, and remote sensing — aerial and satellite imagery, **LiDAR**, drone photogrammetry, **ground-penetrating radar**, magnetometry. Survey data underlie big-picture claims, such as population and settlement shifts in the highlands during Iron Age I (the emergence of Israel).

### §16.2 Excavation methods

- **Wheeler-Kenyon method:** Developed by Mortimer and Tessa Wheeler at Verulamium (1930–35) and refined by Kathleen Kenyon at Jericho (1952–58). The site is gridded into squares (commonly 5×5 m), with unexcavated **balks** (typically about 1 m wide) left standing between squares. The balks preserve vertical **sections** you can read and draw, tying finds to layers.
- **Open-area / architectural approaches** (the older Reisner-Fisher tradition and modern open-area excavation): remove balks to expose whole buildings and horizons. Most modern Levantine digs use a **hybrid**: grids for control, balks removed as architecture emerges.
- **Loci and baskets:** A **locus** is the basic excavation unit — a distinct deposit or feature (a floor, a fill, a wall, a pit). Finds are collected in numbered **baskets** tied to a locus and precise elevation.
- **Recording:** daily top plans, section drawings, elevations by total station or GPS, photography, now routinely **3D photogrammetry**; databases replace paper.
- **Recovery:** dry sieving, **wet sieving** (for tiny bones, beads, seals), and **flotation** (to recover charred seeds for plant and radiocarbon analysis).

### §16.3 Stratigraphy: reading the dirt

A **tell** (Arabic *tall*, Hebrew *tel*) is an artificial mound created by millennia of rebuilding on the same spot: walls collapse, debris is leveled, new buildings rise. Key principles:

- **Superposition:** in an undisturbed sequence, lower is earlier.
- **But disturbances are the rule:** **pits** and **foundation trenches** cut down through earlier layers, bringing later material down; **robber trenches** remove walls; **fills** move old debris into new places. Distinguishing a sealed floor from a fill is the craft.
- **Destruction layers** (burned debris sealing complete pots and objects in place) are gold: they freeze a moment and often can be tied to known events — e.g., Lachish Level III to Sennacherib's 701 BCE campaign, or the 586 BCE Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem.
- **The Harris Matrix** (Edward Harris, *Principles of Archaeological Stratigraphy*, 1979; 2nd ed. 1989): a diagram of stratigraphic relationships (above/below/cuts/same-as) that captures the sequence as **events**, not just horizontal layers — the standard theoretical tool.
- **Strata** are numbered per site (Megiddo VA–IVB, etc.), so "Stratum X" means nothing outside its site report.

### §16.4 After digging (where most of the work is)

Pottery reading (sorting, dating by typology), registration, restoration, specialist analyses (bones, seeds, metals, residues), and **publication**. The **publication backlog** is one of the field's chronic ethical problems: a dig destroys its own evidence as it excavates, so unpublished excavation is lost data.

## §17 Dating: how archaeologists know when

### §17.1 Relative dating

- **Stratigraphy** (above/below).
- **Typology and seriation:** artifacts (especially pottery) change style in datable sequences. Petrie's insight: a sherd can date a layer.
- **Ceramic assemblages** define periods: Late Bronze, Iron I, Iron IIA, etc.

### §17.2 Absolute dating

- **Radiocarbon (¹⁴C):** measures decay of carbon-14 in once-living material. Modern **AMS** dating needs milligrams. Raw ages must be **calibrated** against a curve (currently **IntCal20**) built from tree rings and other records.
- **Pitfalls:** the **old wood effect** (charcoal from a long-lived tree or reused beam gives dates older than the event — so short-lived samples like seeds and olive pits are preferred); **calibration plateaus**, above all the "**Hallstatt plateau**" (c. 800–400 BCE), where different real dates produce similar radiocarbon ages, blurring precisely the period of the late Judahite monarchy and exile.
- **Bayesian modeling** (e.g., OxCal) combines radiocarbon dates with stratigraphic order to narrow ranges. The Iron Age chronology debate (below) is largely a Bayesian-modeling debate.
- **Dendrochronology** (tree rings), **archaeomagnetism** (the Levant has a well-studied Iron Age geomagnetic "spike"), **OSL** (luminescence dating of sediments).
- **Historical synchronisms:** Egyptian and Assyrian chronologies (anchored by astronomical records such as the eclipse of 763 BCE in the Assyrian eponym list) provide fixed points; biblical events are tied to them via inscriptions (e.g., Shoshenq I's campaign list at Karnak; Assyrian annals naming Israelite and Judahite kings).
- **Coins** (from the Persian period on) and **inscriptions** date contexts directly.

## §18 The science toolbox

- **Zooarchaeology:** animal bones reveal diet and economy. The near-absence of **pig bones** at Iron Age highland sites is a much-discussed possible marker of Israelite identity (vs Philistine sites with pig) **\[contested as an ethnic marker\]**.
- **Archaeobotany:** seeds, pollen, phytoliths — agriculture, climate, and datable short-lived samples.
- **Residue analysis:** organic traces in vessels (wine, oils, incense, cannabis and frankincense residues on the Tel Arad shrine altars published in 2020).
- **Ancient DNA:** a 2019 study of skeletons from Ashkelon showed an early Iron Age influx of **European-related ancestry** among the Philistines, diluted within a few centuries — matching textual/material hints of Aegean origins.
- **Isotopes** (strontium, oxygen): mobility and origin of individuals and animals.
- **Petrography and chemical sourcing:** where clay, stone, or metal came from.
- **Archaeometallurgy:** the Faynan (Jordan) and Timna (Israel) copper districts, excavated by Thomas Levy and Erez Ben-Yosef, revealed large-scale 10th-century BCE copper production by largely **nomadic** societies — the basis of Ben-Yosef's argument (2019) that archaeology has an "**architectural bias**" that underestimates non-sedentary polities (with implications for Edom and early Israel). **\[contested\]**
- **Microarchaeology:** microscopic sediment analysis (e.g., Weizmann Institute) to identify floors, fires, dung, and site-formation processes invisible to the eye.

Inscriptions are where archaeology and the biblical text meet most directly. A starter list:

## §20 The big interpretive debates

### §20.1 Minimalists and maximalists (1990s–2000s)

The "**Copenhagen school**" and allied scholars (Niels Peter Lemche, Thomas L. Thompson, Philip Davies, Keith Whitelam) argued that the Hebrew Bible is largely a Persian- or Hellenistic-era literary creation with little reliable history about the monarchy — "minimalism." Critics (William Dever, Kenneth Kitchen, Baruch Halpern, many Israeli archaeologists) argued for more historical kernel — "maximalism," though few accept the label. The Tel Dan inscription's discovery landed in the middle of this fight. Today the extreme positions have receded; debate focuses on specific periods and evidence. **\[field has moved to the middle\]**

### §20.2 The Iron Age chronology debate (David and Solomon)

In 1995–96, **Israel Finkelstein** proposed a **Low Chronology**, lowering the dates of key Iron I–IIA strata by about 60–100 years. The implication: monumental structures once attributed to Solomon (e.g., the Megiddo, Hazor, and Gezer gates and palaces) belong to the 9th-century Omride dynasty of the northern kingdom, and 10th-century Judah was a small highland chiefdom. **Amihai Mazar** proposed a **Modified Conventional Chronology** (Iron IIA roughly 980–840/830 BCE), which overlaps with both. The debate drove large-scale radiocarbon projects in Iron Age archaeology; it now turns largely on Bayesian models, sample selection (short-lived vs charcoal), and the stratigraphic security of samples. Finkelstein himself has written that the gap between his system and the "reasonable voice" of the traditional camp is narrowing, but that the dispute is far from resolved. **\[contested\]**

**Khirbet Qeiyafa** (excavated 2007–2013 by Yosef Garfinkel and Saar Ganor): a fortified early-10th-century BCE town overlooking the Elah Valley, with a casemate wall, two gates, and an ink-inscribed ostracon. Garfinkel argues it shows a Judahite state administration in David's time; others identify it with Philistine, Canaanite, or northern Israelite polities, or accept the date while disputing the interpretation. **\[contested\]**

### §20.3 Exodus, conquest, and the emergence of Israel

- **Jericho:** John Garstang (1930s) dated a destroyed wall to Joshua's era; Kathleen Kenyon's stratigraphic work redated it to the Early Bronze Age — nearly two thousand years earlier — and found little Late Bronze occupation. Bryant Wood's 1990 reassessment argued for a later destruction date; most archaeologists follow Kenyon's general picture. **\[majority\]**
- **Models of Israel's emergence:** (1) **conquest** (Albright), (2) **peaceful infiltration** of pastoralists (Alt, Noth), (3) **peasant revolt** (Mendenhall, Gottwald), (4) **gradual emergence** from within Canaanite society, supported by highland survey data showing hundreds of small new villages in Iron I (Finkelstein, Dever, and others). The majority of archaeologists hold some form of (4), often with (2), and with debate over how much memory of an Egyptian sojourn the Exodus traditions preserve. **\[majority/contested\]**
- A minority of evangelical archaeologists (e.g., the Associates for Biblical Research, excavating at Shiloh and Khirbet el-Maqatir) argue for a 15th-century BCE conquest ("early date") — a position not accepted in mainstream academic archaeology. **\[minority\]**

### §20.4 Jerusalem

Jerusalem is continuously occupied, holy to three faiths, and politically contested — archaeologically among the hardest sites on earth. The debate over the size and nature of 10th-century Jerusalem (Eilat Mazar's "Large Stone Structure," claimed as David's palace; skeptics date parts later or read it differently) continues. **\[contested\]** Excavations in the **City of David**, largely funded by the Elad foundation in the Palestinian neighborhood of Silwan, are simultaneously productive and politically fraught (Part VI — §31 → `bible-forgeries-looting-and-provenance`).

## §21 What's new in 2026 (a snapshot) **\[as of 2026\]**

- **Burnt wooden roof beams from the 586 BCE Babylonian destruction** found in the Givati Parking Lot excavation in the City of David, preserved beneath melted plaster; their tree rings may allow unusually precise radiocarbon work.
- **The first known mold for Judean pillar figurines** (First Temple period) found in the Tower of David excavations in July 2026 — evidence such figurines, sometimes linked to the goddess Asherah, were manufactured in Jerusalem.
- A **standing stone concealed at Tel 'Eton**, argued in a 2026 study to reflect Hezekiah's cultic reforms. **\[new; interpretation debatable\]**
- **Nineveh's Shamash Gate:** new excavation after ISIS-era looting tunnels, publishing evidence of the city's 612 BCE destruction (arrowheads, skeletons, burn layer).
- **Codex H** multispectral recovery (§13 → `bible-textual-criticism`).
- The **Great Isaiah Scroll** went on display in its entirety for the first time since 1968 (reported February 2026).
- A 2026 *Levant* study reassigning a fortress to the Kingdom of Israel in the early 8th century BCE rather than to the Assyrians; a Persian-period mass infant burial at Tel Azekah.

## §22 How to read an archaeology headline (checklist)

1. **Is it peer-reviewed?** Press releases, museum announcements, and ministry websites often precede (or replace) publication.
2. **Who funded and who is interpreting?** Not disqualifying, but relevant.
3. **Is the object from a controlled excavation, or the market?** Unprovenanced = suspect (Part VI — §29–§30 → `bible-forgeries-looting-and-provenance`).
4. **What is the dating basis** — stratigraphy, pottery, radiocarbon (which samples? how modeled?), palaeography?
5. **Is the biblical link a discovery or an interpretation?** "Evidence of King X" usually means "a find consistent with a period in which the Bible places X."
6. **What do other specialists say?** Look for responses in *BASOR*, *Tel Aviv*, *IEJ*, *Levant*, *Radiocarbon*, *NEA*.
7. **Beware of both hype directions:** "Archaeology proves the Bible!" and "Archaeology disproves the Bible!" are both headline genres, not scholarly conclusions.
