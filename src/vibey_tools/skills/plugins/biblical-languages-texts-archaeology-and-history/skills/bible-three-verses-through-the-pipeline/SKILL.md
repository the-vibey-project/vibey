## Part VII — Three Verses Through the Whole Pipeline

These case studies show how all five disciplines interact on a single text.

## §32 Case 1 — Isaiah 7:14 ("the *'almah* shall conceive")

1. **Language (Hebrew):** The word *'almah* denotes a young woman of marriageable age; Hebrew has a different word, *betulah*, often (though not exclusively) used for "virgin." *'Almah* does not in itself specify virginity or its absence. **\[majority\]**
2. **Textual criticism:** The Hebrew text is secure — the Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa^a, c. 2nd century BCE) and the Masoretic Text agree on the key word.
3. **Septuagint:** The Greek translator rendered *'almah* with *parthenos* (usually "virgin," though it can mean a young unmarried woman).
4. **New Testament:** Matthew 1:23 quotes the **Greek** form, applying it to Mary's virginal conception.
5. **History:** The oracle is set in the **Syro-Ephraimite crisis** (c. 734–732 BCE), when Aram and Israel pressured King Ahaz of Judah. Assyrian records of **Tiglath-pileser III** list "Jehoahaz \[Ahaz\] of Judah" among tribute payers — archaeology independently corroborating the historical setting.
6. **Translation:** The KJV and most evangelical translations (NIV, ESV, CSB) render "virgin," often with a note; the RSV (1952) rendered "young woman," provoking public Bible-burnings in some churches; the NRSVue, NET, and Jewish translations (NJPS) have "young woman." Each choice encodes a view of how Isaiah's text relates to Matthew's reading of it.
7. **Lesson:** The dispute is not primarily about manuscripts but about **lexicography, the relationship between the Hebrew text and the LXX, and hermeneutics** (how a NT author reads an OT text).

## §33 Case 2 — Deuteronomy 32:8 ("according to the number of the sons of ...")

1. **Textual criticism:** The Masoretic Text reads "sons of **Israel**"; a Qumran manuscript (4QDeut^j) reads "sons of **God**" (*bene 'elohim*), and the Septuagint tradition reflects a similar reading ("angels/sons of God"). By the principle of the harder reading, scholars widely judge "sons of God" earlier — a later scribe more plausibly changed it to avoid implications of a pantheon than the reverse. **\[majority\]**
2. **Archaeology:** Texts from **Ugarit** (Ras Shamra, discovered in 1928; 14th–13th century BCE) describe the high god El presiding over a council of divine "sons" — providing the ancient Near Eastern conceptual background.
3. **History of religion:** The verse fits the divine-council imagery also seen in Psalm 82; it is central to scholarship on the development of Israelite monotheism.
4. **Translation:** ESV and NRSVue follow "sons of God"; NIV and some others keep "sons of Israel" with a footnote.
5. **Lesson:** The Dead Sea Scrolls did not just confirm the Masoretic Text; in places they **changed** which reading scholars print.

## §34 Case 3 — Luke 2:1–2 (the census "when Quirinius was governor of Syria")

1. **Textual criticism:** The text is stable — this is not a manuscript problem.
2. **Translation:** Most versions read "the **first** registration, when Quirinius was governor." A minority suggest *prōtē* can mean "**before**" ("before Quirinius was governor"), a rendering noted in some footnotes. **\[minority\]**
3. **History:** Josephus dates Quirinius' census of Judea to **6 CE**, after Archelaus was deposed; Matthew and Luke place Jesus' birth under Herod the Great, who died in **4 BCE** on the usual reckoning (1 BCE on a minority reckoning). Proposed resolutions include an earlier governorship or special role for Quirinius, a registration begun earlier and completed later, the alternative translation above, or simply that Luke erred. **\[contested\]**
4. **Archaeology/epigraphy:** Egyptian papyri document household-registration censuses and return-to-residence requirements in Roman Egypt; a fragmentary Tivoli inscription (*Lapis Tiburtinus*) mentioning a man who governed Syria twice has been claimed for Quirinius, but the name is not preserved. Coins and inscriptions help fix Herod's reign and Roman governors.
5. **Lesson:** Some problems are historical, not textual or linguistic; each discipline has to state what it can and cannot settle.
