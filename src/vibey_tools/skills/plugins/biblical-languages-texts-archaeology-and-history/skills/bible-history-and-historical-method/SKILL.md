## §23 What history is

**The past** is everything that happened. **History** is the disciplined, evidence-based reconstruction and interpretation of the past — always partial, always revisable, always argued. Historians do not "report" the past; they make **inferences** from surviving traces (texts, objects, landscapes, bodies, data) to claims about what happened, why, and what it meant.

Three features distinguish history from mere chronicle:

1. **Evidence-dependence:** every claim should be traceable to sources, openly cited.
2. **Critical handling of sources:** no source is taken at face value; each is interrogated.
3. **Argument and explanation:** history explains causes, contexts, and meanings, and does so in public, contestable arguments.

## §24 How the discipline developed (the ideas that still matter)

| When | Who | Contribution still in use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5th c. BCE | **Herodotus**, **Thucydides** | Inquiry (*historia*); Thucydides' critical stance toward sources and his explicit method statement |
| 2nd c. BCE | **Polybius** | Pragmatic, causal history; the historian should have political/military experience and visit the places |
| 14th c. | **Ibn Khaldun** (*Muqaddimah*) | Social and economic causes; systematic skepticism of implausible reports |
| **1440** | **Lorenzo Valla** | Proved the *Donation of Constantine* a forgery through **philology** (anachronistic Latin) — the founding demonstration that language dates documents |
| 1681 | **Jean Mabillon**, *De re diplomatica* | **Diplomatics**: rules for authenticating charters by script, formulae, seals — the birth of scientific document criticism |
| 19th c. | **Leopold von Ranke** | Archival research; history "as it essentially was" (*wie es eigentlich gewesen*); the seminar method |
| 1889 / 1898 | **Ernst Bernheim**; **Langlois & Seignobos** | Handbooks codifying **external** and **internal criticism** (below) |
| 1929– | **Annales school** (Marc Bloch, Lucien Febvre, Fernand Braudel) | Social, economic, and environmental history; Braudel's *longue durée*; Bloch's *The Historian's Craft* (posthumous) |
| 1946 | **R. G. Collingwood**, *The Idea of History* | History as answering questions; the historian "re-enacts" past thought |
| 1961 | **E. H. Carr**, *What Is History?* | Facts are selected by historians; history as a dialogue between present and past |
| 1973 | **Hayden White**, *Metahistory* | Historical writing uses narrative/literary structures ("emplotment") — the postmodern challenge |
| 1970s– | Microhistory (Carlo Ginzburg, *The Cheese and the Worms*), social history "from below," gender history, global history, **memory studies**, digital history | Broadening whose past counts and how evidence is read |

The postmodern critique sharpened historians' awareness of narrative and perspective without abolishing the discipline's commitment to evidence; most working historians today practice a chastened realism. **\[majority\]**

## §25 The historian's method, step by step

### §25.1 Heuristics: finding sources

Archives, libraries, excavation reports, databases, collections of inscriptions and papyri, oral histories. For ancient history, the corpus is **small and largely fixed**, so progress often comes from new finds (papyri, inscriptions, scrolls) or new questions asked of old sources.

### §25.2 Types of sources

- **Primary** (produced at or near the time: documents, letters, inscriptions, artifacts) vs **secondary** (later analyses) vs **tertiary** (reference works).
- **Narrative sources** (histories, chronicles — written to tell a story) vs **documentary sources** (receipts, laws, letters — produced for practical purposes).
- **Intentional vs unintentional evidence.** Marc Bloch prized "witnesses in spite of themselves": what a source reveals unintentionally (assumptions, incidental details) is often more reliable than what it sets out to tell you.

### §25.3 External criticism (is it what it claims to be?)

The classic handbooks broke source criticism into questions: **when** was it produced (date), **where** (localization), **by whom** (authorship), **from what** earlier material (analysis of sources), **in what original form** (integrity — this is where *textual criticism* sits), and **with what evidential value** (credibility). The first group is external criticism; the last is internal criticism. Tools: palaeography, diplomatics, codicology (the physical book), materials science, radiocarbon, linguistic dating, provenance research.

### §25.4 Internal criticism (can we believe it?)

- **Competence:** Was the author in a position to know (eyewitness, informed, contemporary)?
- **Sincerity/interest:** What were the author's motives, biases, audience, and genre conventions?
- **Genre:** A royal victory inscription, a hymn, a legal text, and a biography make claims in different registers. Misreading genre is the most common error in using ancient sources — including biblical ones.
- **Corroboration:** Are there **independent** sources that agree? (Dependence must be established or ruled out — two sources copying a third are one witness, exactly as in textual criticism.)
- **Plausibility:** Does the claim fit what else we know?

### §25.5 Synthesis: arguing to conclusions

Historians generally reason by **inference to the best explanation** (abduction). C. Behan McCullagh's *Justifying Historical Descriptions* (1984) formalized criteria for the best hypothesis: explanatory **scope**, explanatory **power**, **plausibility**, being **less ad hoc**, fitting better with accepted beliefs, outstripping rivals on these criteria. Some philosophers of history (Aviezer Tucker) model historiography in explicitly **Bayesian** terms; Bayesian arguments are also used (and disputed) in biblical history debates.

### §25.6 Characteristic errors

- **Anachronism / presentism** — reading modern categories into the past.
- **Argument from silence** — "X isn't mentioned, so X didn't happen." Valid only when a source *would be expected* to mention X and survives fully enough to check.
- **Parallelomania** — Samuel Sandmel's 1962 term for overstating similarities between texts or traditions and inferring dependence too quickly.
- **Confirmation bias** and **cherry-picking**.
- **Single-source overreliance** and **counting sources without testing their independence**.
- **Consensus counting** — citing "most scholars" as evidence rather than engaging the arguments (and misstating the consensus).

## §26 The special problems of ancient history

- **Scarcity and survival bias:** most ancient writing is lost. Tacitus' *Annals* 1–6 survive in a single medieval manuscript; much of Livy is gone. We see the ancient world through elite, male, literate, urban, and often victorious eyes.
- **Late manuscripts:** almost all classical texts survive in medieval copies centuries removed from their authors — the same textual-criticism problems as the Bible, with far fewer witnesses.
- **Numbers and rhetoric:** ancient authors routinely inflate army sizes, invent speeches (Thucydides says so openly), and follow genre conventions foreign to modern history-writing.
- **Integrating material and textual evidence:** archaeology and texts answer different questions at different resolutions; forcing them to "match" is a classic error in both directions.

## §27 History applied to the Bible: the historical-critical toolkit

### §27.1 The main methods

| Method | Question | Landmark figures |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Source criticism** | What earlier written sources did the author use? | Pentateuch: Documentary Hypothesis (J, E, D, P) — Julius Wellhausen (1878/1883); now heavily revised (many European scholars work with P / non-P models and later datings: Erhard Blum, Reinhard Kratz, Konrad Schmid). Gospels: the **Synoptic Problem** |
| **Form criticism** | What oral forms (genres) lie behind units, and what setting in life (*Sitz im Leben*)? | Hermann Gunkel (OT), Rudolf Bultmann and Martin Dibelius (NT) |
| **Tradition history** | How did traditions develop over time? | Gerhard von Rad, Martin Noth |
| **Redaction criticism** | How did editors/authors shape sources to their purposes? | Günther Bornkamm, Hans Conzelmann, Willi Marxsen |
| **Social-scientific criticism** | How do honor/shame, kinship, patronage, economy illuminate texts? | Bruce Malina, Wayne Meeks, the Context Group |
| **Literary / narrative criticism** | How do texts work as literature? | Robert Alter, Meir Sternberg, Adele Berlin |
| **Canonical criticism** | How do texts function in the final form of the canon? | Brevard Childs, James Sanders |
| **Reception history** | How have texts been read and used? | A major 21st-century growth area |
| **Memory studies** | How do communities remember, shape, and transmit the past? | Jan Assmann; in Jesus research: Chris Keith, Anthony Le Donne, Rafael Rodríguez |

### §27.2 The Synoptic Problem

Matthew, Mark, and Luke share extensive material, often verbatim. Leading solutions:

- **Two-Source Hypothesis:** Mark came first; Matthew and Luke used Mark plus a lost sayings source **Q**. **\[majority\]**
- **Farrer Hypothesis:** Markan priority, no Q — Luke used Matthew (Austin Farrer; Mark Goodacre, *The Case Against Q*, 2002). **\[significant minority, growing\]**
- **Matthean Posteriority** (Mark → Luke → Matthew) and the **Griesbach/Two-Gospel Hypothesis** (Matthew first, Mark last) have smaller followings.

### §27.3 The quests for the historical Jesus

| Phase | Characteristics |
| --- | --- |
| **First (Old) Quest** (Reimarus, published by Lessing 1774–78; D. F. Strauss 1835; liberal lives of Jesus) | Ended by **Albert Schweitzer's** *Quest of the Historical Jesus* (1906), which showed each "life of Jesus" looked like its author, and that Jesus was an apocalyptic prophet alien to modern liberalism |
| **"No Quest"** | Bultmann: the historical Jesus is largely inaccessible and theologically unnecessary |
| **New (Second) Quest** (Ernst Käsemann's 1953 lecture) | Renewed interest using form-critical **criteria of authenticity** |
| **Third Quest** (1980s–) | Jesus in his Jewish context: E. P. Sanders, John P. Meier, N. T. Wright, Dale Allison, Paula Fredriksen, Geza Vermes; also the **Jesus Seminar** (Robert Funk, John Dominic Crossan), known for voting with colored beads |
| **Memory/"post-criteria" turn** (2010s–) | Critique of the criteria and a shift to social memory, gist, and the Gospels as receptions of Jesus |

### §27.4 The criteria of authenticity — and their critique

The traditional criteria: **multiple attestation** (a tradition in independent sources), **dissimilarity** (unlike both Judaism and the early church — so not invented by either), **embarrassment** (awkward material the church would not invent — e.g., Jesus' baptism by John), **coherence** (fits the "authentic" core), and **Semitic/Palestinian features**.

Critique: **Morna Hooker** argued in 1970–72 that the criteria were the "wrong tool," imported from form criticism (designed to analyze literary units, not establish history), and that dissimilarity creates a Jesus artificially cut off from Judaism and his followers. The critique crested with ***Jesus, Criteria, and the Demise of Authenticity*** (Chris Keith and Anthony Le Donne, eds., 2012), whose contributors variously argued the criteria should be abandoned or significantly recast. Alternatives include **social memory** approaches (the Gospels as memories shaped by communities — the question becomes why Jesus was remembered this way), **gist memory** reasoning (Dale Allison, *Constructing Jesus*, 2010: trust recurrent patterns more than individual sayings), and **eyewitness testimony** arguments (Richard Bauckham, *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses*, 2006). **\[contested — the field is mid-transition\]**

### §27.5 What historians broadly agree on about Jesus (and why the list is short)

Across confessional lines, most critical historians accept that Jesus of Nazareth existed; was a Galilean Jewish teacher and healer/exorcist in reputation; was baptized by John; gathered disciples; spoke of the kingdom of God; caused a disturbance in the Temple; and was crucified under Pontius Pilate around 30 CE; and that his followers soon proclaimed he had been raised. **\[settled/majority for the core\]** The "Jesus myth" (non-existence) position is rejected by nearly all specialists. Beyond this core, interpretations diverge sharply.

The resurrection claim sits at the boundary of historical method: historians can study the **beliefs** and **reported experiences** of early Christians, and arguments are offered that the resurrection best explains them (the "minimal facts" approach). Whether a miracle can ever be the *best historical explanation* depends heavily on prior commitments (Ernst Troeltsch's principles of criticism, analogy, and correlation; methodological naturalism vs openness to the supernatural) — so agreement on the facts does not settle the conclusion. **\[contested at the level of method, not only evidence\]**

## §28 How scholarly consensus is formed — and how to read it

- **Peer review** in journals (*JBL*, *NTS*, *VT*, *JSOT*, *BASOR*, *IEJ*, *Tel Aviv*) and academic presses.
- **Professional societies:** Society of Biblical Literature (SBL), Society for New Testament Studies (SNTS), International Organization for the Study of the Old Testament (IOSOT), American Schools of Overseas Research / ASOR, Evangelical Theological Society (ETS), Catholic Biblical Association.
- **Institutional plurality:** secular universities, divinity schools, seminaries, and yeshivas. Each has pressures — confessional commitments in some, skeptical group-norms in others. The most reliable conclusions are the ones **shared across** these communities.
- **Reading a consensus claim responsibly:** ask *which* scholars (specialists in that exact subfield?), *how measured* (surveys are rare; "most scholars" is usually an impression), and *what arguments* support it. A consensus is strong evidence but not an argument by itself.
