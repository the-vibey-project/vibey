# Wood and Metal Shop Craft: The Two Materials Compared, Shop Safety, Reading Wood, and Stock Selection and Preparation

> **Part 1 of 5** of the *Working with Wood and Metal* reference (plugin `working-with-wood-and-metal`), covering §0–§4. Sibling skills: `shop-woodworking-tools-sharpening-joinery-and-finishing` (§5–§12), `shop-metalwork-cutting-drilling-and-machining` (§13–§18), `shop-welding-forging-heat-treatment-and-shop-setup` (§19–§26), `shop-reference` (§27–§32). Section numbers are shared across the set; a reference written as §N → `skill` points into that sibling skill.
>
> **Currency:** The craft is centuries old. Two areas moved. See §27 → `shop-reference` for US table saw safety regulation, and welding fume exposure control.

> **⚠️ Two materials that punish opposite mistakes.** ⚠️ **Wood MOVES forever and forgives
> imprecision; metal is dimensionally stable and forgives nothing.** **⚠️ Wood is worked
> with the grain and cut fast; metal is worked against resistance and cut slowly.**
> **The habits that make you good at one will actively hurt you at the other.**
>
> **Complements a manufacturing reference (industrial process and tolerancing), a
> materials/furniture reference (wood movement and finishing at the design level), and a
> thermodynamics reference (heat treatment physics).**
>
> **⚠️ SAFETY IS §2 AND IT IS FIRST FOR A REASON.** ⚠️ **This document describes techniques;
> it does not substitute for supervised instruction on machinery. The table saw, angle
> grinder and welding arc each injure large numbers of people every year, and the
> mechanisms are specific and learnable.**
>
> **⚠️ GOTCHA** boxes mark the habits that cause injuries and the received wisdom that's
> wrong.
>
> **The three ideas that organize this document:**
> 1. **⚠️ SHARP IS SAFE** (§7 → `shop-woodworking-tools-sharpening-joinery-and-finishing`). **A dull tool requires force, force removes control, and
>    loss of control is how people get hurt. Sharpening is not a side skill — it's the
>    gateway to everything else.**
> 2. **⚠️ The material is not neutral** (§3, §14 → `shop-metalwork-cutting-drilling-and-machining`). **Wood grain direction, and metal's
>    heat and work-hardening behaviour, determine technique. Fighting the material is
>    the definition of doing it wrong.**
> 3. **⚠️ The invisible hazards are the ones that get you** (§2, §26 → `shop-welding-forging-heat-treatment-and-shop-setup`, §27.2 → `shop-reference`). **Wood dust
>    and welding fume are classified carcinogens with no warning symptoms — and they're
>    the risks hobbyists systematically ignore in favour of the dramatic ones.**

## §1. The Two Materials Compared

```
                    WOOD                      METAL
STABILITY      ⚠️ moves with humidity      ⚠️ stable; moves with heat
DIRECTIONALITY ⚠️ ANISOTROPIC (grain)      mostly isotropic
CUTTING        fast, low force             ⚠️ slow, high force, heat
TOLERANCE      ⚠️ ±0.5 mm is often fine    ⚠️ ±0.02 mm is routine
JOINING        glue is stronger than wood  ⚠️ welding melts the parent
MISTAKES       ⚠️ often recoverable        ⚠️ removed metal never returns
HAZARD         ⚠️ dust, kickback, blades   ⚠️ fume, heat, sparks, swarf
```
**⚠️ The habit transfer that hurts**: ⚠️ **woodworkers coming to metal push too hard and too
fast and burn tools; metalworkers coming to wood ignore grain direction and tear out.**
**⚠️ In both, the fix is the same — let the tool cut at its own rate.**

## §2. ⚠️ Shop Safety

> **⚠️ Read this before anything else. The injuries in this domain are predictable and
> the mechanisms are specific.**
```
⚠️ THE ACUTE HAZARDS
   ⚠️ TABLE SAW KICKBACK and blade contact (§9) — ⚠️ the single most
      injurious woodworking machine
   ⚠️ ANGLE GRINDER — ⚠️ disc shatter, kickback, and the sparks it
      throws are burning metal that starts fires metres away
   ⚠️ ENTANGLEMENT — ⚠️ NO gloves near rotating machinery (lathes,
      drill presses); no loose sleeves, no jewellery, tie back hair.
      ⚠️ Gloves on a lathe is how people lose hands
   ⚠️ ARC EYE — ⚠️ UV burns the cornea, symptoms appear HOURS later,
      and BYSTANDERS are exposed too (§19)
   ⚠️ HOT METAL looks identical to cold metal
   ⚠️ SWARF and chips — ⚠️ never clear by hand; use a brush, machine off
⚠️ THE INVISIBLE HAZARDS — the ones people ignore
   ⚠️ WOOD DUST is a classified carcinogen (⚠️ hardwood dust
      particularly, associated with nasal and sinus cancers) and a
      respiratory sensitizer. ⚠️ The FINE fraction you can't see is
      the dangerous part
   ⚠️ WELDING FUME — ⚠️ IARC Group 1 carcinogen; hexavalent chromium
      from stainless is severely regulated (§27.2)
   ⚠️ GALVANIZED metal releases zinc oxide when heated — metal fume fever
   ⚠️ NOISE — cumulative and irreversible. ⚠️ Hearing protection every time
   ⚠️ SOLVENTS and finishes (see a materials reference on oily rags —
      ⚠️ linseed and tung oil rags SPONTANEOUSLY COMBUST)
⚠️ THE HIERARCHY (see a civil/industrial reference)
   ⚠️ ELIMINATE the hazardous operation > SUBSTITUTE a safer process >
   ENGINEERING CONTROLS (guards, extraction, riving knife) >
   ADMINISTRATIVE (procedure, training) > ⚠️ PPE LAST
   ⚠️ PPE is the WEAKEST control and it is what hobbyists reach for first
```
**⚠️ The habits that actually prevent injury**: ⚠️ **never reach over or behind a spinning
blade; use push sticks and hold-downs; unplug before changing blades or bits; keep the
floor clear; work at a pace that permits thinking; ⚠️ and stop when tired, because
fatigue-driven shortcuts are the common precondition to most shop injuries.**
**⚠️ Fire**: ⚠️ **have an extinguisher; understand that grinding sparks smoulder in sawdust
and rags for a long time before igniting; and never grind near flammables.**

# PART I — WOOD

## §3. Reading Wood

**⚠️ Wood is a directional, hygroscopic, biological material, and every technique follows
from that.**
```
⚠️ GRAIN DIRECTION governs everything. ⚠️ Planing "uphill" against
   the grain TEARS OUT; planing downhill cuts cleanly. ⚠️ Read the
   grain on the edge before you cut
⚠️ MOVEMENT  ⚠️ wood expands and contracts across the grain with
   humidity, FOREVER. Tangential movement is roughly 2× radial,
   which is why flatsawn cups and quartersawn stays flat
   ⚠️ Never fix a wide solid panel rigidly across its grain
⚠️ EARLYWOOD vs LATEWOOD  soft and hard bands; ⚠️ this is why ring-porous
   species like oak plane and finish differently from diffuse-porous
⚠️ FIGURE  curl, quilt, birdseye, crotch — ⚠️ beautiful and usually
   accompanied by wild grain that tears out under a plane
⚠️ DEFECTS  knots (⚠️ hard, and they deflect blades), checks, shakes,
   wane, ⚠️ REACTION WOOD (moves unpredictably — reject it)
```
**⚠️ Moisture content is the number that matters**: ⚠️ **acclimatize stock to the shop and
to the destination before working it; kiln-dried to roughly 6–8% for heated interiors.**
**⚠️ A cheap moisture meter prevents expensive failures.**
**⚠️ Hardwood vs softwood is a botanical distinction, not a hardness one** — ⚠️ **balsa is a
hardwood and yew is a softwood.** **Use the Janka scale for actual hardness.**

## §4. Stock Selection and Preparation

**⚠️ Board defects to check before buying**: ⚠️ **cup, bow, crook and TWIST — and twist is
the worst because it costs the most thickness to remove.**
**⚠️ Sawing patterns**: **flatsawn (cheap, wide figure, moves most), quartersawn (⚠️ stable,
ray fleck in oak, expensive), riftsawn.**
**⚠️ The four-square process is the foundation of accurate work:**
```
⚠️ 1. ONE FACE FLAT (jointer or hand plane)
⚠️ 2. ONE EDGE SQUARE to that face
⚠️ 3. SECOND FACE PARALLEL (thicknesser/planer)
⚠️ 4. SECOND EDGE PARALLEL (table saw)
⚠️ Everything downstream depends on this. Skipping it means every
   joint fights you
```
**⚠️ Sheet goods**: **plywood (⚠️ dimensionally stable — which is why casework uses it),
MDF (⚠️ flat and its dust is particularly fine and hazardous), particleboard, and veneered
panels.**
