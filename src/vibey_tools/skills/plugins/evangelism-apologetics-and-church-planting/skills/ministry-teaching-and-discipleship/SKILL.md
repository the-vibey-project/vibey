## The framing for Part IV

Christian teaching is the **formation of disciples through intentional instruction** in Scripture,
theology and Christian living. The source's claim is that the pedagogy evidence bears on this
directly, and it states four things up front:

- **Learning is a change in long-term memory, not performance during a lesson.**
- **Working memory is tiny.** (The source gives no capacity figure, and none is supplied here.)
- **Prior knowledge is the great multiplier.**
- **Things that feel like good learning usually are not** — fluency, familiarity and ease are
  systematically misleading, for the teacher as much as the student.

From which it draws its teaching prescription: the most effective teaching is **explicit, structured
and rich in worked examples, with guidance that fades as competence grows.**

Two honest boundaries before any of that is used. First, the source names no studies anywhere in this
part, so nothing here should be quoted as though it carried a citation; §18 separates the claims that
are well-replicated from the ones that are practitioner judgement. Second, the evidence that exists is
about **instruction** — getting content into durable memory and skill into practice. Discipleship as
the source itself defines it is more than that: character formation, habit, and relational attachment.
The pedagogy literature does not reach most of that, and should not be made to pretend it does.

## §16 Small Groups: Facilitation and Questioning

Small groups are the primary context for Christian formation in most churches. They are where people
ask questions they would not ask in a sermon, build the relationships that sustain faith, and apply
Scripture in community. The sermon is the other half of that (§12–§15 → `ministry-preaching`), and it
is the half that cannot take questions.

### The facilitator serves the process, not the outcome

The source's governing principle: **the facilitator serves the group's process, not the outcome.**
Holding both roles at once — running the discussion *and* advocating a position in it — is a conflict,
and it should be **named or separated**. Named means saying out loud that you are stepping out of the
chair to argue a case; separated means someone else runs the discussion.

This is the same structural problem the ethics section handles for evangelistic conversation: an
influence that depends on the other person not noticing the mechanism is a different thing from one
that survives being described. A facilitator steering toward a predetermined conclusion while
presenting as neutral fails that test. See the disclosure test at §5 → `ministry-evangelism-ethics`.

> **THE MOST VALUABLE MOVE — DECIDE HOW YOU WILL DECIDE BEFORE DISCUSSING**
>
> Is this a **study group** where the leader teaches, a **discussion group** where the leader
> facilitates, or a **dialogue group** where everyone contributes equally? **Ambiguity about the
> format is the cause of most small group frustration.** People arrive with different formats in
> their heads and read the same evening as a lecture that invited too little, or a free-for-all that
> taught too little. Settle it before the first question, not after the third disagreement.

| Format | Who carries the content | The leader's job |
|---|---|---|
| Study group | The leader | Teach it, then check that it landed — with specific questions, not a show of hands |
| Discussion group | The group, with the leader steering | Facilitate: draw people in, keep the text in view, park tangents |
| Dialogue group | Everyone equally | Contribute as one voice among several, and protect the equality that makes it a dialogue |

*(The three formats are the source's; the third column applies its own facilitation guidance to each.)*

### The before / during / after discipline

| Phase | What it covers |
|---|---|
| **Before** | Purpose, desired output, the right people, and **material circulated in advance**. The group should know what they are studying and why before they arrive. |
| **During** | Start on time. **Manage airtime actively** — the loudest voice is not the best signal, and quiet people often have the insight that matters. Go round to draw people in. **Park tangents visibly.** **Surface disagreement rather than smoothing it** — conflict is not failure, it is what happens when people who care about different things engage together. Name the dynamic when the dynamic is the problem. |
| **After** | Decisions, owners and next steps, **circulated**. A group meeting with no recorded action usually did not have one. |

"Surface disagreement rather than smoothing it" is the same conviction the church-planting material
applies at congregational scale — conflict addressed early and handled consistently, rather than kept
quiet (§19–§22 → `ministry-church-planting`).

### Questioning

The purpose of questioning is to **find out what people actually think** and to help them think more
deeply — which means **sampling widely, not accepting the first hand up**.

> **DOES EVERYONE UNDERSTAND? SAMPLES CONFIDENCE, NOT UNDERSTANDING**
>
> Confidence is miscalibrated, so the answer to that question tells you almost nothing. Ask instead a
> **specific question that reveals whether the concept was grasped**. This is the small-group form of
> the failure the preaching section calls the curse of knowledge: what is obvious to you is not a
> guide to what is obvious to them, and introspection will not tell you the difference
> (§13 → `ministry-preaching`).

**Wait time.** About **three seconds** of silence after a question dramatically improves the length
and quality of answers — the source calls it one of the cheapest available improvements. Treat the
number as a working rule of thumb rather than a constant: the direction of the effect is the robust
part, the source cites no study, and the classroom research this figure comes out of is not the same
setting as an adult small group on a Tuesday night.

| Question type | The source's example | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Open** | What does this passage tell us about God? | Generates discussion |
| **Specific** | What does verse 4 mean by justification? | Checks understanding |
| **Follow-up** | Can you say more about that? / Where do you see that in the text? / Does anyone see it differently? | Keeps an answer from dying on arrival, and sends the group back to the text rather than to opinion |

Use both of the first two. Silence and open questions are also the core listening technique in §2 →
`ministry-evangelism-persuasion-and-listening`, for the same reason: people fill silence with what
they actually think. And when an objection surfaces in a group, the stated objection is often not the
real one — that diagnostic is §11 → `ministry-apologetics-evil-objections-and-worldview`.

## §17 Discipleship, the Discipleship Process and Spiritual Disciplines

Discipleship is the **lifelong process of growing in Christlikeness through teaching, modelling,
practice and community**. It is more than information transfer: **character formation, habit
development, and relational attachment to Christ and his people**.

> **THE GOAL IS CHRISTIANS WHO CAN FEED THEMSELVES**
>
> Explicit instruction works for novices, but **as knowledge grows, guidance should fade**.
> Discipleship is a **fading-guidance process** — the goal is mature Christians who can feed
> themselves, **not perpetual dependents on the pastor**. A ministry whose people still need the
> pastor for everything after ten years has not been discipling; it has been performing.

### The four-stage process

| Stage | What happens | What the learner needs at this stage |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Proclaim** (evangelism) | The gospel is presented. This is the entry point. | A presentation (§3–§4 → `ministry-evangelism-methods-and-presentations`) and an invitation that survives the disclosure test (§5 → `ministry-evangelism-ethics`) |
| **2. Establish** (foundation) | New believers are grounded in the basics: the gospel, Scripture, prayer, community, and the core practices of Christian life. | **Explicit, structured, guided instruction.** This is where it is most needed. **Novices need clear teaching, not open-ended exploration.** |
| **3. Equip** (training) | Believers are trained in ministry skills: interpreting Scripture, sharing the gospel, serving others, leading. | **Worked examples and fading guidance — model the skill, do it together, then let them do it independently.** |
| **4. Multiply** (reproduction) | Mature believers disciple others. The goal is not self-perpetuation but multiplication — **disciples who make disciples who make disciples**. | Real responsibility with real support; the same ladder the planting material describes (§22 → `ministry-church-planting`) |

This is a **practitioner model**, widely used, and the source presents it as a process rather than as
a measured developmental sequence. Read it that way: it is a useful ordering of what to teach whom,
not a finding that people move through four discrete stages. The pedagogy claim inside it — that
guidance should fade as expertise grows — is the part with an evidence base behind it.

### Spiritual disciplines

Spiritual disciplines are **practices that open us to God's transforming work**. The source is
explicit that they are **not works that earn God's favour but means of grace through which God changes
us** — a theological claim, standing within the tradition the reference is written from, not an
empirical one, and it carries the weight of the whole section. Detach the practices from it and
discipline becomes performance.

The core disciplines the source names, all nine:

**Scripture reading and study, prayer, worship, fasting, silence and solitude, confession, service,
generosity, and fellowship.**

The pedagogy principle applies here as everywhere else in this part. Disciplines must be:

- **Taught explicitly** — not just assumed. Most people have never been shown how.
- **Modelled** — not just described.
- **Practised in community** — not just individually.

## §18 Teaching That Works With How People Learn

> **LEARNING IS A CHANGE IN LONG-TERM MEMORY**
>
> **Performance during a lesson is not learning.** A group that nods and says "that makes sense" has
> not necessarily learned anything — they are experiencing **fluency (the feeling of familiarity),
> not mastery**. Learning requires **retrieval, repetition and application over time**. A Bible study
> that is interesting on Sunday and forgotten by Monday **has entertained but not discipled**.

| What works | What does not work |
|---|---|
| **Review previous material at the start of each session** — short, spaced review | **Lecture without interaction** |
| **Worked examples** — show how to interpret a passage, then have the group interpret a similar one | **Covering too much material** (working memory is tiny — three main points, not ten) |
| **Example-problem pairs** — study one passage together, then have the group work a parallel passage | **Assuming that hearing equals learning** |
| **Check understanding with specific questions**, not "does everyone understand?" | **Discovery learning for novices** — beginners need explicit guidance, not open-ended exploration |
| **Feedback that is specific, task-focused and actionable**, with **time and opportunity to act on it** | **The feedback sandwich** — people learn the pattern and discount the praise as packaging. Be direct, be kind, and let praise be real praise at another time |

"Three main points, not ten" is the same working-memory constraint that makes three points a preaching
cliché for a good reason (§12 → `ministry-preaching`).

### How firmly does each of these stand?

These are empirical claims and they do not all carry the same weight. Scoped honestly:

| Claim | Standing |
|---|---|
| Performance during a lesson is not learning | **Well-supported.** The distinction between performance in the moment and durable retention is one of the sturdier results in the learning literature, and it is the load-bearing claim of this whole section. |
| Learning *is* a change in long-term memory | **A working definition, not a finding.** It is adopted from instructional-design research precisely because it forces the distinction above. It is also deliberately narrow — it says nothing about motivation, disposition or affection, which is most of what formation is. Useful as a discipline on teaching; wrong as a whole account of discipleship, and the source's own definition of discipleship agrees. |
| Spaced review and retrieval build durable memory | **Among the better-replicated findings** in the field, across ages and materials. |
| Worked examples and example-problem pairs | **Well-supported**, principally in well-structured domains where a correct procedure exists. Applying it to interpreting a passage is a reasonable analogy the source draws, not a result anyone measured on Scripture. |
| Specific, task-focused, actionable feedback | **Well-supported**, with the strong caveat that feedback people get no chance to act on does little — which is why the source pairs it with time to act. |
| Working memory is tiny; prior knowledge is the great multiplier | **Well-supported qualitatively.** Exact capacity figures are contested; the source gives none and neither does this skill. |
| About three seconds of wait time | **Direction robust, figure a rule of thumb.** No citation is given, and the setting it comes from is not an adult small group. |
| Discovery learning fails novices | **Genuinely contested.** The strong version — that minimal-guidance instruction leaves beginners worse off than explicit instruction — has substantial support and is a live position in education research, not a fringe one. It is also disputed, mostly over scope: how novice is novice, and whether guided inquiry counts as the thing being criticised. Present it as the source's position with that argument acknowledged, not as settled. |
| The feedback sandwich teaches people to discount praise | **Practitioner observation.** The advice that flows from it — be direct, be kind, praise separately and genuinely — is sound regardless, but the mechanism is asserted rather than evidenced here. |

The pattern worth carrying: what the evidence supports best is the **unglamorous** half — spacing,
retrieval, worked examples, specific checks, feedback you can act on. What feels like good teaching —
a smooth session, a room that nods, material covered — is exactly what the source warns is
systematically misleading.
