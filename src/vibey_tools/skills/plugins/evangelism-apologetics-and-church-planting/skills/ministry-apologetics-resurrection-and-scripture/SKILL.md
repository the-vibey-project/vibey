These two sections do two different jobs, and conflating them is the most common way the argument is
misused. **§7 argues that the resurrection is the best explanation of facts a skeptic can be brought
to grant. §8 argues that the documents reporting it have reached us substantially intact.** Neither
substitutes for the other, and neither is a proof: a reliable text can report something that did not
happen, and a true event can be reported in a corrupted text.

> **THESE ARE HISTORICAL ARGUMENTS, NOT DEMONSTRATIONS**
>
> Everything in §7 operates at the standard the source itself names: **historical probability, not
> mathematical certainty**. That is the correct standard for a historical question, and it is also a
> real limit. Each piece here is contested by competent scholars, and the argument is presented
> throughout the reference as *cumulative* — no single move carries the weight
> (§6 → `ministry-apologetics-arguments-for-god`). An apologist who overstates the certainty of this
> material hands the skeptic a fair objection for free, and the overstatement is usually what the
> listener remembers.

## §7 The Resurrection: Minimal Facts, the Alternatives, and the Cumulative Case

The resurrection is the central apologetic claim of Christianity. The approach is to establish
certain historical facts that are widely accepted by New Testament scholars — **including skeptical
and non-Christian scholars** — and then argue that the resurrection is the best explanation of those
facts.

### §7.1 What the minimal-facts approach is, and why it is built this way

The design of the argument is its main strength. Rather than starting from the inspiration or
inerrancy of Scripture — which the skeptic does not grant and which would make the argument circular
— it starts from a deliberately **small** set of claims that a non-believing historian has
independent reason to accept, and asks what explains them. The Christian and the skeptic are then
arguing about explanation, not about authority. That design also fixes the argument's ceiling: it can
only ever deliver *the best available explanation of a short list*, and it establishes nothing by
itself about the rest of the New Testament.

### §7.2 The three facts

| # | The fact | What the source offers in support |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Jesus died by crucifixion** | Multiple independent attestation in Roman and Jewish sources — Tacitus, Josephus, the Talmud — and acceptance by virtually all critical scholars. The Roman executioners were professional killers who knew when a victim was dead. |
| 2 | **The tomb was empty, and the disciples believed they had seen the risen Jesus** | The empty tomb is attested by multiple independent sources and is part of the earliest resurrection proclamation (1 Corinthians 15:3-5, dated to within 5 years of the events). It was not challenged by the Jewish authorities, who claimed the disciples stole the body — implicitly confirming the empty tomb. The disciples' experiences are multiply attested and transformed them from fearful deserters into bold proclaimers willing to die for their testimony. |
| 3 | **Paul's conversion and James's conversion** | Paul was a persecutor of the church who became its greatest missionary after an encounter he understood as the risen Christ. James, Jesus's skeptical brother, became a leader of the Jerusalem church after an encounter he understood as the risen Christ. Both conversions are psychologically difficult to explain apart from a genuine experience. |

The **1 Corinthians 15:3-5 dating is the load-bearing item** in the whole argument. It is what makes
the legend objection hard: a proclamation traceable to within five years of the events leaves very
little room for a story to grow and displace living eyewitnesses. If you remember one thing from §7,
remember that, and remember that it is doing most of the work.

### §7.3 What "widely accepted" does and does not mean

> **THE CONSENSUS CLAIM IS PART OF THE ARGUMENT, SO CHECK IT**
>
> "Widely accepted by scholars across the theological spectrum" is not a neutral preamble — it is a
> premise, and the argument is only as strong as it is. The three facts are not equally well placed.
> Death by crucifixion is about as close to uncontested as ancient history gets. The disciples'
> sincere belief that they had seen the risen Jesus, and the conversions of Paul and James, are also
> very broadly granted, because they are claims about what people *believed*, not about what
> occurred. **The empty tomb is the weakest of the group** and is the one a critical scholar is most
> likely to decline. The source's Fact 2 bundles the empty tomb together with the disciples'
> experiences into a single item, and that fusion is where the criticism lands: it lets the strength
> of one claim carry the other. State the empty tomb as its own claim and defend it separately.

### §7.4 The criticisms the approach actually faces

The minimal-facts approach is a **specific apologetic method with named critics in the scholarly
literature**, not a neutral summary of the historical record. The source document does not name them
and this pack will not supply names it was not given — but you should know the shape of the
criticism before you use the argument in public, and you should read the critical literature
directly rather than through a proponent's summary of it. The recurring lines of objection:

- **The consensus is selected.** Critics argue that the survey of scholarship behind "widely
  accepted" is assembled by proponents and weighted toward scholars already committed to the
  conclusion, and that the empty tomb in particular does not have the support claimed for it.
- **"Best explanation" smuggles in a prior.** Whether a miracle can be the best historical
  explanation depends on how probable you thought a miracle was before looking at the evidence.
  Two people can agree on all three facts and still disagree, rationally, because they started from
  different priors. The argument does not settle that disagreement; it relocates it.
- **The alternatives are assessed singly.** The table in §7.5 refutes each rival explanation on its
  own. Critics point out that a combination — say, a misremembered burial location plus grief
  experiences plus later elaboration — is not tested anywhere in that table, and that ruling out five
  clean alternatives is not the same as ruling out every alternative.
- **"Died for their testimony" is doing more work than the evidence supports.** The inference that
  people do not die for what they know to be false is sound as far as it goes; the detail of who died
  for what, and with what opportunity to recant, rests on later sources.

None of this makes the argument worthless. It makes it an argument — one a thoughtful skeptic can
resist without irrationality. Say so plainly; it costs you nothing you actually had.

### §7.5 Explaining the facts

| Alternative explanation | Problems |
|---|---|
| **Swoon theory** (Jesus didn't really die) | Contradicts expert Roman execution; a beaten, crucified, stabbed Jesus reviving in a cold tomb and convincing disciples he was the conqueror of death is less plausible than the resurrection itself. |
| **Hallucination theory** | Group hallucinations of the same type are psychologically unprecedented; the disciples were not psychologically disposed to hallucinate a resurrection they didn't expect; it doesn't explain the empty tomb; it doesn't explain Paul or James. |
| **Legend / theory developed later** | The resurrection proclamation is dated to within 5 years of the events (1 Cor 15:3-5), too early for legend to develop and replace eyewitness testimony; the Gospels contain details that are counterproductive if fabricated (women as first witnesses, in a culture where women's testimony was inadmissible). |
| **Theft / conspiracy** | The disciples were willing to die for this claim — people die for things they believe are true, but not for things they know are false; it doesn't explain Paul or James; it requires explaining the guard at the tomb. |
| **Cognitive dissonance** | Doesn't explain the empty tomb; doesn't explain why the *specific* belief was resurrection (not, say, spiritual vindication or parousia); doesn't explain the conversions of Paul and James. |

Use this table as the source gives it, and resist the urge to strengthen any row. Each entry is a
genuine difficulty for the alternative it addresses; none is a refutation a specialist would call
decisive on its own. The "psychologically unprecedented" claim about group hallucinations in
particular is an empirical claim about a literature — do not assert it more confidently than you can
defend it.

### §7.6 The cumulative case

> **THE CUMULATIVE CASE**
>
> The resurrection argument is cumulative: each alternative explanation fails to account for all
> three facts simultaneously, while the resurrection accounts for all of them. The standard of proof
> is not mathematical certainty but **historical probability** — and in history, the best explanation
> is the one that accounts for the widest range of evidence with the fewest ad hoc assumptions. On
> that standard, the resurrection is the best explanation of the established facts.

Two things follow. **Do not present any single row of §7.5 as a knockout**: the argument's own
structure puts the weight in the aggregate, and over-claiming on one row invites the listener to
dismiss the aggregate with it. And the "fewest ad hoc assumptions" criterion is what your
interlocutor is most likely to contest — whether "God raised him" counts as few assumptions or as one
enormous one is exactly what is under dispute. Name that rather than gliding past it.

### §7.7 Using this in a real conversation

The historical case is a removal of obstacles, not a conversion technique. Three practical rules:

- **Find out whether this is the actual objection.** The stated objection is usually not the real one
  (§11 → `ministry-apologetics-evil-objections-and-worldview`), and a forty-minute case for the empty
  tomb aimed at someone whose real difficulty is suffering or a church injury is a well-argued answer
  to a question nobody asked (§2 → `ministry-evangelism-persuasion-and-listening`).
- **The disclosure test applies to argument too.** If you would be uncomfortable telling the person
  what you were doing with a line of evidence, do not use that line (§5 → `ministry-evangelism-ethics`).
  Overstating a scholarly consensus to win a point is manipulation with footnotes.
- **Concede what is genuinely contested.** Conceding the contested points early buys you the
  credibility to press the ones that hold, and it is the honest description of the state of play.

## §8 The Reliability of the New Testament

The source's claim is that **the New Testament is the best-attested document from the ancient
world**, on three grounds: the number of Greek manuscripts, the earliness of the earliest of them,
and the number of early translations (Latin, Syriac, Coptic) — which together far exceed any other
ancient text.

### §8.1 Manuscript attestation

| | New Testament | Homer's *Iliad* |
|---|---|---|
| Greek manuscripts | Over 5,800 | About 600 |
| Gap, composition to earliest copy | Fragments within decades of the originals; complete codices within 300 years | About 400 years |
| Early translations | Latin, Syriac, Coptic | — |

### §8.2 Textual variants

The textual variants are numerous — **about 400,000 across all manuscripts** — but the vast majority
are trivial spelling differences or word-order changes that do not affect meaning. Scholars can
reconstruct the original text with **over 99% confidence**, and **no Christian doctrine depends on a
disputed reading**.

The 400,000 figure is large because the manuscript count is large: more copies means more copying
differences, so the same abundance that produces the attestation argument also produces the variant
count. That is worth saying out loud, because "400,000 errors in the Bible" is a common form of the
objection and the number in it is the apologist's own.

### §8.3 Authorship and dating

The source dates the Gospels **within the lifetime of eyewitnesses**:

| Text | Date given |
|---|---|
| Mark | around 55–65 AD |
| Matthew and Luke | around 60–80 AD |
| John | around 80–95 AD |
| The accepted Pauline epistles | the 50s AD — within 20 years of the crucifixion |

By ancient historical standards this is extraordinarily early, and the Pauline dating is the firmest
item in the table — close to scholarly common ground, and what anchors the 1 Corinthians 15:3-5 point
in §7.2.

The Gospel dates are another matter. **These are one dating scheme, at the early end of a contested
range**, and Mark in the 50s is an early-dating position rather than a settled result. Quote it as a
range, say it is a range, and do not build an argument that collapses if Mark is twenty years later.
The Pauline dating carries the early-proclamation point on its own.

### §8.4 Internal evidence of truthfulness

The Gospels include material that is **counterproductive if fabricated**:

- The disciples' repeated failures and misunderstandings.
- Jesus's cryptic and difficult sayings.
- The women as first witnesses, in a culture where women's legal testimony was not accepted.
- Jesus's cry of dereliction — "My God, why have you forsaken me?"

These details suggest the writers were **recording what happened, not constructing a legend** — the
reasoning being that an author inventing a founding story does not invent the parts that embarrass
the founders. It is a real and widely used historical consideration, and a probabilistic one: it
raises the cost of the fabrication hypothesis rather than closing it, since an author can have
reasons for an awkward detail that are invisible at this distance.

### §8.5 What the reliability case establishes — and what it does not

> **TRANSMISSION IS NOT VERACITY**
>
> The manuscript argument in §8.1–§8.2 establishes that **what the authors wrote is what we now
> read** — that the text has been transmitted with very high fidelity. It does not establish that
> what the authors wrote is true. A well-copied document is still only as good as the people who
> wrote it. Apologists slide from "reliable text" to "reliable history" constantly, and it is the
> easiest move for a well-read skeptic to catch. Keep them separate: §8.1–§8.2 answers "has it been
> changed?", §8.3–§8.4 asks "were the authors close enough and candid enough to be worth taking
> seriously?", and §7 argues the historical question itself. Three claims, three arguments.

The same discipline applies to the "over 99%" figure — a claim about reconstructing **wording**, not
about the truth of the content — and to "no doctrine depends on a disputed reading", which is about
doctrinal load-bearing, not about whether any passage is disputed. Both are defensible as stated, and
both become indefensible the moment they are stretched.
