**The organising idea: the method is a fit question, not a preference question.** Six approaches are
in common use and the source tabulates all six, but the comparison is not a ranking exercise with a
winner. Each approach buys something and costs something, the source names non-scalability outright
in two of the six rows, and the only one it describes as effective at scale spends the trust the
others build. The same shape repeats one level down in §4: four gospel presentations, each a
compression of the same content, each dropping something different. The decision in both halves is
the same — match the tool to the person and the relationship you actually have, and know what you are
giving up.

## §3 Methods and Approaches Compared

An evangelistic *approach* is an answer to the question of how the gospel travels from one life into
another's hearing — through a friendship, through a visible life, through a place, through a
conversation, through an event, or through met need. The source names six and compares them on
description, strengths and limitations.

| Approach | Description | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Relational / Friendship | Share the gospel through ongoing authentic friendship | Highest conversion effectiveness; trust already built; permission earned | Slow; requires genuine investment; cannot be scaled through programs |
| Lifestyle / Witness | Let a transformed life provoke questions; answer when asked | Authentic; no pressure; sustainable as a daily practice | Can become passive; words must eventually accompany works |
| Missional Living | Embed in the culture as a Christian presence; engage the needs of the community | Engages the unchurched in their context; holistic; breaks the sacred/secular divide | Requires long-term commitment to a place and people; results slow |
| Conversational / Dialogical | Two-way dialogue; explore questions together; respect their journey | Honours the person; listens before speaking; adaptable | Can lose gospel clarity; can become relativistic if not grounded |
| Proclamation / Event | Preach the gospel to groups; crusades, events, street preaching | Clear proclamation; reaches many; historically effective at scale | Impersonal; reduced trust; best as one layer among many |
| Service / Justice | Meet physical and social needs as the context for sharing the gospel | Demonstrates the gospel's power; addresses the whole person; builds credibility | Service can become an end in itself; needs verbal proclamation |

### Reading the table

- **The first column is a relationship, not a technique.** Relational, lifestyle and missional differ
  mainly in the unit they operate on — a friendship, a visible life, a place. Conversational is a
  posture that can sit inside any of the first three. Proclamation and service are the two that can
  be run as programs, and they are the two whose limitations name the same gap: words without
  relationship, and relationship without words.
- **The limitations column is two different kinds of claim.** *Slow*, *cannot be scaled*, *requires
  long-term commitment*, *results slow* and *impersonal* are structural facts you can observe.
  *Words must eventually accompany works*, *needs verbal proclamation* and *can become relativistic
  if not grounded* are theological judgements held inside the source's tradition. Both belong in the
  table; they are not the same sort of statement, and a reader from another tradition may weigh the
  second kind differently.
- **"Highest conversion effectiveness" is the strongest empirical claim here, and the source gives no
  figure for it.** What it rests on is the observation carried in §1 → `ministry-evangelism-persuasion-and-listening`
  that most people who come to faith do so through a trusted relationship. That is an observation
  about *where* conversions happen, not a controlled comparison between methods — the people reached
  relationally are not a random sample. Treat it as a strong prior with weak measurement, and do not
  attach a number to it that the source does not supply.
- **What relational evangelism actually buys is permission.** The table's second strength — *trust
  already built* — is the competence/reliability/care decomposition of §1 cashed out: the friendship
  did the work of earning the right to be heard before the conversation started.
- **Only proclamation is described as effective at scale, and its limitation is exactly what it
  spends.** Proclamation reaches many and reduces trust. The source names non-scalability outright in
  two of the other rows — relational *cannot be scaled through programs*, missional *requires
  long-term commitment to a place and people* — and credits none of the other five with reach. A
  ministry built only on the scaling method is buying reach with the asset the others produce, which
  is why the source puts proclamation "as one layer among many" rather than as a strategy on its own.
- **The final criterion is about the evangelist, not the hearer.** The method should match the
  relationship and context — and the failure mode the source names is choosing by the evangelist's
  own comfort, which is a fact about the evangelist rather than about the person in front of them.

> **THE INTEGRATED APPROACH**
>
> No single method is sufficient. The most effective evangelism combines: a life that provokes
> questions (lifestyle), relationships that build trust (relational), service that demonstrates the
> gospel's power (justice), words that explain the gospel clearly (proclamation), and answers to
> honest objections (apologetics). The method should match the relationship and context, not the
> evangelist's preference.

The five parts of that combination are not all in this section. Lifestyle and relational rest on the
listening and trust material of §1–§2 → `ministry-evangelism-persuasion-and-listening`; service and
proclamation are in the table above and, for the public setting, in §12–§15 → `ministry-preaching`;
answers to honest objections are the whole of Part II, §6–§11, across
`ministry-apologetics-arguments-for-god`, `ministry-apologetics-resurrection-and-scripture` and
`ministry-apologetics-evil-objections-and-worldview`. Read the callout as an argument against
specialising, not as a sixth method.

## §4 Gospel Presentations

A gospel presentation is a structured way of communicating the core Christian message. The source
sets three requirements: it should be **clear enough to be understood by someone with no church
background**, **faithful to the biblical gospel**, and **adaptable to the conversation**. Those pull
against each other, which is why there is more than one presentation in use.

### The four elements common to faithful presentations

| Element | What it must carry |
|---|---|
| God's character | Holy, loving, creator |
| Humanity's condition | Created for relationship, broken by sin, separated from God |
| Christ's work | Incarnation, sinless life, substitutionary death, bodily resurrection |
| The response required | Repentance and faith, **not merely intellectual assent** |

Two notes on that list. The fourth element is the one most often lost in compression — a
presentation that secures agreement and stops there has produced intellectual assent, which the
source explicitly says is not the response required. And the third element states *substitutionary
death* as part of the common floor: that is a particular account of the atonement, asserted here
rather than argued for, and a reader whose tradition words the atonement differently should know the
claim is being carried as a premise.

### The four common presentations

| Presentation | What it is | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Roman Road | A sequence of verses from Romans that trace the gospel | Simple, memorable, Scripture-based | Can feel disconnected from the larger biblical narrative |
| The Bridge to Life | A diagram: God on one side, humanity on the other, a gap caused by sin, and the cross as the bridge | Visual, intuitive, works well in conversation | Can reduce the gospel to a transactional diagram if not accompanied by the larger story |
| The Four Spiritual Laws (Campus Crusade/CRU) | God's plan and purpose; humanity's problem (sin); God's provision (Christ); our response | Structured, tested, available in many languages | Can feel formulaic; reflects a systematic theology that not all traditions share |
| The Story (Creation–Fall–Redemption–Restoration) | The gospel as the arc of the biblical narrative: God created a good world, humanity fell into sin and brokenness, God redeemed through Christ, and Christ will restore all things | Works with postmodern audiences who think in stories rather than propositions; connects individual salvation to the larger story of what God is doing in the world | Longer; requires more biblical literacy from the presenter |

The Roman Road's sequence, as the source gives it:

- **Romans 3:23** — all have sinned
- **Romans 6:23** — the wages of sin is death
- **Romans 5:8** — God demonstrates love in Christ's death
- **Romans 10:9** — confess and believe
- **Romans 10:13** — whoever calls on the Lord will be saved

### What each one costs

- **Every presentation is a compression, and the limitation column is the list of what each one
  drops.** The Roman Road drops the narrative frame. The Bridge drops the story around the
  transaction. The Four Spiritual Laws drop tradition-neutrality. The Story drops brevity and shifts
  the cost onto the presenter.
- **The source judges The Story "the most biblically grounded and narratively compelling."** That is
  the source's judgement, made from a narrative-theology preference, not a measured result — carry it
  as the source's verdict rather than as a finding. The trade underneath it is real in both
  directions: the two shortest presentations are the ones usable in a five-minute conversation, and
  the one the source prefers is the one that asks the most of the person delivering it.
- **Presenter literacy is a hard constraint, not a soft one.** A long presentation carried
  inaccurately is worse than a short one carried accurately; the limitation "requires more biblical
  literacy from the presenter" is a selection criterion about you, not a caveat.
- **The Four Spiritual Laws' limitation is unusually candid and worth keeping.** A presentation that
  encodes a systematic theology is doing doctrinal work in the background while presenting itself as
  a neutral summary. That is a reason to know which one you are using and why, not a reason to
  discard it.

> **ADAPT THE PRESENTATION, NOT THE MESSAGE**
>
> The gospel message does not change, but the presentation must adapt to the person. A postmodern
> skeptic needs a different entry point than a churched-but-unconverted person. Someone from a Muslim
> background needs different framing than someone from a secular Western background. The Story
> approach works well across cultures because every culture has a creation-fall-redemption narrative
> structure. But the core content — God, sin, Christ, response — is non-negotiable.

Which presentation fits is downstream of what you actually heard, which is the listening material of
§1–§2 → `ministry-evangelism-persuasion-and-listening`; the source is blunt there that most
evangelistic conversations fail because the evangelist was answering questions no one was asking.
*How* the presentation is delivered — whether the invitation engages reasoning or manufactures
pressure — is governed by the disclosure test in §5 → `ministry-evangelism-ethics`, and a
presentation is not exempt from it because its content is true. Objections raised in response belong
to §6–§11, starting at §6 → `ministry-apologetics-arguments-for-god`, with the discipline of finding
the objection behind the stated one at §11 → `ministry-apologetics-evil-objections-and-worldview`.
Terms used here without definition are defined in §23 → `ministry-reference`.

## Choosing in practice

1. **Find out what the conversation is before choosing anything.** The one-sentence question of §2 —
   help thinking, or listening — decides whether a presentation belongs in this conversation at all.
2. **Match the method to the relationship you have, not the one you wish you had.** A proclamation
   move inside a friendship and a friendship move inside an event both misread the context.
3. **Do not deliver a presentation to answer a question that was not asked.** That is the source's
   named failure mode, and no amount of fidelity in the presentation fixes it.
4. **Order the four elements however the person can hear them, then check all four are present.**
   The response element is the one compression drops first.
5. **Use the longest presentation you can carry accurately, not the longest one you admire.**
6. **Check the delivery against the disclosure test before you use it** (§5 →
   `ministry-evangelism-ethics`), especially where there is a power asymmetry.
7. **Name the limitation you are living with.** Every choice in both tables leaves one open; the
   integrated approach is a portfolio that covers other people's limitations, not a way of escaping
   your own.
