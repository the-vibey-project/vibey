## §9 The problem of evil

The problem of evil is **the most powerful objection to theistic belief** and the one that resonates
most deeply with most people. Do not treat it as one argument. It is two, they have different
strengths, and they take different answers.

| Form | The claim | Where it stands |
|---|---|---|
| **Logical** (J.L. Mackie) | The existence of evil is *logically incompatible* with an omnipotent, omniscient, wholly good God | Widely regarded as answered by Plantinga's Free Will Defense |
| **Evidential** | The *amount and severity* of evil make God's existence improbable | Remains live; the theodicies below are partial answers with stated limits |

### The logical problem

Mackie's argument, in the form the reference gives it:

1. If God is omnipotent, he can prevent all evil.
2. If God is wholly good, he wants to prevent all evil.
3. If God is omniscient, he knows about all evil.
4. Evil exists.
5. Therefore God does not exist, or lacks one of these attributes.

The claim is strong — not that God is unlikely but that the set of propositions is inconsistent.
That strength is also the weakness: a single coherent possibility breaks it.

### Plantinga's Free Will Defense

**It is possible** that God could not have created a world with significantly free creatures and no
evil, because significant freedom logically requires the possibility of choosing wrong. If God's goal
was a world with genuinely free creatures capable of moral good — greater than a world of programmed
robots — then the existence of evil is **logically compatible** with God's goodness. The defense is
widely acknowledged to have defeated the logical problem; even atheist philosophers, William Rowe
among them, conceded this.

> **WHAT THE CONCESSION ACTUALLY COVERS.** Read the claim precisely. It is a **defense**, not a
> theodicy — it claims a possibility, not an account of why any particular person suffered. And the
> concession is to the **logical** form only. Nobody conceded the evidential problem, which the
> reference says in the next breath remains. Quote this result as "the logical form has an answer",
> never as "the problem of evil has been solved". The second claim is false and the person you are
> talking to will know it.

Name the contested premise before an objector does: whether significant freedom *requires* the real
possibility of choosing wrong is exactly what critics of the defense deny. And a free-will account speaks most directly to moral evil — evil chosen by agents. It speaks
least directly to the natural evil the evidential problem leads with.

### The evidential problem remains

Even if evil is logically compatible with God, the sheer amount of suffering seems to make God's
existence improbable — **animal suffering before humans existed, natural disasters, diseases, and the
suffering of children**. None of those four is chosen by a free creature. That is why this is the
form that survives, and the form you will meet in real conversations.

| Response | The argument | The limitation the reference itself states |
|---|---|---|
| **Soul-making theodicy** (Irenaean, developed by John Hick) | God permits suffering because character development — courage, compassion, patience, moral growth — requires real challenges; the world is not a pleasure palace but a soul-making environment | Does not adequately address horrendous suffering that produces no growth, or suffering that **destroys rather than develops** the person |
| **Greater-good defense** | God permits particular evils because they are necessary for greater goods unattainable without them; we are not positioned to judge whether any instance is gratuitous, because we see only a small part of the picture | Can seem like a blank check for any evil, and is pastorally problematic applied to a specific tragedy |

Neither limitation is a concession you can skip. Soul-making meets a counterexample in any case where
suffering visibly unmade a person, and such cases are not rare. The greater-good defense buys its
protection by limiting what human moral judgement can see — and that limit does not stay put, since
the move that stops us calling an evil gratuitous also weakens moral inferences we rely on. Say the
limits out loud; an objector who finds them for you has learned you were selling, not reasoning.

> **THE PASTORAL DIMENSION.** The problem of evil is not only philosophical but existential. **People
> asking about suffering are often suffering.** A purely intellectual answer to a person in pain is
> pastorally inadequate. The Christian response includes the intellectual argument but also the
> personal one: God did not remain distant from suffering but entered it. The cross and the
> incarnation are God's answer to suffering — not an explanation but a presence. The book of Job does
> not answer the question of suffering; it presents a God who shows up.

Before answering at all, establish which conversation you are in — the argument or the grief. That is
the one-sentence check in §2 → `ministry-evangelism-persuasion-and-listening`, and it matters here more
than anywhere else in the reference.

## §10 Responding to common objections

Four objections, answered in turn. Three of the four are weaker than their reputation; the shape of
each answer is different, so learn the shape rather than a script.

| Objection | The move that answers it | The trap |
|---|---|---|
| The Bible has contradictions | Ask for the specific one; engage the passage | Claiming there are no difficulties |
| Religion is a crutch for weak people | Name the genetic fallacy | Arguing only about whether believers are weak |
| All religions lead to God | Pluralism is itself a claim needing defense | Treating a counter-claim as a refutation |
| Science has disproven religion | Scope — empirical method cannot settle a metaphysical question | Overclaiming what the historical examples show |

### "How can you believe the Bible when it has contradictions?"

**Ask what specific contradiction they have in mind.** Most people who raise this cannot name one —
they have heard the claim but not examined it. If they can, engage that passage. Most alleged
contradictions resolve by attention to context, genre, authorial intent, or harmonization of
complementary accounts; the field that addresses this systematically is textual criticism and
harmonization, and the scholarly literature is substantial.

**Do not claim there are no difficulties. Do claim that none are fatal** to the text's reliability or
to any Christian doctrine. That distinction is the whole answer, and it is an honest one only if you
hold both halves. The manuscript and dating evidence behind the second half is §8 →
`ministry-apologetics-resurrection-and-scripture`.

### "Religion is a crutch for weak people"

Even if it were, that would not make it false — **a crutch is the appropriate response to a broken
leg**. The questions are whether the leg is actually broken (the human condition includes genuine
brokenness) and whether the crutch actually works (Christianity has produced transformation across
cultures and centuries).

The deeper response is structural: this is a **genetic fallacy**. Explaining why someone holds a
belief does not address whether the belief is true. People may believe in God for psychological
reasons and God may still exist. Note that the fallacy cuts both ways and an honest use of it says
so — a psychological account of *un*belief is equally irrelevant to whether God exists, so do not
answer this objection by producing one.

### "All religions lead to God"

This claim — religious pluralism — **is itself a religious claim that needs defense**, not a
self-evident truth. The major religions make mutually exclusive claims about the nature of ultimate
reality (one personal God, many gods, no God, an impersonal absolute), the human condition (sin,
ignorance, desire, illusion), and the solution (grace, effort, enlightenment, liberation). They
cannot all be true in the same sense. The question is which, if any, is true — not whether they all
lead to the same place. Christ's own claims were explicitly exclusive: *"I am the way, the truth, and
the life. No one comes to the Father except through me"* (John 14:6).

Be honest about what this accomplishes. It shifts a burden; it does not close the question. The
developed pluralist position does not deny the surface contradictions — it argues the traditions are
culturally conditioned responses to one ultimate reality that none of them describes literally. The
reference does not engage that version, and an objector who holds it will not recognise their
position in the answer above.

### "Science has disproven religion"

Science addresses the natural world through empirical observation and testable hypotheses. **It
cannot address whether a transcendent God exists, because that is a metaphysical question outside
the scope of scientific method.** Science can settle specific claims — the age of the earth,
evolutionary mechanisms — but not whether there is a God who created the natural order that science
studies. Many of history's greatest scientists (Newton, Kepler, Mendel, Boyle, Faraday) were devout
Christians who saw science as the study of God's creation.

Two honest caveats. The boundary claim is itself philosophically contested — from one side by theists
who take fine-tuning (§6 → `ministry-apologetics-arguments-for-god`) to be empirical support for
design, from the other by naturalists who hold that any claim with empirical consequences is testable.
And the list of devout scientists establishes **compatibility**, historically demonstrated; it is a
biographical argument, and does not show the beliefs are true.

## §11 Worldview engagement, the three methods, and the most important skill

Effective apologetics engages not just individual arguments but **whole worldviews**. A worldview is
a comprehensive framework of beliefs about reality, knowledge, and ethics. The most effective move is
often not to argue within the other person's worldview but to show that their worldview **cannot
support the things they already believe** — that it is internally inconsistent, or cannot account for
what they take for granted: moral values, human dignity, rationality, meaning.

| Method | Where it starts | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Presuppositional** (Van Til, Bahnsen, Frame) | The claim that the Christian worldview alone provides a coherent foundation for knowledge, morality and meaning; all others borrow from Christian assumptions while denying the foundation | Exposes the foundational assumptions that underlie the disagreement | Can feel circular to outsiders; can seem to assume what it sets out to prove |
| **Classical / evidential** (Craig, Habermas, Geisler) | Neutral ground, building a case from evidence and argument — cosmological, fine-tuning, resurrection — to show Christianity more probable than its alternatives | Accessible to outsiders; uses publicly available evidence | Some people do not weigh evidence as rational agents; deeply held worldviews function more like identities than hypotheses |
| **Cumulative case** (Swinburne; the approach most apologists actually use) | No single argument proves God; the combined weight of cosmological, teleological, moral, experiential and historical arguments makes theism more probable than not | Mirrors how people actually reason about complex questions — probably the most effective in practice | Inherits every limitation of its components; a cumulative case built from weak arguments is not thereby strong |

Keep the hedge the reference keeps: cumulative case is **probably** the most effective in practice.
That is a judgement about persuasion, not a proof of superiority, and it is consistent with the
framing in §6 → `ministry-apologetics-arguments-for-god` that no single argument carries the weight
alone. The two live objections to the set are worth stating plainly — the presuppositional move looks
circular to precisely the person it is aimed at, and the classical move assumes an evidence-weighing
rational agent that the persuasion evidence in §1 → `ministry-evangelism-persuasion-and-listening`
says people often are not.

> **THE MOST IMPORTANT APOLOGETIC SKILL.** Listening. Find out what the person actually objects to
> before you start answering. Most people's objections to Christianity are not the ones their stated
> objection addresses. Someone who says "there's no evidence for God" may actually be reacting to a
> painful church experience. Someone who says "the Bible is full of contradictions" may actually be
> struggling with a moral command they don't want to follow. Someone who says "religion causes wars"
> may actually be asking why God allows suffering. **Ask "what's behind that?" and listen to the
> answer.** The real objection is usually deeper than the stated one.

This is why §9 and §10 are the *second* thing you do, not the first. A complete, accurate, well-cited
answer to an objection the person does not actually hold is a wasted conversation and often a costly
one — it tells them you were waiting to deploy rather than listening. The levels and techniques that
make the listening real are §2 → `ministry-evangelism-persuasion-and-listening`; the ethical line
that keeps the answering from becoming pressure is §5 → `ministry-evangelism-ethics`.

Apologetics removes obstacles and provides rational warrant; it does not produce faith, and it is not
a way of winning. Conversion is the work of the Holy Spirit, not the apologist — the framing §6 →
`ministry-apologetics-arguments-for-god` opens with, and the one to hold when an argument lands and
nothing changes, or when nothing lands and something changes anyway. The terms this part uses —
*Problem of Evil*, *Soul-Making Theodicy*, *Worldview* — are defined in §23 → `ministry-reference`,
and the books behind these arguments are in §24 → `ministry-reference`.
