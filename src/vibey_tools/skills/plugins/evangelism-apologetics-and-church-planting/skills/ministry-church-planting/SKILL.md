## The framing for Part V

Church planting is the establishing of a new Christian community in a specific context. The source
holds two descriptions together: a **theological act** — the church is God's instrument for his
mission — and a **community-building act**, because a church is a community of people who are
*maintained*, not merely founded. That second description sets the shape of this part. Almost all the
failure modes are in sustaining rather than founding: the founder bottleneck, volunteer burnout,
unhandled conflict, and the transition from a group where everyone knows each other to one where they
cannot. A plant that launches well and then fails did not fail at the thing it spent eighteen months
preparing for.

## §19 Church planting models

| Model | Description | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Apostolic/Pioneering | Planter starts from zero with no existing group; builds from new converts and transfers | Reaches the unchurched directly; high contextual relevance; entrepreneurial | Slow; high risk; requires exceptional leadership and resilience |
| Daughter/Satellite | An existing church sends a group and a planter to start a new congregation | Shared resources; existing support; proven model; lower risk | Can reproduce the parent church's limitations; less contextual innovation |
| Multi-site | One church expands to multiple locations with shared leadership and brand | Leverages strong teaching; reaches multiple areas; efficient use of gifted communicators | Campus pastors can be under-developed; brand-driven; centralization risk |
| House/Simple church | Small groups meet in homes; no dedicated building; lay-led or bi-vocational | Low cost; intimate community; reproducible; flexible | Limited reach without multiplication; quality control; doctrinal drift risk |
| Team-based | A team of 3–6 leaders plants together rather than a solo planter | Shared load; complementary gifts; built-in community; resilience | Requires team cohesion; slower initial decisions; potential for conflict |
| Bi-vocational | Planter works a secular job while planting; the church supports itself partially | Financially sustainable; engaged with the secular world; models workplace Christianity | Time constraints; burnout risk; slower growth |

The limitations column is not decoration: each is a real cost that surfaces later, usually in the
sustaining phase (§21), and choosing a model is choosing which of those costs you pay. The models are
not mutually exclusive either — team-based and bi-vocational describe how leadership is constituted
and funded, and either can combine with a pioneering or daughter start.

> **THE SOURCE DOES NOT RANK THESE, AND NEITHER SHOULD YOU**
>
> No survival or success figure is attached to any model here, and none should be supplied from
> elsewhere without saying where it came from — comparative outcome data is thin, self-reported and
> confounded by who chooses which model in which context. Match the model to the context, the sending
> relationship and the gifts actually present.

## §20 The church planting process, assessment to post-launch

The source lays the process out in five phases with month ranges. Treat the months as the **shape of
the sequence and the relative weight of each phase**, not as a schedule with authority — a planning
frame rather than a measured median, and a context with no sending church or no existing
relationships will run longer.

### Phase 1 — Assessment and calling (months 1–6)

Before anything else, assess: is the planter called, gifted and ready? Church planting assessment
centres evaluate **character, competency and chemistry**.

> **NOT EVERYONE WHO WANTS TO PLANT A CHURCH SHOULD**
>
> The source states this plainly. Assessment exists to prevent failures that are painful and
> expensive for everyone involved — the planter, the family, the core team, the sending church, and
> the people who joined something that then collapsed. A negative assessment is the process working.

The key questions:

- Is there a genuine calling — **not just frustration with a current church**?
- Is there apostolic gifting: the ability to start something from nothing?
- Is there a team?
- Is there a target context?
- Is there a sending church or network providing accountability and support?

### Phase 2 — Team building and vision casting (months 6–12)

Gather a core team, typically **10–30 people** who share the vision and commit to the plant. The team
should include complementary gifts: someone who teaches, someone who pastors, someone who
evangelizes, someone who organizes, someone who welcomes.

Start with a real purpose and real people, not a platform. The founding problem here is **critical
mass** — an empty room is unattractive, so early effort goes into a small core who show up reliably.
**Consistency beats scale**: a small group present every week is a church; a larger number each
attending occasionally is not yet one.

### Phase 3 — Pre-launch and community engagement (months 12–18)

Before launching public worship services, build relationships in the target community: serve it, meet
needs, develop a presence. The goal is not to fill a room with strangers on launch Sunday but to
build enough relational trust that the first service is full of people **who already know the
planters**.

This is where relational evangelism and service-based approaches bear fruit — trust mechanics in
§1–§2 → `ministry-evangelism-persuasion-and-listening`, the service, missional and relational
approaches compared in §3 → `ministry-evangelism-methods-and-presentations`. **Rhythm matters more
than content**: a predictable, sustainable cadence builds community faster than occasional excellent
events.

### Phase 4 — Launch (month 18)

The first public worship service. Market it appropriately — invitations, social media, word of mouth.
Make it **excellent but not overproduced**; the goal is authenticity, not performance. Preach the
gospel clearly (§12–§15 → `ministry-preaching`). Collect contact information. **Follow up with every
visitor within 48 hours.**

The first three months after launch are critical. The source's observation is that people who come in
the first month and are followed up well often stay, and people who are not followed up almost never
return — practitioner observation about the direction of the effect, not a measured retention rate,
and uncontrolled for the obvious confounder that the people who get followed up well are often the
ones who already knew somebody. Follow up regardless: the cost is low and the failure mode is
invisible.

### Phase 5 — Post-launch and sustainability (months 18–36+)

**This is where most plants fail.** The adrenaline of launch fades, the core team is tired, visitors
stop coming, and support from the sending church may be ending.

The most common failure mode named here is the **founder bottleneck**: a church where one person does
everything cannot grow, cannot survive that person's absence, and quietly discourages others from
taking initiative. The third clause is the one people miss — it is not only a capacity limit, it
teaches everyone else that initiative is unwanted.

Two rules follow, both counterintuitive enough to state flatly:

- **Delegating badly early beats delegating well later.** A task done imperfectly by someone learning
  it is worth more than the same task done perfectly by the one person who must not stop.
- **Succession should be planned before it is needed.** Planning it after it is needed is not
  succession, it is a crisis.

## §21 Building sustainable community: onboarding, norms, volunteers and conflict

### Onboarding is the growth constraint

Most churches are **unintentionally** hostile to newcomers — through in-jokes, unexplained context,
and nobody assigned to talk to the person standing alone. None of that is malice, which is exactly
why nobody inside the church notices it.

A **named greeter role** is a small intervention with a large effect: assigning the job to a specific
person converts something everyone vaguely assumes someone is doing into something someone is doing.
The source's claim that the first three minutes of a visitor's experience determine whether they
return is a practitioner heuristic carrying no measurement here — use it as an argument for taking
arrival seriously, not as a finding.

- **Train greeters.** The role is not a smile at the door; it is noticing the person standing alone.
- **Have a clear next step** — a class, a group, a meal with the pastor. One nameable thing.
- **Make it easy** to go from "I visited once" to "I belong here." Every undefined step in that path
  is a place where someone who wanted to belong quietly stops.

### Explicit norms and clear structure

**Norms are set early and by behaviour, not by statements.** What a church tolerates in its first
months becomes what it is — and the gap between the stated norm and the tolerated one is itself a
norm, teaching everyone that the stated ones are decorative.

Establish explicitly: what we believe; how we make decisions; what membership means; how we handle
conflict; what we expect of members. Deciding *how you will decide* before there is something
contentious to decide is the same move that keeps small groups functional (§16 →
`ministry-teaching-and-discipleship`).

> **THE TYRANNY OF STRUCTURELESSNESS APPLIES**
>
> A church without formal structure does not lack a power structure — it has an **invisible and
> unaccountable one**, which is worse, because there is no process by which anyone can contest it.
> Establish governance, membership and decision-making processes early, while the group is still
> small enough to have the conversation without it being about a particular person.

### Volunteers and burnout

> **BURNOUT IS A STRUCTURAL PROBLEM, NOT A PERSONAL WEAKNESS**
>
> In mission-driven and faith communities, burnout is elevated **precisely because the work feels
> non-negotiable** — you cannot decline work you believe God assigned. "Self-care" advice addressed
> to an overloaded person is usually a way of avoiding the structural fix. The structural fixes:
> term limits for volunteers, rotation of responsibilities, genuinely making it acceptable to stop,
> sabbaticals, and more than one person per critical function.

The **specific, bounded ask** is the whole technique. "Would you run the children's ministry?" is a
life sentence. "Would you teach the 3–5 year olds for the next three months?" gets a yes, and is
renewable. The bounded version is also the honest one — it discloses what is actually being asked,
the same test the ethics of influence applies to evangelistic asks (§5 →
`ministry-evangelism-ethics`). An unbounded ask made to someone who cannot say no to the church is
not a request.

**Why people volunteer:** purpose; relationships; **being asked** — the most underrated factor, since
most people who do not participate were never personally asked; using a skill; being needed.

**Why they stop:** the ask was unbounded; unclear expectations; feeling unnecessary; being taken for
granted; conflict that nobody addressed; life changed and there was no honorable exit.

The last is a design fault, not a character fault: if the only way out of a role is to let people
down, people stay until they break or leave the church entirely.

### Conflict in church communities

Conflict is not failure — it is **what happens when people who care about different things work
together**. A church with no visible conflict is usually suppressing it rather than lacking it.

The sources: substantive disagreement (theology, direction, priorities); process disagreement (how
decisions are made); unmet interests; misunderstanding; values; and personality. **Treating a
substantive conflict as a personality clash is a classic and damaging misdiagnosis** — it converts a
real disagreement into a fault in someone's character, failing to resolve the disagreement and
injuring the person.

- **Address early and directly.** Hoping it resolves is the standard mistake, and the cost of an
  awkward early conversation is always lower than the cost of the late one.
- **Handle privately where possible, publicly where the community needs to see it handled.**
- **Enforce consistently, proportionately, and documented**, with a path back where appropriate. The
  willingness to *actually remove someone* is what makes every other norm credible; a rule that has
  never been enforced is not a rule.

> **THE MISSING STAIR IS A LEADERSHIP FAILURE**
>
> A community that quietly works around a person who harms others is protecting that person and
> sacrificing everyone who encounters them unwarned. The source is direct: this is common in
> churches, it is a leadership failure, and it is **usually rationalised as keeping the peace or
> extending grace**. Naming the rationalisation is the point — the people doing it rarely believe
> they are choosing the harm.

> **SAFEGUARDING IS A LEGAL OBLIGATION, NOT A JUDGEMENT CALL**
>
> Safeguarding in any community involving children or vulnerable adults is a **formal obligation with
> legal requirements** — not a matter of judgement, discretion or community feeling. The duties,
> reporting thresholds and mandatory-reporter rules are set by the law where you are; find yours out
> from your jurisdiction, insurer or denomination *before* you need them. Nothing here substitutes
> for that.

## §22 Sustainability beyond the plant

**Financial sustainability.** Most plants require **3–5 years** to reach financial self-sufficiency.
Plan for **declining external support and growing internal giving** — the two curves have to cross,
and assuming they will is how plants run out of money in year three. Teach generosity early and
regularly; be transparent about finances. **The budget is a theological document**: it shows what the
church actually values, as distinct from what it says it values, and a stated commitment to mission,
mercy or multiplication that does not appear in it is a preference rather than a commitment.

**Leadership development.** The transition **from a founder-led church to a team-led church is the
critical sustainability threshold** — the founder bottleneck of §20 seen from the solution side.
Develop leaders from within the congregation, identify them early, and give them **real
responsibility with real support**: the fading-guidance pattern that runs through discipleship —
model it, do it together, then let them do it — is the same mechanism here (§17 →
`ministry-teaching-and-discipleship`).

The **ladder of engagement**: give people a first step that costs little, then a real one. The most
common failure is **having no rung between "attend" and "lead the thing"** — which looks from the
inside like a congregation that will not step up, and is in fact a missing rung.

**Multiplication.** A healthy church plant eventually plants other churches. Build it into the DNA
from the beginning — talk about it, budget for it, pray for it. A church that exists only for itself
has lost its missional identity.

> **COMMUNITIES ARE MAINTAINED, NOT FOUNDED**
>
> The energy and vision required to start a church are different from the faithfulness and endurance
> required to sustain one. **Both are needed.** The founder who can plant but cannot sustain has
> started something that will not last. The leader who can sustain but cannot plant has something
> that will not reproduce. The healthiest churches have both — or have the humility to partner with
> people whose gifts complement their own.
