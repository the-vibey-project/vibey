## The framing for Part I

Evangelism is **the communication of the gospel message with the intent to invite someone to follow
Jesus Christ**. It is a privilege and a responsibility — and it is also a communication task. That
last clause is what makes this part of the reference possible: because evangelism is communication,
the evidence on what persuades, what builds trust, and what constitutes ethical influence applies
to it directly.

That evidence points consistently at **substance over technique**: relevant content, genuine
engagement with the other person's actual position, and conversation rather than broadcast. The
source is candid that this is both reassuring and challenging — **reassuring because you do not need
a system, challenging because you need genuine relationship**, and relationship cannot be scheduled
into a campaign.

The ethics of that influence are not an appendix to this. They are §5 → `ministry-evangelism-ethics`,
and the disclosure test there governs everything in this skill: a technique that only works because
the other person cannot see it is not on the table, however effective it is.

## §1 Evangelism as a communication task, and what the persuasion evidence says

### What the evidence supports

> **WHAT THE PERSUASION EVIDENCE SAYS**
>
> Listening first — eliciting the other person's view before advancing yours — produces durable
> change. Conversation beats broadcast, substantially. Moral reframing — arguing from their values
> rather than yours — is difficult, effective, and rarely done, because it requires understanding
> values you may not share. Relevant facts and evidence are repeatedly the strongest identified
> lever. More forceful assertion persuades less than assumed. Facts alone against identity-linked
> beliefs persuade less than assumed. Attitude change and behaviour change are different things and
> may not correlate. **Winning the argument is not the same as changing what someone does.**

Two of those lines are easy to misread in opposite directions, and the source states both:

- **Evidence is the strongest identified lever** — so preparing an honest answer is worth the
  work. The apologetic material in §6 → `ministry-apologetics-arguments-for-god` exists for this
  reason.
- **Facts alone against identity-linked beliefs persuade less than assumed** — so the same evidence
  aimed at a belief someone's identity is built on will underperform your expectation. These are
  not in tension: evidence is the best lever available *and* the lever is weaker than people think
  when identity is load-bearing.

Read the list as a set of tendencies, not as measured effects. The source supplies no studies, no
sample sizes and no numbers, and this skill does not invent any. What the list is good for is
**ruling techniques in and out** — it is a reason to stop preparing speeches and start preparing
questions, not a formula with a known conversion rate.

### Relational evangelism

**Relational evangelism** is sharing the gospel through ongoing authentic relationships rather than
through strangers-at-a-distance approaches. Its grounding claim is an observation, not a statistic:
**most people who come to faith do so through a trusted relationship** — a friend, family member or
coworker who earned the right to be heard over time. The source gives no figure; do not supply one.

The approach has real costs, and the source names them where it tabulates the six methods in
§3–§4 → `ministry-evangelism-methods-and-presentations`: relational evangelism is **slow, requires
genuine investment, and cannot be scaled through programs**. It is the highest-trust method and the
one that resists being turned into a campaign.

### Trust decomposes into three things

The research on trust is directly relevant, because the relationship is the mechanism:

| Component | The question the other person is actually asking | Where it is won or lost |
|---|---|---|
| **Competence** | Can you do it? | Knowing what you are talking about; not bluffing an answer you do not have |
| **Reliability** | Do you do what you said? | Small kept commitments, repeated over time |
| **Care** | Are my interests in your calculation? | Whether you are a friend or a project manager with a conversion target |

> **MOST TRUST FAILURES ARE CARE, MISTAKEN FOR COMPETENCE**
>
> When someone stops trusting you, the diagnosis you reach for is usually competence — *I did not
> have a good enough answer*. The source says the third component is the usual culprit: the person
> concluded their interests were not in your calculation. Studying harder does not fix that, and it
> is the failure most likely to be invisible to the person who caused it.

Care is also where evangelism is most easily corrupted, because a relationship maintained *in order
to* produce a decision is a relationship with a hidden purpose. That is precisely what the
disclosure test in §5 → `ministry-evangelism-ethics` is built to catch.

## §2 Listening, earning the right to be heard, and spiritual conversations

### Listening is the highest-return skill, and the least taught

Listening is **the highest-return skill in relational evangelism, and the one least taught**. The
conversational approaches that durably change minds begin by eliciting and listening, not by
arguing — and the source is explicit that **this is not a softening tactic, it is the mechanism**.
Listening that is performed in order to get a turn to speak is the first level below, not the
fourth.

| Level | What you are doing | What it costs you |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Waiting to talk | Nothing — which is why it is the default |
| 2 | Hearing content | Attention to the words |
| 3 | Hearing content **and what it means to them** | Attention to the person behind the words |
| 4 | Hearing **what has not been said** | Enough time and enough silence for the unsaid to surface |

### The techniques, and what undermines them

| Technique | Why it works | The failure mode beside it |
|---|---|---|
| **Open questions** | Lets them set the terms rather than confirming yours | Questions that are assertions with a question mark |
| **Silence** — the most underused | People fill it with what they actually think | Filling it yourself, at the moment it was about to do its work |
| **Paraphrasing to check** | Tests whether you understood | Paraphrasing **to demonstrate** that you understood — the person can tell |
| **Asking about the specific** | Concrete answers; the general invites a position statement | Asking about the general and receiving a rehearsed one |

What actively undermines listening, from the source:

- **Formulaic reflecting that sounds like technique.** The moment it reads as a method, it stops
  being listening and becomes something being done to them.
- **Jumping to solutions.** Most people describing a spiritual struggle are not asking you to fix it.
- **Relating it to yourself.** The story returns to you and the door closes.
- **Finishing sentences.**
- **Mentally drafting your rebuttal while they are still speaking** — which is level 1 wearing the
  face of level 3.

> **ASK BEFORE ADVISING**
>
> "Do you want help thinking about it, or do you want me to listen?" resolves most mismatches in one
> sentence. Most evangelistic conversations fail not because the gospel was poorly presented but
> because **the evangelist was answering questions no one was asking**. Find out what the person
> actually struggles with before you start explaining.

That failure has an apologetic twin: the stated objection is usually not the real one, and the fix
is the same question asked differently — *what's behind that?* The worked treatment is
§9–§11 → `ministry-apologetics-evil-objections-and-worldview`.

### Earning the right to be heard

**Trust is built by small kept commitments over time and destroyed asymmetrically** — a single
defection undoes a great deal. The asymmetry is the whole reason the following list is unglamorous:
there is no single act that builds trust the way one act can destroy it.

What genuinely builds it:

- **Remembering things about people and following up.**
- **Introducing people to each other.**
- **Attributing credit publicly and in specifics.**
- **Being the same person in different rooms.**
- **Being useful before you need anything.**

In evangelism this means: **be a genuine friend first, not a project manager with a conversion
target. People can tell the difference, and the difference is the whole game.** This is the same
line the ethics section draws structurally — a friendship instrumentalised toward a decision fails
the disclosure test in §5 → `ministry-evangelism-ethics` — and the same discipline that makes a
church plant's pre-launch phase work rather than look like recruitment
(§19–§22 → `ministry-church-planting`).

### Spiritual conversations in natural context

**The goal is not to insert a gospel presentation into every conversation** but to develop the
sensitivity to recognise when spiritual questions naturally arise — and the courage to follow them.

The source says these moments are **more common than people think**, and names them:

| The moment | Why the question surfaces there |
|---|---|
| A crisis | The working account of how life holds together has just failed |
| A loss | Grief asks about meaning whether or not anyone invites it |
| A birth | The same question from the other end |
| A beauty | Something that seems to mean more than it should |
| A moral question | A judgement that feels like more than preference |
| An existential question about meaning or purpose | Asked directly, usually once, and usually briefly |

The skill at that moment is **asking a genuine follow-up question rather than launching into a
prepared speech**. The source's two examples:

- *"What do you think about that?"*
- *"Has that always been your view?"*

Both are open, both are specific to what was just said, and both leave the other person in
possession of the conversation. Having a clear presentation ready matters — that is
§3–§4 → `ministry-evangelism-methods-and-presentations` — but readiness is not a cue to deploy.

> **A NOTE ON WHAT LISTENING CANNOT DO**
>
> Nothing in §1–§2 makes conversion a communication outcome. The skills here earn a hearing, remove
> the obstacle of not being understood, and keep the relationship honest. The source's own framing
> of apologetics applies equally to evangelism (§6 → `ministry-apologetics-arguments-for-god`): the
> work is removing obstacles and giving reasons, and the outcome is not in the practitioner's hands. Judging your faithfulness by the decision rate
> is both bad theology and, by the ethics section's own test, the pressure that produces decisions
> that do not last.
