## The framing for Part III

Preaching is the public proclamation of God's Word — grounded in Scripture, applied to the lives of
the hearers, empowered by the Holy Spirit. The source holds two descriptions together without
letting either dissolve the other: it is **a theological act** (speaking God's truth) and **a
communication act** (speaking to human beings). The second is why four claims carried in from
§1 → `ministry-evangelism-persuasion-and-listening` bear on the sermon directly:

- structure aids comprehension and recall;
- specific evidence persuades more than assertion;
- **substance is the technique**;
- the audience cannot re-read what you said.

> **HOW FAR THAT EVIDENCE REACHES.** These are findings about communication and persuasion in
> general, not measurements taken on congregations hearing sermons. The source applies them by
> analogy — reasonably, since a sermon is spoken, one-directional and unrepeatable — but that is an
> application, not a result. Treat the rules below as well-motivated craft.

The last generates most of the rest: because the listener cannot go back, **signposting, repetition
and clear transitions that would feel excessive on the page are not optional**.

## §12 Sermon Structure and the Patterns That Work

### Structure is for the listener, not the speaker

A reader can re-read; a listener cannot. The listener needs signposting, repetition and transitions
at a density that looks heavy-handed in a manuscript and sounds right out loud.

**Three main points is a cliché because working memory is limited** — five is usually too many for a
listener to hold. The working-memory constraint is a robust finding (the same one §16 →
`ministry-teaching-and-discipleship` builds its teaching advice on); "three good, five too many" is
the source's heuristic drawn from it, not a measured threshold for sermons.

### The core sermon structure

| # | Movement | What it does | The discipline |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Introduction** | Open **in** something concrete — a specific scene, a question, a surprising fact, a story — rather than approaching it | Do not open with "turn in your Bibles to…" or throat-clearing about context. **Write it out verbatim and know it cold** — it carries you through the worst of the anxiety |
| 2 | **The text** | Read the passage clearly, with appropriate emphasis | Practise it aloud. **The public reading of Scripture is a pastoral act, not a perfunctory one** |
| 3 | **Exposition** | Explain the text in its context — what it meant to its original audience. This is where exegesis happens | **The text cannot mean what it never meant.** Find the original meaning before the contemporary application |
| 4 | **Application** | Move from what the text meant to what it means | Specific, not generic. "Trust God more" is not an application; "the next time you face a financial crisis, the text says to pray first rather than panic" is. **Aim at the will, not just the intellect** — what should the listener do, feel or believe differently? |
| 5 | **Conclusion** | Land the one sentence, then stop | A call to action if there is one; a return to the opening image if you have one. Do not end on the announcements or the prayer — **the last thing said should be yours** |

On movement 3: "the text cannot mean what it never meant" is a rule of grammatical-historical
interpretation, and it guards against reading a contemporary concern back into a passage. It is not
uncontested as a *total* account of meaning — traditions that read typologically or christologically
hold that a later canonical reading can carry more than the original audience heard, a debate the
source does not engage. What the rule settles is the *order of operations*: original meaning first,
application second.

### Four patterns that work

| Pattern | Shape | Best fit |
|---|---|---|
| **Problem–Solution** | The text identifies a human problem (sin, suffering, alienation, fear) and points to God's solution (grace, redemption, reconciliation, hope) | The most natural biblical pattern, because it mirrors the gospel arc |
| **Claim–Evidence–Implication** | State the main truth of the text, support it with the biblical evidence, draw out the implications for the hearers | Didactic passages — epistles, Proverbs, the Sermon on the Mount |
| **Situation–Complication–Resolution** | Set the historical or narrative scene, introduce the tension within the narrative, show how the text resolves it | Narrative passages — Old Testament history, the Gospels, Acts |
| **Monroe's Motivated Sequence** | Attention, need, satisfaction, visualization, action | Evangelistic or topical sermons where the goal is not just information but **decision** |

> **A SERMON AIMED AT DECISION IS STILL UNDER THE DISCLOSURE TEST.** Monroe's Motivated Sequence is
> a persuasive structure, and the moment a sermon is built to produce a decision rather than to
> explain a text, the ethics of influence in §5 → `ministry-evangelism-ethics` apply to it in full —
> including the hard cases about altar calls and public invitations. Persuasion that engages the
> hearer's reasoning survives disclosure; engineered urgency does not become acceptable because it
> happens from a pulpit.

## §13 Expository Preaching, and the Curse of Knowledge

### What expository preaching is

Expository preaching takes **a specific text of Scripture as its starting point** and expounds its
meaning, with **the main point of the sermon derived from the main point of the text**.

| Method | Starts with | Risk the source names |
|---|---|---|
| **Expository** | A specific text; the sermon's main point comes from the text's main point | — |
| **Topical** | A topic, then finds supporting texts | The agenda is the preacher's, not Scripture's |
| **Textual** | A text as a launching point | May not develop the text's content systematically |

### Why it matters

- It lets **Scripture set the agenda** rather than the preacher.
- It **teaches the congregation how to read the Bible** by modelling it.
- It **prevents hobby horses**.
- It **covers the whole counsel of God over time**, not just the preacher's favourite themes.
- It **grounds authority in the text** rather than in the preacher's personality.

These are arguments about *tendency*, not guarantees: sequential exposition makes hobby horses
harder, not impossible, and none of it claims a topical sermon cannot be faithful.

### The expository process

1. **Select the text** — preferably preaching through a book sequentially.
2. **Read it multiple times in multiple translations.**
3. **Determine the historical and literary context.**
4. **Identify the main point of the text** — what was the author communicating to the original audience?
5. **Develop the sermon structure from the text's structure** — find the natural divisions and movements in the text and let them shape the outline.
6. **Apply the text's truth** to the contemporary congregation.

Step 5 is what distinguishes the method in practice. An outline imposed on a text produces a topical
sermon wearing expository clothes.

> **THE CURSE OF KNOWLEDGE IN PREACHING**
>
> The single most common failure in preaching: you cannot un-know what you know, so you
> systematically overestimate what is obvious to your congregation. The fix is **testing on a real
> non-expert, not introspection**. Biblical scholarship that took you years to acquire is not obvious
> to someone hearing it for the first time. Explain terms. Define concepts. Don't assume your
> congregation knows what "justification" or "covenant" or "eschatology" means just because you've
> used those words for twenty years.

The curse of knowledge is a widely reported effect in cognitive and behavioural research; the source
gives no citation and no effect size for it, and neither does this pack. The part that matters here
is *why introspection fails as a fix*: the bias sits in the estimate itself, so checking your own
sense of what is obvious re-runs the same faulty estimate. That is the whole case for testing on a
real non-expert. Of the three terms the callout names, *covenant* is defined at §23 →
`ministry-reference`; *justification* and *eschatology* are the callout's examples and the reference
does not define them — which is itself the point the callout is making.

## §14 Delivery and Speech Anxiety

**Substance matters more than delivery** — that is the persuasion evidence in §1 →
`ministry-evangelism-persuasion-and-listening` — **but delivery still matters, and poor delivery can
obscure good substance.** The goal is delivery that serves the message rather than drawing attention
to itself.

| Element | What to do | The failure it prevents |
|---|---|---|
| **Voice** | Vary pitch, pace and volume. Slow down for important points. **Pause after significant statements** — silence is the most underused tool in preaching, and it gives the hearer time to process | **Monotone is the most common delivery failure** |
| **Pace** | Aim for **130–150 words per minute**. Slowing down feels excruciatingly slow to you but sounds normal to the listener | Most preachers speak too fast, especially when nervous |
| **Movement** | Purposeful movement, not pacing. **Stand still for important points.** Move during transitions | Repetitive gestures — the congregation starts counting them instead of listening |
| **Eye contact** | Look at people, not at notes. Sustained contact with different sections of the room | Notes as script. Notes should be a reference — ideally you know the sermon well enough that they carry structure, not content |

The 130–150 wpm figure is a practitioner's working range, not a measured optimum for comprehension.
Its value is as a calibration check: time a rehearsal, divide, and see whether you are inside it.

### Speech anxiety

The source lists the interventions that **genuinely help**, and is specific about the mechanism in
each case — which is what makes the list usable rather than reassuring.

| Intervention | Mechanism the source names |
|---|---|
| **Exposure** — repeated preaching | **Habituation, not tuition.** You do not think your way out of it; the response attenuates with repetition |
| **Preparation and rehearsal** | Reduces *genuine* uncertainty — this is the part of the fear that is well-founded |
| **Reappraisal** | Interpreting arousal as readiness rather than fear; **the physiology is nearly identical** |
| **Slow exhalation** | Steadies the breath |
| **Attention shift** | From self-monitoring to the audience and the message. Much of the distress is self-focus, and **having a task outside yourself displaces it** |

**The audience cannot see most of what you feel.** The internal experience of anxiety is far louder
to the speaker than its outward signs are to anyone watching.

These come from the public-speaking-anxiety and emotion-regulation literature. The source presents
them as the interventions that genuinely help and names a mechanism for each, but supplies no
citation and no effect size for any of them, and neither does this pack — read the short list as the
source's selection, not as a graded evidence review. This is craft guidance, not clinical guidance:
anxiety that is disabling rather than uncomfortable is a matter for a clinician, and the source
offers nothing on it.

## §15 Sermon Preparation: the Weekly Rhythm, and the One-Sentence Test

A six-day cycle. The value is not the calendar — it is that exegesis finishes before construction
starts, and that the sermon is spoken aloud before it is preached.

| Day | Stage | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **Monday** | Text selection and first reading | Read the text multiple times in multiple translations. Read the surrounding context — the whole chapter, the whole book. Pray through the text. Write down initial observations and questions |
| **Tuesday–Wednesday** | Exegesis | Study the text in depth. Use the original languages if you have them. Consult commentaries — **at least two, preferably one technical and one pastoral**. Study the historical and cultural context. Identify the main point. Determine the structure and natural divisions |
| **Thursday** | Sermon construction | Develop the outline **from the text's structure**. Write the introduction and conclusion. Identify applications. Decide what to cut — **nearly every draft sermon is too long, and the fix is removing a whole point rather than speaking faster** |
| **Friday** | Manuscript or outline | Write it out — full manuscript or detailed outline. **Writing forces clarity.** If you manuscript, practise delivering it so it does not sound read. If you outline, make sure the transitions are clear |
| **Saturday** | Rehearsal | Preach it **aloud, standing, at full volume**. Time it. Cut if necessary. Practise the introduction and conclusion until you know them cold. Preaching it aloud reveals what silent reading does not — awkward transitions, unclear logic, sentences too long to deliver in one breath |
| **Sunday** | Preaching | Trust your preparation. Be present with the congregation. **Preach to the faces in front of you, not to an idealized audience.** If you lose your place, pause, find it, and continue — the congregation cares about the message, not your perfection |

Two steps are load-bearing and most often skipped. **Thursday's cut rule** is the answer to
over-length — speaking faster to fit damages both pace (§14) and comprehension. **Saturday's
rehearsal** is the only step that surfaces delivery problems before the congregation does, and it is
also the exposure §14 says works by habituation.

> **THE ONE-SENTENCE TEST**
>
> If you cannot state the point of the sermon in one sentence, the sermon does not have a point yet.
> Every sermon should have one governing idea — one thing the listener should take away. If the
> listener remembers nothing else, what is the one thing? That one sentence should be **stated in the
> introduction, developed in the body, and landed in the conclusion**.

The test is diagnostic in both directions. Applied before Thursday it tells you whether the exegesis
has finished; applied after Saturday, whether the sermon you built is the one you meant to build —
and a sentence you can only produce by reading your own outline back is one the congregation will
not leave with.
