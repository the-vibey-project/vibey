## §6 What apologetics is, and the arguments for God's existence

### §6.1 The task, and its limits

Apologetics is the discipline of **giving reasons for Christian faith and responding to objections
against it**. The term comes from the Greek *apologia* — a defense, as in a legal defense. The source
grounds it in 1 Peter 3:15: "always being prepared to make a defense to anyone who asks you for a
reason for the hope that is in you."

> **APOLOGETICS IS NOT ABOUT WINNING ARGUMENTS**
> It is about **removing obstacles** and **providing rational warrant for faith**, while
> recognising two things the source states plainly: faith is more than intellectual assent, and
> **conversion is ultimately the work of the Holy Spirit, not the apologist**. An apologist who
> forgets the second turns a defense into a contest, and a won contest is not a conversion.

Two consequences follow, and both are load-bearing:

- **The goal is obstacle removal, not proof.** If the stated obstacle is dissolved and the person
  still does not believe, the argument did its job and something else is in the way — finding it is
  the listening skill of §11 → `ministry-apologetics-evil-objections-and-worldview`.
- **The reasons are given to a person, not to a position.** The ethics governing evangelistic
  influence govern apologetic influence too — engage reasoning, do not manufacture pressure (§5 →
  `ministry-evangelism-ethics`; the listening that precedes it is §1–§2 →
  `ministry-evangelism-persuasion-and-listening`).

### §6.2 Cumulative, not demonstrative

The source is explicit that **the classical arguments do not compel belief — if they did, every
philosopher would be a theist**. What they do instead is narrower and worth stating precisely: they
demonstrate that **theistic belief is rationally warranted**, that **theism has explanatory power**,
and that **the burden of proof is shared** — the last of which is itself a contested framing, denied
by anyone who defines atheism as an absence of belief rather than a positive claim. The source
asserts it rather than arguing for it, and an informed interlocutor will say so.

> **CUMULATIVE, NOT DEMONSTRATIVE**
> The most effective apologetic approach is typically **cumulative** — multiple arguments that
> together make theism **more probable than not** — rather than a single argument that claims to
> prove God conclusively. Presenting any one of these as a knock-down proof claims more than the
> source does. The source makes the same move for the historical case (§7 →
> `ministry-apologetics-resurrection-and-scripture`), and cumulative-case apologetics as a named
> method is §11 → `ministry-apologetics-evil-objections-and-worldview`.

This framing is also the honest one: each argument below is live philosophy with competent people on
both sides. A cumulative case survives a conceded premise in one strand; a demonstrative one does not.

### §6.3 The cosmological argument

Two formulations, both reproduced as the source gives them.

**The Kalam Cosmological Argument** (attributed by the source to William Lane Craig):

1. Whatever begins to exist has a cause.
2. The universe began to exist.
3. Therefore, the universe has a cause.

The cause must be **spaceless, timeless, immaterial, uncaused, and enormously powerful** — properties
that fit a theistic God. The source's support for the key premises: the universe is **not eternal**
(Big Bang cosmology and the **Borde-Guth-Vilenkin theorem**), and whatever begins to exist has a
cause — **an intuitive metaphysical principle, though contested by some**, which is the source's own
concession.

**The Leibnizian Contingency Argument:**

1. Everything that exists has an explanation, either in its own nature (a necessary being) or in an
   external cause (a contingent being).
2. If the universe has an explanation, that explanation is God — a necessary, non-contingent being.
3. The universe exists.
4. Therefore, the universe has an explanation, and that explanation is God.

**Strength** (the source's): it addresses why there is something rather than nothing. **Limitation**
(the source's): the premise that the universe is contingent rather than necessary **is disputed**.
For the Kalam the source gives grounding in contemporary cosmology as the strength, and as the
limitation that it **establishes a first cause but not all of God's attributes**.

**What these arguments actually face.** The Kalam's causal principle is drawn from things beginning
*within* time out of pre-existing material; whether it transfers to the beginning of time and matter
themselves is exactly what critics deny, and the source already grants the principle is contested.
The Borde-Guth-Vilenkin result is a theorem about past-incompleteness of expanding classical
spacetimes under a specific condition — reading it as an absolute beginning of all physical reality
is a contested interpretation rather than a settled deliverable of cosmology, and models built to
evade its condition exist. The step from "a cause" to a *personal* cause who chose to act is a
further argument the Kalam does not supply — the source's own limitation restated. Against the
contingency argument, the standard replies are to deny the explanatory principle in its strong form,
or to hold that the universe itself is the necessary being.

### §6.4 The teleological argument (fine-tuning)

The physical constants of the universe — **the strength of gravity, the cosmological constant, the
mass of fundamental particles, the electromagnetic force, and dozens more** — are fine-tuned to a
degree that permits life, and the probability of those values falling in the life-permitting range by
chance is, on the source's account, **vanishingly small**. The argument then runs as a three-way
elimination:

1. The fine-tuning is due to **physical necessity**, **chance**, or **design**.
2. Not physical necessity — the constants are not determined by the laws of physics; they could have
   been different.
3. Not chance — the probabilities are too small.
4. Therefore, design.

**Strength** (the source's): grounded in contemporary physics, and **the fine-tuning data is widely
accepted by physicists of all worldviews**. **Limitation** (the source's): design of the universe's
physical structure **does not directly establish a personal God**.

**The multiverse objection, and the source's reply.** The objection is that there are many universes
and we happen to be in a life-permitting one. The source answers that this **does not eliminate
design — it pushes the question back to what produced the multiverse generator, which itself requires
fine-tuning**.

> **KEEP THIS EXCHANGE HONEST**
> That reply is a real move and a live one, but it is not a closure: multiverse proponents answer
> that a generating mechanism need not be fine-tuned as the constants are, and that some multiverse
> proposals fall out of independently motivated physics rather than being posited to dodge the design
> inference. Two further objections target step 3 rather than the data in step 1. First, the
> probability claim needs a defined measure over the space of possible constant values, and whether
> such a measure normalises at all is disputed — "vanishingly small" presupposes what the objection
> denies. Second, observer selection — no observer could find themselves in a non-life-permitting
> universe, so observing a life-permitting one is not by itself surprising. Present this as the
> strongest empirical strand of a cumulative case, not as a calculation that settles it.

### §6.5 The moral argument

1. If God does not exist, objective moral values and duties do not exist.
2. Objective moral values and duties do exist — some things are **really** right or wrong, not just
   culturally preferred.
3. Therefore, God exists.

The source's key claim is that **without a transcendent moral lawgiver, morality is reduced to
cultural convention or evolutionary adaptation — neither of which produces genuine moral
obligation**. Its illustration is that the intuition that **torturing children for fun is objectively
wrong, not just culturally unpopular, is nearly universal and is difficult to ground in atheistic
naturalism**. **Strength:** resonates with universal moral intuition. **Limitation** (the source's):
**the existence of objective moral values is contested by moral relativists and nihilists**.

**What it actually faces.** The source's stated limitation understates the opposition, and that is
worth correcting rather than repeating. Premise 2 is the one most theists and most secular
philosophers *agree* on; the contested premise is 1. Many non-theist philosophers are moral realists
— objective moral facts exist, grounded in something other than a divine lawgiver — so the objection
is not confined to relativists and nihilists. The other standard objection is the dilemma
named after Plato's *Euthyphro*: is an act good because God commands it, in which case goodness looks
arbitrary, or does God command it because it is good, in which case the standard is independent of
God? The usual reply grounds goodness in God's nature rather than in bare command; critics reply that
this relocates the dilemma rather than dissolving it. The exchange is unresolved; say so.

### §6.6 The ontological argument

Various formulations — the source names **Anselm, Plantinga, and Craig's modal version**:

1. If a maximally great being is possible, then a maximally great being exists in some possible
   world.
2. A maximally great being exists in every possible world, because maximal greatness includes
   necessary existence.
3. Therefore, a maximally great being exists in the actual world.

**Strength:** a priori, independent of empirical evidence. **Limitation** (the source's): **the key
premise — that a maximally great being is possible — is precisely what the atheist denies**, and
**most philosophers consider the ontological argument inconclusive but intellectually interesting**.

**What it actually faces.** The symmetry objection is the one to know: run the same modal machinery
from the opposite premise — that it is possible no maximally great being exists — and it delivers the
conclusion that no such being exists in any world. The argument's whole weight therefore rests on
which possibility claim you were already inclined to grant, which is the source's limitation in
sharper form. Two older objections attach to the Anselmian version: parody arguments running the same
form for a greatest conceivable island, and the objection that existence is not a property that makes
a thing greater. The source's verdict — inconclusive — is the honest one, and this is the weakest
member of a cumulative case.

### §6.7 The four side by side

Derived from §6 above. Every "source's strength" and "source's limitation" cell traces to a sentence
in this section; the objection column is what the argument faces in the wider literature and is
flagged as such.

| Argument | Form | Source's stated strength | Source's stated limitation | The objection it actually faces |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Cosmological (Kalam)** | A posteriori, from a beginning | Grounded in contemporary cosmology | A first cause, not all of God's attributes | The causal principle may not transfer to the beginning of time itself; reading Borde-Guth-Vilenkin as an absolute beginning is contested |
| **Cosmological (Leibnizian)** | A posteriori, from contingency | Addresses why there is something rather than nothing | The universe's contingency is disputed | Deny the explanatory principle, or make the universe itself the necessary being |
| **Teleological (fine-tuning)** | A posteriori, from the constants | Fine-tuning data widely accepted across worldviews | Does not directly establish a personal God | The probability claim needs a measure that may not normalise; observer selection; the multiverse exchange is unsettled |
| **Moral** | From the existence of moral obligation | Resonates with universal moral intuition | Objective values contested by relativists and nihilists | Secular moral realism denies premise 1; the Euthyphro dilemma |
| **Ontological** | A priori, modal | Independent of empirical evidence | The possibility premise is what the atheist denies | The symmetry objection reverses it; parody cases; existence as a perfection |

### §6.8 Using this in an actual conversation

- **Lead with the person, not the argument.** The stated objection is usually not the real one, and
  finding the real one is a listening problem before it is a philosophy problem — §1–§2 →
  `ministry-evangelism-persuasion-and-listening`, then §11 →
  `ministry-apologetics-evil-objections-and-worldview`.
- **Claim what the source claims and no more.** "This makes theism rationally warranted and shifts
  the burden" is defensible; "this proves God" is not, and an interlocutor who knows the literature
  will spend the rest of the conversation on the overclaim rather than on the case.
- **Concede the limitation out loud.** Each argument establishes less than the God of Christian
  confession — which is why the case is cumulative, and why the historical argument carries its own
  distinct weight (§7 → `ministry-apologetics-resurrection-and-scripture`).
- **Expect the problem of evil.** On the source's own assessment it is the most powerful objection
  to theistic belief, and it is handled in §9 → `ministry-apologetics-evil-objections-and-worldview`.
- **Removing an obstacle is not presenting the gospel.** The presentation itself — God, sin, Christ,
  response — is §4 → `ministry-evangelism-methods-and-presentations`.
