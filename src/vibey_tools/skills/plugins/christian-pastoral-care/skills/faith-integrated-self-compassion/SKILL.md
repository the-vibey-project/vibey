# Faith-Integrated Self-Compassion: Grace, Shame and Recovery

> **Part 1 of 1** of the *Christian Pastoral Care* reference (plugin `christian-pastoral-care`).
>
> **Currency:** As of October 2026. The theology is stable — the confessional text cited dates
> from 2000 and the authors wrote between the 1970s and the 2020s. The clinical evidence in §7 is
> young and moving quickly (self-compassion research, religiously adapted therapy, and adult
> autistic-burnout research especially); recheck its figures before relying on them.

> **⚠️ Scope — read this first.** This is **pastoral and educational material, not therapy, not
> medical advice, and not a treatment plan.** It is written for any Christian working through
> burnout, shame or trauma, and for the pastors, elders, friends and Christian counsellors who walk
> with them.
>
> - **If there is any risk of suicide or self-harm, act now:** contact local emergency services or a
>   crisis line (in the United States, call or text **988**). Pastoral care does not wait on that and
>   does not replace it.
> - **Trauma symptoms, suicidal thinking, severe or prolonged burnout, depression, or any loss of
>   ability to function need a licensed clinician** — a psychologist, licensed counsellor or
>   therapist, or physician. Evidence-based trauma-focused therapies exist and work (§9).
> - **Abuse that is ongoing, or involves a child or vulnerable adult, is a safeguarding matter** with
>   legal duties that depend on where you are.
>
> Used well, everything below sits **alongside** professional care, not in place of it. §9 says how.

## 0. What this is and how to read it

**This is a practitioner reference written from inside the evangelical Protestant tradition**, and
it says so rather than pretending to neutrality. That has a consequence for every page:

| Kind of claim | How it is marked | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Theological** | *the tradition teaches*, *on this view* | A claim held within evangelical Christianity, grounded in Scripture as that tradition reads it. **Not an empirical finding**, and not tested by the studies in §7 |
| **Empirical** | a **Standing** label: well-supported, moderate, preliminary, gap | A claim about what research shows, with the strength of the evidence attached |
| **Practitioner** | *practitioner guidance* | Pastoral or clinical wisdom that is widely used and has not been tested as such |

Two boundaries before any of it is used. First, **other Christian traditions** — Catholic, Orthodox,
mainline Protestant, and different evangelical streams — frame grace, justification, assurance and
self-regard differently; this reference states the evangelical framing and does not adjudicate
between traditions. Second, **the clinical studies measure practices and attitudes, not doctrines.**
A trial can show that a Christian-framed compassion practice reduces shame; it cannot show that the
doctrine behind it is true, and it does not need to.

The central claim the whole reference works out:

> **On the evangelical view, extending God's grace to yourself is not self-indulgence — it is
> faith.** Receiving a verdict God has already given is trust; insisting on a harsher verdict of
> one's own is, on this view, a quiet form of unbelief. The practical problem is moving that from
> agreement in the head to something lived in the body, the nervous system and the community.

## 1. The theology of grace — the tradition's case

**Theological claims throughout this section.**

### 1.1 The confessional anchor
The **Baptist Faith and Message 2000** (the Southern Baptist confession, widely representative of
evangelical teaching on these points) frames the matter in three articles:

- **Article III (Man)** grounds every person's dignity in creation in God's image and in Christ's
  death: worth is conferred, not earned.
- **Article IV (Salvation)** describes justification as God's gracious and **"full acquittal"** of all
  who repent and believe, bringing them into a relationship of peace and favour with God — **a
  settled verdict, not a probationary status**.
- **Article V (God's Purpose of Grace)** affirms that those God has accepted in Christ will not fall
  away from grace — so standing does not rise and fall with daily performance.

⚠️ Article V states a **Baptist and Reformed-leaning** position on perseverance. Wesleyan and
Arminian evangelicals hold assurance differently; the pastoral point below — that a believer's
standing is not re-earned each day — is shared more widely than the doctrine of perseverance itself.

### 1.2 The theologians the argument leans on
| Writer | The claim, paraphrased |
|---|---|
| **Tim Keller** | **The verdict comes before the performance**, not after it: acceptance is the cause of obedience, not its reward. We are more sinful than we dared believe and more loved than we dared hope — both at once, so neither despair nor complacency follows. Gospel humility is not thinking more or less of oneself but **thinking of oneself less**: received grace produces self-forgetfulness, not self-preoccupation |
| **Jerry Bridges** | No day is so bad that it is beyond God's grace, and no day so good that it is beyond the need of it. Basing one's daily standing on performance is the same error on good days (self-righteousness) and bad days (despair) — both live by works. His remedy, a phrase he credited to Jack Miller: **"preach the gospel to yourself"** every day |
| **John Piper** | Identity comes first not from what one does but from **being acted upon** — from having received mercy |
| **Paul David Tripp** | Performance-based Christianity is a game no one can win, scored by a perfect judge; *New Morning Mercies* builds daily devotion around receiving fresh grace, drawing heavily on Romans 8 |
| **Wayne Grudem** | Justification is an instantaneous legal act of God that does not merely cancel guilt but **credits the believer with Christ's righteousness** (*Systematic Theology*) |
| **Ed Welch** (CCEF) | The cross is God's fullest word to people who feel unworthy |
| **Scotty Smith** | *Everyday Prayers* models trusting Christ's righteousness and love as the way shame's grip loosens |

The Scripture the argument rests on: **Romans 8:1, 33–34** (no condemnation; God justifies), **Galatians
4:4–7** and **1 John 3:1** (adoption), **2 Corinthians 5:17** (new creation).

### 1.3 The inference, and its pastoral edge
If God has justified, adopted and made new, then — the tradition argues — refusing to receive those
realities is not humility but **disagreeing with God's own verdict**, and on Bridges' account wallowing
in guilt on bad days is the same rejection of grace as self-righteousness on good ones.

> **⚠️ Handle this inference with care.** It is the theological engine of the whole reference, and
> said carelessly to someone already drowning in shame it becomes **one more accusation**: now they
> are failing at receiving grace too. Offer it as an invitation and a relief, never as a diagnosis of
> unbelief. §10 lists this as the first failure mode.

### 1.4 Where evangelicals disagree
Some writers in the **biblical-counselling** stream are wary of the language of self-esteem and
self-compassion altogether, preferring to speak of **self-forgetfulness** and of God's compassion
rather than one's own. The disagreement is mostly about **warrant and vocabulary**, not about whether
believers should stop condemning themselves. This reference uses "self-grace" for receiving God's
grace toward oneself, and keeps "self-compassion" for the research construct in §7.

## 2. Gospel self-grace and secular self-compassion

The source tradition draws a sharp line between the two. Psychologist **Joshua Knabb** names it in
directional terms: secular self-compassion is **inside-out** (kindness generated from awareness of
one's own suffering); Christian self-compassion is **outside-in** (drawn from God's active, loving
presence).

| | Gospel self-grace (the tradition's account) | Secular self-compassion (the research construct) |
|---|---|---|
| **Source** | Received from outside — God's declaration | Generated within — kindness toward oneself |
| **Ground** | Christ's finished work, held as objectively true | Shared humanity: everyone fails and suffers |
| **Sin and failure** | Taken with full seriousness — it required Christ's death — and fully forgiven | Acknowledged without harsh self-judgement |
| **Points to** | Christ | One's own kindness and shared humanity |

*(The secular column follows the standard research model — self-kindness, common humanity and
mindfulness, after Kristin Neff — which is wider literature, not the source. The common caricature of
secular self-compassion as ignoring wrongdoing is unfair: the construct includes facing failure.)*

**What follows practically.** The **behaviours** overlap heavily — noticing suffering, speaking to
oneself without contempt, refusing to make one failure a verdict on the whole self. **The difference
is the warrant.** For a believer, the outside-in warrant is often what makes the behaviour possible at
all: kindness toward oneself can feel like self-indulgence until it is understood as agreement with
God. That is exactly the gap Knabb's adapted interventions (§7) target.

## 3. Shame, guilt, conviction and condemnation

Four words that are routinely blurred, and the blur does damage.

| Term | Kind of claim | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Guilt** | Empirical | A painful focus on a **specific act** — "I did a bad thing." Research associates it with repair and apology |
| **Shame** | Empirical | A painful focus on the **whole self** — "I am bad." Research associates it with hiding, withdrawal, and higher rates of depression and other difficulties |
| **Conviction** | Theological | The Spirit's specific, hopeful prompting toward repentance and restoration |
| **Condemnation** | Theological | A global verdict of rejection — which, on Romans 8:1, does not stand against those in Christ |

*(The guilt/shame distinction is wider literature — notably June Tangney's research programme —
**Standing: well-supported** as a distinction, with the usual cautions about self-report measures.)*

**The pastoral use.** Conviction looks like guilt: specific, actionable, ending in relief. Shame and
condemnation look alike: global, paralysing, ending nowhere. A believer stuck in global
self-condemnation is, on this framework, **not** experiencing more holiness — and much of what they
carry may not be sin at all.

**Ed Welch adds two corrections that matter for trauma.** First, shame can be **opposed** rather than
promised away; promising that it will vanish turns healing into another performance target. Second,
**shame from being victimised is real** — the person has in fact been shamed, and the feeling is an
honest response to genuine harm, **not a failure of faith** and not something to think one's way out
of alone.

Three different things often arrive tangled together and need different responses: **guilt for
wrongs actually done** (confession and repair), **shame from harm done to the person** (lament,
justice, safety, often professional help), and **reactions that are neurological or sensory** (an
overloaded nervous system, executive-function failure) that are **not moral events at all**.

## 4. Identity in Christ as a counter-narrative to shame

**The tradition's claim** is that identity in Christ works through the same psychological channels as
cognitive restructuring — replacing a false belief with a true one — **but with an ontological claim
attached**: the new identity is declared true because of what God has done, not adopted because it
is useful.

### 4.1 McGee's four false beliefs
**Robert McGee**, *The Search for Significance*, is the most explicitly CBT-shaped evangelical
framework:

| False belief | Its core message | Theological remedy |
|---|---|---|
| **The Performance Trap** | I must meet certain standards to feel good about myself | **Justification** |
| **Approval Addiction** | I must be approved by certain others | **Reconciliation** |
| **The Blame Game** | Those who fail, including me, deserve punishment | **Propitiation** |
| **Shame** | I am what I am; I cannot change; I am hopeless | **Regeneration** |

### 4.2 Other frameworks
- **Neil Anderson**, *Victory Over the Darkness*, groups scriptural identity statements under
  **Accepted, Secure and Significant**. ⚠️ His wider *Steps to Freedom in Christ* process carries a
  spiritual-warfare framing that is **disputed among evangelicals**; the identity lists are the widely
  used, uncontroversial part.
- **Curt Thompson**, *The Soul of Shame* and *Anatomy of the Soul*, argues that shame heals through
  **being known in community** — in his phrase there is no "Jesus and me", only "Jesus and us".
  ⚠️ **Standing of his neuroscience language:** claims that shame physically damages neural pathways
  or that practices "rewire" the brain are **popular interpersonal-neurobiology framing, not
  established neuroscience**. The relational claim beneath them — that being met with acceptance by
  another person helps in a way self-talk alone does not — is consistent with the broad research on
  social support, and stands on that.
- **"New name" texts** — Revelation 2:17 and Isaiah 62:2–4 (no longer "Deserted" but "My Delight") —
  are used as God's re-naming over an old shame identity.

### 4.3 Rejection sensitivity
For believers with ADHD, Christian coaches such as **Dawn Swayne** describe identity truths as an
**anchor** when a perceived rejection sets off an intense emotional spiral: other people's verdicts
become subordinate to God's, without denying that the pain is real.

> **⚠️ Two claims to hold loosely.** "Rejection sensitive dysphoria" is a **clinical and popular
> term, not a validated diagnosis**; emotional dysregulation in ADHD, by contrast, is well documented.
> And a figure circulating in this literature — that children with ADHD receive some **20,000 more
> negative messages by age 12** — **has no traceable study behind it**. Do not repeat it as fact. The
> pastoral point survives without it: many neurodivergent believers carry a great deal of criticism
> from others that is **not the Spirit's conviction**.

## 5. Spiritual disciplines as means of grace

**The tradition's claim:** disciplines are **means of grace** through which the Holy Spirit carries
truth from head to heart — **not performances that earn favour**. Dallas Willard's line carries the
section: grace **"is not opposed to effort, but to earning."** Willard also identified a
misunderstanding of grace that keeps it out of daily life as the great obstacle to growth.

| Discipline | What it is, and the tradition's claim | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Scripture meditation** | **Donald Whitney** contrasts hearing, reading and studying a text with letting it steep, like a tea bag left in the water. Methods: re-read a verse stressing a different word each time; paraphrase it; pray it using **ACTS** (adoration, confession, thanksgiving, supplication); memorise grace texts | Whitney's advice to **read less in order to meditate more** suits short attention spans |
| **Preaching the gospel to yourself** | Bridges' method: begin the day acknowledging sin, confess anything specific, then meditate on rotating grace texts — **Psalm 103:12, Isaiah 53:6, Romans 8:1, Colossians 2:13–14** — and on the hardest days **Isaiah 1:18** | Piper frames this as combat against accusation. ⚠️ For anxious, scrupulous or trauma-affected people, a gentler framing usually serves better |
| **Lament** | A large share of the Psalms are laments — commonly given as **over 40%**, though counts vary with how psalms are classified. **Mark Vroegop's** four movements: **turn** to God, **complain** honestly, **ask** boldly, **choose to trust**. **Elizabeth Lewis Hall** (Biola) argues words do not just express experience but shape it. Templates: **Psalms 13, 22, 42, 77, 88** | Lament is faith expressed in pain, not faith abandoned. On timing in trauma recovery, see §9.4 |
| **Sabbath** | Stopping work as an act of trust that the world runs on God's power, not one's productivity — a direct counter to performance-based identity | Especially relevant to burnout |
| **Community and confession** | **James 5:16**; CCEF teaches that the body of Christ is God's primary context for change. Hearing grace from another person lands differently from telling it to oneself | ⚠️ Only with **safe** people — see §10 |
| **The Lord's Supper** | Grace made physical — tasted and touched; the elements declare Christ's work regardless of how one feels that day | Embodied, not merely cognitive |
| **Gratitude** | **Psalm 103:1–5** — forget not his benefits. Three to five specific evidences of grace a day, written down | Evidence in §7 is modest |
| **Singing and memorised hymns** | Engages memory, emotion and body together; memorised hymns become "stored gospel" in a hard moment | — |

### 5.1 Adapting the disciplines for neurodivergent believers
Practical principles gathered in a 2024 *Christianity Today* feature and from Christian ADHD writers
(**practitioner guidance**, untested but low-risk):

- **Short blocks** — 5–10 minutes rather than 30 or more.
- **Variety** — rotate disciplines (lectio divina one week, prayer walking the next, art journaling
  the next) rather than enforcing a single routine.
- **Movement** — prayer walks; fidget-friendly worship; a standing or walking alternative to every
  seated practice.
- **External structure** — reminders, apps, body-doubling (doing the practice alongside someone).
- **Sensory options** — audio Bible, worship music, tactile prayer beads, candles; or, for
  sensory-sensitive people, quiet and low stimulation.
- **Grace for inconsistency — non-negotiable.** Bridges' axiom applies to the disciplines
  themselves: missed days are as much within grace as kept ones.

Stephen Grcevich of Key Ministry puts the theological point plainly: God did not set out to make
identical Christians. Liturgical variety, written prayers, walking prayer and sensory engagement with
communion are **old forms of worship**, not concessions.

## 6. Neurodivergent believers — what the emerging theology says

A growing body of theology is being written **by** autistic and otherwise neurodivergent Christians,
not only about them. Its recurring themes (**theological proposals and testimony, not research**):

- **Shame is the central wound; grace the central remedy.** Late recognition of neurodivergence often
  reframes a lifetime of unexplained difficulty — and adults may receive **less** grace than children,
  because others assume they "should know better" (**Lamar Hardwick**).
- **"Fearfully and wonderfully, if differently, made"** — **Daniel Bowman Jr.**'s phrase, from *On
  the Spectrum: Autism, Faith, and the Gifts of Neurodiversity*.
- **Participation in Christ.** **Grant Macaskill** (Centre for Autism and Theology, University of
  Aberdeen, founded 2018) argues that autistic identity within the body of Christ is understood
  through union with Christ — autism as something united to Christ and his body.
- **Recognition as a grace.** Writers such as **Laura Hurley** (The Gospel Coalition Australia) draw a
  parallel between a person coming to understand their neurotype and conversion: both explain why life
  has been hard and let a person see themselves clearly, and both can serve sanctification.
- **Fallen creation and "fearfully made."** Most neurodiversity-affirming evangelicals distinguish
  **the neurotype itself** from **the suffering of living in a world not built for it** (a Liberty
  University thesis; a Southeastern Baptist Theological Seminary piece reading **1 Corinthians
  12:12–26**). ⚠️ This is a live question; other evangelicals see some features of neurodivergence as
  effects of the fall, and the reference does not settle it.
- **Masking in church.** **Rebekah Drumsta**'s "Masking for Jesus" describes neurodivergent people
  learning that their natural traits — restlessness, questions, intensity, quirkiness — were treated
  as incompatible with godliness, until masking became exhausting and shame became the default. The
  counter-texts: **Psalm 139** (knit together by God), **1 Corinthians 13:12** (fully known). A writer
  for the Evangelical Alliance UK describes identity rooted in Christ as freedom **not to mask**.

**What a congregation can do:** stop moralising sensory, attention and social differences; offer
flexible ways to participate (quiet spaces, permission to move, clear and literal communication); and
treat the practices in §5.1 as normal, not as exceptions granted reluctantly.

## 7. What the clinical evidence shows

**Empirical claims, each with its standing.** Citations are given as the source gives them.

| Finding | Source | Standing |
|---|---|---|
| Higher self-compassion goes with fewer PTSD symptoms; intervention evidence "tentative" | Winders et al. (2020), systematic review of 35 studies, *Clinical Psychology & Psychotherapy* | **Moderate** for the association (mostly cross-sectional); **preliminary** for interventions |
| Self-compassion interventions reduce PTSD symptoms, medium effect (f = 0.25) | Luo et al. (2021), meta-analysis | **Preliminary–moderate** — small, heterogeneous trial base |
| Self-compassion is especially linked to the "disturbances in self-organisation" (identity, shame, relationships) of complex PTSD | Karatzias et al. (2019), *Behavioural and Cognitive Psychotherapy* | **Moderate** — cross-sectional |
| A 10-day self-compassion intervention reduced complex-PTSD symptoms, medium effect, largest on self-organisation | Huang et al. (2025), RCT | **Preliminary** — single trial |
| Self-compassion in general: strong inverse association with distress; interventions show moderate benefits | *Wider literature*: MacBeth & Gumley (2012) and Ferrari et al. (2019) meta-analyses | **Well-supported** association; **moderate** for interventions. The main self-compassion scale's negatively worded items are debated |
| Religiously/spiritually adapted treatments outperformed standard psychotherapy (g = 0.52 post-treatment, 0.72 follow-up), most for strongly religious clients | 2023 multilevel meta-analysis of 23 RCTs | **Moderate — read with the next row** |
| Against **other bona fide** therapies, adapted therapies are roughly **equal** on psychological outcomes and **better on spiritual** outcomes | *Wider literature*: Captari et al. (2018) meta-analysis | **Moderate.** ⚠️ So "outperforms" depends on the comparison; the safe claim is **at least as effective, and preferred by many religious clients** |
| About half of ~300 studies found lower anxiety in more religious people; 69% of 32 RCTs found R/S interventions reduced anxiety more than standard care | Koenig (2012) review | **Moderate** — vote-counting across mixed designs |
| A Christian accommodative mindfulness intervention reduced repetitive negative thinking | Knabb (2020), multisite RCT, *Spirituality in Clinical Practice* | **Preliminary** — compared with a **wait-list**, not an active treatment |
| A Christian mindfulness protocol with a self-compassion module reduced shame, depression and anxiety | Jones, Garzon & Ford (2021), N-of-1 time series | **Preliminary** — single-case design |
| Gratitude relates to lower PTSD (veterans; protective via positive affect); gratitude writing helped by **reducing negative emotion words** rather than adding positive ones | Kashdan et al. (2006); Israel-Cohen et al. (2015); Wong et al. (2018), RCT, *Psychotherapy Research* | **Moderate** for association; *wider literature* (Cregg & Cheavens, 2021) finds gratitude interventions' effects on depression and anxiety **small** |
| Positive religious coping (secure relationship with God) predicts better outcomes; **negative religious coping** (feeling abandoned or punished by God) predicts worse; collaborative coping does best | Pargament's Brief RCOPE research; *wider literature*: Ano & Vasconcelles (2005) meta-analysis | **Well-supported.** ⚠️ Spiritual struggle is a **risk marker** — a reason for closer care, not a verdict on someone's faith |
| The REACH forgiveness model, distinguishing decisional from emotional forgiveness | Worthington; 22+ RCTs per the source; *wider literature*: Wade et al. (2014) meta-analysis of forgiveness interventions | **Moderate–well-supported** for forgiving others; **less studied** for self-forgiveness |
| A trauma-focused therapy significantly reduced guilt and shame, which predicted PTSD symptoms | 2024 RCT, 107 participants | **Preliminary** — single trial |

## 8. Where the evidence runs out

The source is candid about the gaps, and they are large:

- **No studies** at the intersection of autism, trauma and Christian faith-based coping.
- **No validated "Christian self-compassion" measure.**
- **No self-compassion interventions studied specifically in autistic adults.**
- **Autistic burnout** was only defined in research terms in 2020 (Raymaker et al.) and has **no
  validated, evidence-based intervention**.
- **No study has compared theologically framed positive self-statements** (for example, "I am a new
  creation in Christ") with standard ones in trauma therapy. Faith-based practitioners use them; whether
  they add anything is **unknown**.
- Self-compassion publications grew from almost none in 2003 to over 4,000 by 2021 — a fast-moving
  field whose early findings will be revised.

> **⚠️ Adjacent evidence is not intersection evidence.** The source concludes that "the direction of
> evidence is clear" for faith-integrated self-compassion in neurodivergent trauma survivors. The
> honest version: **each ingredient has some support, and the combination is untested.** That is
> reason enough to try low-risk practices alongside proven treatment — not reason to treat them as
> proven themselves.

## 9. Working alongside professional care

### 9.1 When to refer — the red flags
Refer to, or insist on, licensed clinical care when there is:

- **Any suicidal thinking, plan or self-harm** — urgently (see the scope note).
- **Trauma symptoms** that disrupt life — intrusive memories, nightmares, flashbacks, dissociation,
  constant alertness, avoidance that is shrinking the person's world.
- **Burnout or depression with loss of function** lasting more than a couple of weeks, or getting worse.
- **Substance misuse, disordered eating, psychosis, or mania.**
- **Ongoing abuse** — and if a child or vulnerable adult is involved, the legal reporting duties where
  you are.
- **Spiritual struggle that is intensifying** — feeling abandoned or punished by God (§7: a recognised
  risk marker).
- **Suspected undiagnosed autism or ADHD** — assessment by a qualified clinician, not a pastoral guess.

### 9.2 What professional treatment looks like
For trauma, **trauma-focused psychotherapies** — such as trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy,
EMDR, cognitive processing therapy and prolonged exposure — are **first-line in major clinical
guidelines** (**Standing: well-supported**). Depression and anxiety have their own effective
treatments, including medication where a physician judges it appropriate. Taking them is not a lack
of faith.

### 9.3 How the two fit together
- **The person decides** whether and how faith enters their therapy. Many clinicians will work with a
  client's faith respectfully; Christian clinicians exist, but **licensure and trauma competence come
  first**, and a neurodivergent-informed clinician matters for autistic and ADHD adults.
- **Keep roles separate.** A pastor is not the therapist and should not become one; pastoral care
  supplies what therapy does not — presence, prayer, Scripture, sacrament, community, practical help.
- **With consent, coordinate.** Practices in §5 can reinforce therapeutic work — for instance, an
  identity truth the person has chosen can be rehearsed in prayer — while §8 is honest that the
  faith-framed versions are not shown to work better.
- **Confidentiality and safety.** What is shared pastorally stays within the limits the person agreed
  to, except where law or immediate safety requires otherwise.

### 9.4 Pacing and the stabilisation question
Many trauma clinicians follow a **phase-based** approach after **Judith Herman** — safety and
stabilisation, then processing and grieving, then reconnection. **Diane Langberg**'s summary of what
healing takes — **"talking, tears, and time"** — is good pastoral counsel about speed. ⚠️ Whether a
long stabilisation phase must come before trauma processing is **debated among trauma researchers**
(*wider literature*, for example de Jongh et al., 2016); that sequencing is the **clinician's call**,
not the pastor's. The source, for instance, holds that lament should wait until stabilisation is
established; others find the structure of lament a safe container from the start. Follow the person
and their clinician.

## 10. How this goes wrong — pastoral failure modes

| Failure | What it looks like | The correction |
|---|---|---|
| **Grace as a new accusation** | "Not accepting grace is unbelief" said to someone already crushed | Offer grace as relief and invitation (§1.3) |
| **Spiritual bypassing** | Verses instead of treatment; "just pray more"; symptoms treated as sin or weak faith | Refer (§9.1); Scripture and therapy are not rivals |
| **Moralising neurology** | Sensory overload, executive failure or autistic directness treated as character flaws | Separate moral events from neurological ones (§3) |
| **Disciplines as performance** | A missed quiet time becomes fresh evidence of failure | Grace for inconsistency (§5.1) |
| **Unsafe confession** | Confession pressed in a group, or in the setting where harm happened | Confession only with safe, chosen people; never compelled |
| **Spiritual abuse** | The church itself was the source of harm, through control, coercion or misuse of authority | Believe it; slow down; outside help; safeguarding where relevant |
| **Forced forgiveness** | Pressure to forgive quickly, or to reconcile with someone who harmed them | Forgiveness, reconciliation and restored trust are **different things**; REACH separates decisional from emotional forgiveness, and neither requires renewed contact |
| **Promising shame will vanish** | Healing defined as never feeling shame again | Oppose shame rather than promising its eradication (§3) |
| **Combat framing for the scrupulous** | "This is war" language that heightens anxiety | Choose rest and trust framings for anxious or trauma-affected people |
| **Pastoral guesswork** | Diagnosing autism, ADHD, PTSD or "demonic oppression" from the pastor's chair | Leave diagnosis to clinicians |

## 11. An optional pacing outline

**⚠️ Optional, generic, and not a treatment plan.** Months, not weeks; the order and pace are set with
the person and, where trauma is involved, with their clinician (§9.4). Skip, repeat or reorder
freely.

| Movement | Focus | Practices that fit |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Safety** | Steadiness with God and with a few safe people; basic care of body and nervous system | Short identity statements (McGee or Anderson); brief meditation on Psalms of God's character (**Psalms 23, 103, 139:1–6, 145**); gratitude; conversational, unperformed prayer; identifying one or two safe people; examining one's picture of God — safe, punitive or distant |
| **2. Honest engagement** | Telling the truth to God about pain and about wrongs done | Lament using Vroegop's four movements; confession with a safe person; Bridges' gospel preaching after hard moments; mapping performance-trap and rejection patterns to the false beliefs in §4.1; trauma processing, if any, within therapy |
| **3. Reintegration** | Living from received identity in community, work and service | Embodied practices (walking prayer, creative worship); receiving care without performing; serving from identity rather than for approval; a personal **rule of life** built on variety rather than rigid routine; plans for returning to grace after setbacks |
