## §8. Navigation and Being Lost

**⚠️ Prevention**: ⚠️ **carry a map and compass AND know how to use them (⚠️ a compass you
can't take a bearing with is a decoration); ⚠️ carry a GPS or phone with OFFLINE maps and
a battery pack; ⚠️ and use both, because electronics fail and paper doesn't tell you where
you are.**
**⚠️ The habits that prevent lost**: ⚠️ **look BACKWARD periodically (the return route
looks completely different); note landmarks and time at junctions; ⚠️ track your position
continuously rather than reconstructing it later; and set a TURNAROUND TIME and honour it**
(§2 → `survival-what-kills-psychology-and-priorities`'s summit fever).
**⚠️ Handrails, catching features and aiming off** — ⚠️ **the classic navigation
techniques: follow a linear feature, know what you'll hit if you overshoot, and
deliberately aim to one side of a target so you know which way to turn when you reach the
handrail.**
**⚠️ If lost**: ⚠️ **STOP (§2 → `survival-what-kills-psychology-and-priorities`). Do not keep walking. Mark your position. Reconstruct your
last known point.** ⚠️ **If you must move, prefer DOWNHILL toward drainages in most
terrain — water leads to larger water, then to trails, roads and habitation** — **⚠️ with
the important exception of canyon and slot terrain, where downhill leads to
impassable drops and flash flood risk.**

## §9. ⚠️ Signalling and Rescue

> **⚠️ The highest-value section for the effort involved, and the most neglected.**
> ⚠️ **Almost every survival situation ends in rescue, so anything that makes rescue
> faster is worth more than anything that extends survival.**
```
⚠️ THE SINGLE HIGHEST-VALUE ACTION IS TAKEN BEFORE YOU LEAVE:
   ⚠️ TELL SOMEONE WHERE YOU'RE GOING AND WHEN YOU'RE DUE BACK,
   with instructions to call for help if you don't check in.
   ⚠️ SAR research indicates the speed at which someone is reported
   missing is a dominant predictor of outcome (§23.1)
⚠️ SATELLITE MESSENGERS AND PLBs  ⚠️ the single biggest technological
   improvement to backcountry safety. A PLB or two-way satellite
   messenger works where phones don't and transmits your position
   ⚠️ Newer phones with satellite SOS extend this to casual users
⚠️ PASSIVE SIGNALS (work while you rest — ⚠️ prefer these)
   ⚠️ Bright colours in clearings · ground-to-air symbols large and
   with contrast/shadow · ⚠️ anything geometric, because nature has
   few straight lines
⚠️ ACTIVE SIGNALS
   ⚠️ SIGNAL MIRROR — ⚠️ visible at extraordinary distances and
   weighs nothing. Aim through the sighting hole
   ⚠️ WHISTLE — ⚠️ carries far further than shouting and costs almost
   no energy. ⚠️ Three of anything is the distress convention
   ⚠️ FIRE and SMOKE — three fires in a triangle; green vegetation
   for white smoke by day, dry fuel for flame at night
   Torch/headlamp, ⚠️ ideally with a strobe mode
```
**⚠️ When you hear or see searchers**: ⚠️ **respond immediately and keep responding —
searchers frequently pass close by.** **⚠️ Do not assume they have seen you.**
**⚠️ Helicopter safety**: ⚠️ **approach only when signalled, from the front where the pilot
can see you, crouched, never from the rear (tail rotor), and secure everything loose.**
**⚠️ Do not turn off your phone to save battery** — ⚠️ **airplane mode with periodic checks
preserves battery while allowing location attempts; and even without signal, a phone may
be locatable and stores your track.**

## §10. ⚠️ Field Medical Priorities

> **⚠️ Conceptual only. Take a wilderness first aid course — this material genuinely
> requires hands-on training, and a course is a weekend.**
```
⚠️ THE ORDER  scene safety FIRST (⚠️ do not become a second casualty) →
   ⚠️ catastrophic haemorrhage → airway → breathing → circulation →
   disability/spine → environment (⚠️ hypothermia, §4)
⚠️ MASSIVE BLEEDING  ⚠️ direct pressure, hard and sustained; wound
   packing for junctional wounds; ⚠️ TOURNIQUET high and tight for
   life-threatening limb bleeding — ⚠️ modern practice regards
   tourniquets as clearly life-saving and the historical fear of
   them as overstated. ⚠️ Note the time applied
⚠️ WILDERNESS DIFFERENCES FROM URBAN FIRST AID
   ⚠️ Definitive care is HOURS or DAYS away, not minutes
   ⚠️ You may have to move the patient, and you may have to
      decide to leave them to get help
   ⚠️ Improvisation and prolonged care become necessary
   ⚠️ Evacuation decisions are themselves medical decisions
```
**⚠️ Common wilderness problems**: **blisters (⚠️ treat at the "hot spot" stage, before
they form), sprains and fractures (⚠️ splint as found, check circulation before and
after), burns, wounds (⚠️ irrigation with clean water under pressure is the single most
important wound intervention), and dehydration.**
**⚠️ Spinal precautions have been substantially reconsidered** — ⚠️ **rigid immobilization
of every possible spinal injury is no longer standard, and prolonged immobilization in the
field carries its own harms.**
**⚠️ Carry what you'll actually use and know how to use it** — ⚠️ **a large kit you can't
deploy is worse than a small one you can.**

## §11. Heat Illness

```
⚠️ HEAT EXHAUSTION  heavy sweating, weakness, nausea, headache,
   ⚠️ MENTAL STATUS INTACT. Rest, shade, fluids, electrolytes
⚠️ HEAT STROKE  ⚠️ ALTERED MENTAL STATUS + high core temperature.
   ⚠️ A MEDICAL EMERGENCY with a mortality that rises with every
   minute of delay
⚠️ THE DISTINGUISHING SIGN IS MENTAL STATUS, NOT SWEATING —
   ⚠️ "dry skin" is NOT required; exertional heat stroke frequently
   presents with the patient still sweating profusely
⚠️ TREATMENT  ⚠️ COOL FIRST, TRANSPORT SECOND. Cold water immersion
   is the most effective field cooling method where available;
   otherwise wet the skin and maximize airflow, ice to neck,
   armpits and groin
```
**⚠️ Hyponatremia is the mirror-image error and it is mistaken for dehydration**:
⚠️ **drinking large volumes of plain water while losing sodium through sweat produces
confusion, nausea and headache — symptoms that look like dehydration, and giving more
plain water makes it worse and can be fatal.** ⚠️ **Include electrolytes and food on long
exertion, and be suspicious when someone who has been drinking heavily gets worse.**
**⚠️ Wet-bulb temperature is the meaningful heat-stress metric** (see a refrigeration/
psychrometrics reference) — ⚠️ **because evaporative cooling fails as humidity rises.**

## §12. ⚠️ Animals, In Proportion

> **⚠️ Wildlife is far down the risk list (§1 → `survival-what-kills-psychology-and-priorities`), and the fear is disproportionate to the
> hazard almost everywhere.**
**⚠️ The realistic ordering of animal-related harm**: ⚠️ **insects and arachnids as disease
vectors far exceed everything else; then large herbivores (⚠️ which injure more people
than predators in most parks — moose, bison, deer); then dogs; then venomous snakes; then
large predators.**
**⚠️ General principles**: ⚠️ **distance and food storage prevent nearly all conflict;
never come between a mother and young; do not run from most predators; and the correct
response varies by species, so learn the local specifics rather than a general rule.**
**⚠️ Snakebite** — ⚠️ **see §23.2 → `survival-reference` for current guidance, because the folklore here is
actively harmful.**
**⚠️ Insect-borne disease** is the genuine wildlife risk in most of the world: ⚠️ **bite
avoidance, permethrin-treated clothing, repellent, and tick checks with prompt correct
removal.**
