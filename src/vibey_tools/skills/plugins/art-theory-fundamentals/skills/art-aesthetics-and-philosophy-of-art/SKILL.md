## §8. The Questions

**⚠️ Aesthetics asks a small number of hard questions that have not been settled in 2,500
years, and the lack of settlement is not a failure of effort:**
```
⚠️ What is art? (§12)
⚠️ What is beauty, and is it in the object or the response? (§9)
⚠️ Are aesthetic judgements merely personal? (§10)
⚠️ What is aesthetic experience, and is it a distinct kind? (§11)
⚠️ Why do we value art? (§11, §13)
⚠️ Does an artwork's moral character affect its aesthetic value? (§13)
⚠️ Does the artist's intention determine meaning? (§20)
```

## §9. Beauty and the Sublime

**⚠️ The classical position (Plato, Aristotle, Aquinas)**: **beauty is objective —
a property of the object consisting in something like proportion, integrity, harmony and
radiance.** ⚠️ **Aquinas's formulation — *integritas, consonantia, claritas* — is
compact and still worth thinking with.** **Beauty on this view is linked to being and to
truth, not merely to pleasure.**

**⚠️ The turn to the subject (18th century)** — **beauty relocated from object to
response.**
**⚠️ Kant is the pivotal figure and worth understanding properly**, because he tried to
hold both:
- **⚠️ The judgement of beauty is DISINTERESTED** — **not about whether you want, need or
  can use the thing.**
- **⚠️ It claims UNIVERSALITY without a concept**: **when you call something beautiful you
  are not merely reporting a preference — you speak as though others ought to agree** —
  **yet you cannot produce a rule that proves it.**
- **⚠️ "Purposiveness without purpose"** — **it appears designed for a end you cannot
  specify.**
- **Free vs dependent beauty**; **and genius as the capacity to produce exemplary work
  without following rules.**

**⚠️ The sublime is a separate category and a genuinely useful one**: **Burke and Kant on
the overwhelming — vastness, power, terror held at a safe distance** — ⚠️ **pleasure mixed
with a kind of fear, and NOT a species of beauty.** **It's what the beautiful cannot
account for: storms, mountains, the enormous, the terrible.** **Much Romantic and some
contemporary work is aiming at the sublime, not the beautiful, and judging it by beauty's
standards misses the point.**

**⚠️ Modern and contemporary art's relationship to beauty is complicated and worth stating
carefully.** **Much 20th-century avant-garde practice deliberately rejected beauty as
bourgeois, decorative, or complicit** — ⚠️ **and a strand of recent criticism (Scarry,
Nehamas, and others) has argued the rejection was overdone and that beauty deserves
rehabilitation as a serious category.** **This is a live argument, not a settled
progression.**

## §10. Taste and Judgement

**⚠️ Hume's "Of the Standard of Taste" is the best short treatment of the problem, and
his answer is neither relativist nor dogmatic.** **The puzzle: taste appears personal,
yet we do not actually treat all judgements as equal** — ⚠️ **anyone claiming a mediocre
hack equals Shakespeare is not expressing a preference we respect but making an error.**
**Hume's solution is the QUALIFIED JUDGE: delicacy of sentiment, practice, comparison,
freedom from prejudice, and good sense.** ⚠️ **The standard is the converging verdict of
such judges over time — which is a sociological answer to a philosophical question, and
whether that's satisfying is exactly the debate.**

**⚠️ Kant's antinomy of taste** states the difficulty cleanly: **judgements of taste
cannot be based on concepts (or we could prove them by argument), yet they must be based
on something (or we couldn't meaningfully disagree).**
**⚠️ Bourdieu's sociological challenge is the strongest modern objection**: **taste is
socially produced and functions as class distinction** — **"aesthetic disposition" is
acquired through upbringing and education, and disinterested contemplation is itself a
class-marked stance available to those with the leisure for it** (see a sociology
reference §17). ⚠️ **This does not by itself refute Kant — showing how a judgement is
acquired doesn't show it's wrong — but it is a serious pressure on any claim that taste is
purely a faculty.**

## §11. Theories of Art

```
MIMETIC        ⚠️ art as imitation/representation. Plato (suspicious — twice
               removed from the Forms), Aristotle (favourable — catharsis).
               ⚠️ Dominant for two millennia; broken by abstraction
EXPRESSION     ⚠️ art as the expression/transmission of emotion. Tolstoy,
               Collingwood, Croce. ⚠️ Problem: much great art expresses nothing
               in particular, and much emotional outpouring isn't art
FORMALISM      ⚠️ value lies in FORM, not subject. Bell's "significant form,"
               Fry, later Greenberg. ⚠️ Problem: "significant form" is defined
               circularly as the form that produces aesthetic emotion
INSTITUTIONAL  ⚠️ Dickie — art is what the artworld confers the status on.
               Handles Duchamp; ⚠️ criticized as circular and as ceding the
               question to whoever holds institutional power
HISTORICAL     ⚠️ Danto, Levinson — something is art in virtue of its relation
               to prior art and an interpretive context. ⚠️ Danto's insight:
               Warhol's Brillo Box is visually indiscernible from a Brillo box,
               so whatever makes it art is NOT VISIBLE
AESTHETIC      ⚠️ art as what's made for aesthetic experience. Beardsley
CLUSTER        ⚠️ Gaut, Weitz — no necessary and sufficient conditions; a
               family-resemblance cluster of typical features
```
**⚠️ The honest position most philosophers hold now**: **no single definition works, the
cluster/family-resemblance approach handles the cases best, and the failure of definition
is informative rather than embarrassing** (§12).

## §12. ⚠️ The Definition Problem

**⚠️ The interesting fact about "what is art?" is that every proposed definition is either
too narrow or too broad, and this has been true for a century.**
- **Mimetic definitions exclude abstraction.**
- **Expression definitions exclude a great deal of formal, conceptual and decorative work
  — and include a tantrum.**
- **Formalist definitions exclude conceptual art and can't ground "significant."**
- **Institutional definitions include anything a gallery shows and can't explain why the
  institution's judgement should count.**

**⚠️ Weitz's argument, following Wittgenstein**: **art is an OPEN CONCEPT.** **Its
conditions of application are perpetually revisable because novelty and boundary-pushing
are internal to the practice.** ⚠️ **Any definition tight enough to be informative will be
falsified by the next thing artists do — deliberately, because falsifying it is part of
what artists do.**
**⚠️ The practically useful reframe**: **stop asking "is this art?" and ask "what does
calling it art do?"** — **it invokes a mode of attention, a set of institutions, a market,
a critical tradition and a history.** ⚠️ **"Is it art?" is almost always a proxy for "is it
any good?" or "should this be here?", and those are the questions actually in dispute
(§1 → `art-claims-elements-colour-and-composition`).**

## §13. Art and Ethics

**⚠️ The central question: does an artwork's moral character bear on its aesthetic value?**
```
AUTONOMISM     ⚠️ no. Aesthetic and moral value are independent domains
MORALISM       ⚠️ yes, and a moral defect is an aesthetic defect
ETHICISM/      ⚠️ Carroll, Gaut — moral defects can be aesthetic defects
 MODERATE       WHERE they undermine the response the work solicits.
 MORALISM       ⚠️ The most defensible position: if a comedy asks you to find
                cruelty funny and you can't, the work has failed AS a comedy
```
**⚠️ Riefenstahl's *Triumph of the Will* is the standard test case**: **formally
accomplished, morally monstrous, and any theory has to say something about it.**
**⚠️ Separating art from artist is a genuine dilemma, not a slogan.** **The honest position
is that there are real considerations on both sides** — **the work exists independently of
its maker's biography; and consumption has material consequences, and knowledge changes
what you can see in a work** — ⚠️ **and that people weigh these differently in good faith.**
**⚠️ Censorship, iconoclasm, restitution and repatriation** are where these questions
become institutional rather than personal (§22 → `art-interpretation-criticism-and-the-art-world`).
