>
> **Currency:** Aesthetics and formal theory are stable to the point of being ancient. Two areas are live. See §23 → `art-reference` for AI authorship and copyright, and the state of the art market.

> **⚠️ Read §1 → `art-claims-elements-colour-and-composition` first, because the epistemic situation here is unlike the sciences and
> unlike the humanities that model themselves on sciences.**
>
> **⚠️ Art theory is not one thing. It is at least four things with different standards of
> truth**, **and confusing them is the source of nearly every bad argument about art:**
> **craft knowledge that is testable**, **philosophical claims that are argued**,
> **historical claims that are evidenced**, **and interpretive claims that are neither
> true nor false but better or worse.**
>
> **⚠️ GOTCHA** boxes mark widely-taught claims that are false, or fights that are
> commonly mistaken for settled.
>
> **The three ideas that organize this document:**
> 1. **⚠️ "Is it good?" and "is it art?" and "do I like it?" are three different questions**
>    with three different kinds of answer, **and most public argument about art is people
>    answering different ones at each other** (§1 → `art-claims-elements-colour-and-composition`, §9–§12 → `art-aesthetics-and-philosophy-of-art`).
> 2. **⚠️ The formal layer is genuinely teachable and largely objective in its effects.**
>    **Value structure, edge control and composition do predictable things to a viewer**
>    (§2–§7 → `art-claims-elements-colour-and-composition`). **This is the part that's craft, and it's the part most theory-first
>    treatments skip.**
> 3. **⚠️ Almost every "rule" in art instruction is a heuristic with a good hit rate**,
>    **not a law** — **and knowing why it usually works tells you when to break it.**

## §14. A Skeleton

**⚠️ Offered as a filing system, not a narrative of progress** (§16).
```
PREHISTORIC       ⚠️ Chauvet/Lascaux ~30,000+ BP. Sophisticated from the start
ANCIENT           Egyptian (⚠️ hieratic scale, remarkable stability over 3000 yrs)
                  Mesopotamian · Greek (⚠️ archaic → classical contrapposto →
                  hellenistic drama) · Roman (portraiture, engineering, copies)
MEDIEVAL          Byzantine (⚠️ icon, gold ground, non-naturalistic BY INTENT)
                  Romanesque · Gothic (light, verticality, stained glass)
RENAISSANCE       ⚠️ perspective, anatomy, antiquity, the individual artist
                  Early/High Italian · Northern (⚠️ oil, minute observation)
MANNERISM         elongation, artifice, instability
BAROQUE           ⚠️ drama, tenebrism (Caravaggio), motion, Counter-Reformation
                  Dutch Golden Age (⚠️ a genuinely different, market-driven model)
ROCOCO → NEOCLASSICAL → ROMANTIC   ⚠️ the sublime, nature, the individual (§9)
REALISM           Courbet — ordinary life as fit subject
IMPRESSIONISM     ⚠️ optical sensation, plein air, the sketch finished
POST-IMPRESSIONISM  Cézanne (⚠️ structure — the hinge into modernism), Van Gogh,
                  Gauguin, Seurat
MODERNISM         §15
POSTMODERN / CONTEMPORARY   §15
```
**⚠️ Non-Western traditions with their own long theoretical literatures** — **and each has
a developed body of theory, not just objects:** **Chinese painting** (⚠️ **Xie He's Six
Principles, c. 6th century — "spirit resonance / life-motion" first, likeness fifth; the
literati tradition; brushwork as the trace of the person**), **Japanese** (⚠️ ***mono no
aware***, ***wabi-sabi*** — **an aesthetics of imperfection, impermanence and
incompleteness that has no close Western equivalent**), **Indian** (⚠️ ***rasa* theory** —
**the aesthetic emotions a work evokes, systematically enumerated; and the *Natyashastra*
predates most Western aesthetics**), **Islamic** (⚠️ **geometry, calligraphy and
arabesque as a fully developed non-figurative visual system with theological grounding**),
**African, Oceanic, and Indigenous American traditions.**

## §15. Modernism to Contemporary

**⚠️ Modernism's programme, roughly**: **autonomy of the artwork, medium-specificity,
formal innovation, and the rejection of representation as painting's job** — **because
photography had taken it.**
```
Fauvism · Expressionism · ⚠️ CUBISM (the pivot — multiple viewpoints, the
  picture plane asserted) · Futurism · Dada (⚠️ anti-art as art; Duchamp's
  readymade, which broke everything downstream) · Surrealism · De Stijl ·
  Bauhaus · Constructivism · Abstract Expressionism · Minimalism ·
  Pop (⚠️ mass culture as subject and as method) · Conceptual (⚠️ the IDEA is
  the work; the object optional)
```
**⚠️ Greenberg's formalist account — that each art purifies itself toward what only it can
do, painting toward flatness — was enormously influential and is now generally regarded as
too narrow and too teleological.** **It is a theory that made a particular avant-garde
look inevitable.**
**⚠️ Postmodernism, as a set of moves rather than a period**: **the rejection of grand
narratives, pastiche and appropriation, irony, the collapse of high/low distinctions, and
suspicion of originality and the author** (§20 → `art-interpretation-criticism-and-the-art-world`).
**⚠️ Contemporary art is not a style and this confuses people.** **It's a condition:
pluralist, global, discourse-heavy, institutionally mediated, frequently research-based or
socially engaged.** ⚠️ **There is no dominant medium, no shared programme, and no criteria
everyone accepts** — **which is precisely why §12 → `art-aesthetics-and-philosophy-of-art`'s definitional question feels urgent
now and didn't in 1650.**

## §16. ⚠️ The Canon Problem

**⚠️ The Western canon was assembled by particular people, in particular institutions, with
particular commitments — and that is a historical claim, not a polemical one.**
- **⚠️ Vasari essentially invented art history as a genre in 1550, and did so as a
  narrative of Florentine progress culminating in Michelangelo.** **The
  progress-toward-naturalism frame has been shaping the field ever since.**
- **⚠️ The craft/art hierarchy** — **textiles, ceramics, and decorative work relegated
  below painting and sculpture — tracks gendered and class divisions of labour closely
  enough that the correlation is hard to dismiss.**
- **⚠️ "Why have there been no great women artists?" (Nochlin, 1971) is the essential
  text**, and **its answer is the sophisticated one: not that women lacked genius, but
  that the institutional conditions of artistic production — academy access, life-drawing
  from the nude, apprenticeship, patronage, guild membership — were structurally
  closed.** ⚠️ **The question is answered by examining institutions, not by finding
  overlooked geniuses, though there were plenty of those too.**
- **⚠️ Non-Western art was long filtered through categories that distorted it** —
  **"primitive," ethnographic display rather than aesthetic display, and the anonymity
  imposed on makers whose names were simply never recorded by collectors.**

**⚠️ The honest position**: **canon revision is ordinary scholarly work and it has been
enormously productive.** ⚠️ **The genuine methodological risk on the other side — applying
present-day categories anachronistically, or judging works solely by their politics — is
also real and is criticized from within the field.** **Both cautions are live, and a
reader should hold both.**
