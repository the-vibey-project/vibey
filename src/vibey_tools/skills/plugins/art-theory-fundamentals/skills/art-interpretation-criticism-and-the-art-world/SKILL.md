# Art Theory Fundamentals: Iconography, Semiotics, Critical Lenses, Intention, Looking, and the Art World

>
> **Currency:** Aesthetics and formal theory are stable to the point of being ancient. Two areas are live. See §23 → `art-reference` for AI authorship and copyright, and the state of the art market.

> **⚠️ Read §1 → `art-claims-elements-colour-and-composition` first, because the epistemic situation here is unlike the sciences and
> unlike the humanities that model themselves on sciences.**
>
> **⚠️ Art theory is not one thing. It is at least four things with different standards of
> truth**, **and confusing them is the source of nearly every bad argument about art:**
> **craft knowledge that is testable**, **philosophical claims that are argued**,
> **historical claims that are evidenced**, **and interpretive claims that are neither
> true nor false but better or worse.**
>
> **⚠️ GOTCHA** boxes mark widely-taught claims that are false, or fights that are
> commonly mistaken for settled.
>
> **The three ideas that organize this document:**
> 1. **⚠️ "Is it good?" and "is it art?" and "do I like it?" are three different questions**
>    with three different kinds of answer, **and most public argument about art is people
>    answering different ones at each other** (§1 → `art-claims-elements-colour-and-composition`, §9–§12 → `art-aesthetics-and-philosophy-of-art`).
> 2. **⚠️ The formal layer is genuinely teachable and largely objective in its effects.**
>    **Value structure, edge control and composition do predictable things to a viewer**
>    (§2–§7 → `art-claims-elements-colour-and-composition`). **This is the part that's craft, and it's the part most theory-first
>    treatments skip.**
> 3. **⚠️ Almost every "rule" in art instruction is a heuristic with a good hit rate**,
>    **not a law** — **and knowing why it usually works tells you when to break it.**

## §17. Iconography and Iconology

**⚠️ Panofsky's three levels remain the most useful interpretive scaffold in art history:**
```
1. PRE-ICONOGRAPHIC   ⚠️ what is literally depicted. A man, a knife, a lamb
2. ICONOGRAPHIC       ⚠️ what it CONVENTIONALLY means. Requires knowing the
   textual and pictorial tradition — that's Abraham and Isaac. ⚠️ MOST
   pre-modern Western art is illegible without this layer, and modern
   viewers routinely don't have it
3. ICONOLOGICAL       ⚠️ what it reveals about the culture, period, and
   worldview that produced it. The most speculative and most interesting
```
**⚠️ Attributes are the practical vocabulary**: **saints and figures are identified by
objects — keys for Peter, wheel for Catherine, lion for Mark.** ⚠️ **A large fraction of
Western art before 1800 is a legible symbolic language that has simply been forgotten**,
and **learning even a little of it transforms what a museum visit is.**
**⚠️ Warburg's contribution** — **tracing the migration and survival of visual motifs
across periods and cultures** — **and Panofsky's method is criticized for assuming
programmes were more coherent and more textual than they often were.**

## §18. Semiotics

**Sign = signifier + signified** (Saussure); **⚠️ Peirce's more useful triad for visual
work: ICON (resembles — a portrait), INDEX (physically caused by — a footprint, a
photograph, a shadow), SYMBOL (conventional — a flag).**
⚠️ **The indexical character of photography is why it carries evidentiary force that
painting doesn't, and why generative images unsettle that** (§23.1 → `art-reference`).
**Denotation vs connotation; codes; Barthes on the "rhetoric of the image" and the
myth-making function of visual culture.**
**⚠️ Semiotics is powerful for advertising, film and mass imagery, and can become
mechanical when applied to works whose interest isn't primarily communicative.**

## §19. Critical Lenses

```
FORMALIST      ⚠️ what's in the work. Blind to context and honest about it
BIOGRAPHICAL   the maker's life. ⚠️ Prone to §20's fallacy
SOCIAL HISTORY OF ART   ⚠️ T.J. Clark et al. — patronage, class, the market,
   material conditions of production
MARXIST        ideology, commodity, labour, whose interests the work serves
FEMINIST       ⚠️ representation, the gaze (Mulvey), authorship, institutions (§16)
PSYCHOANALYTIC ⚠️ Freud/Lacan — desire, the unconscious, the gaze. Explanatorily
   rich and empirically unfalsifiable; use knowingly
POSTCOLONIAL   ⚠️ Said's Orientalism; representation of the other; museums as
   colonial institutions (§22)
RECEPTION      ⚠️ Jauss, Iser — meaning is completed by the viewer; the
   "period eye" (Baxandall) — viewers of 1450 brought different competences
MATERIAL/TECHNICAL   ⚠️ pigment analysis, X-ray, dendrochronology — the closest
   thing to hard evidence in the field, and it settles real disputes
```
**⚠️ The mature use of these is as a set of questions, not a set of verdicts.** **Each
illuminates some works well and others badly.** ⚠️ **The characteristic failure is
mono-lens reading — where the conclusion is determined before the work is examined, and
the work becomes an occasion rather than a subject.** **This is a criticism made vigorously
from inside the discipline, not only from outside it.**

## §20. Intention and the Author

**⚠️ The intentional fallacy (Wimsatt & Beardsley, 1946)**: **the author's intention is
neither available nor decisive for interpreting the work.** **Barthes' "Death of the
Author" (1967) pushed further — meaning is made in reading, not deposited by writing.**
**⚠️ The counter-case is strong and shouldn't be dismissed**: **intention is unavoidable
for basic questions — is that irony? is that a mistake or a choice? — and moderate
actual intentionalism (the intention constrains but doesn't determine meaning) is a
widely-held middle position.**
**⚠️ The practical resolution most working critics use**: **evidence in the work outranks
stated intention; stated intention is evidence, not proof; and a reading that requires
the artist to have been unaware of something obvious is usually wrong.**
> **⚠️ GOTCHA — "the curtains were just blue" is the internet's favourite objection and
> it's a bad one, but so is the target it aims at.** ⚠️ **Sometimes the curtains are just
> blue, and sometimes an artist has selected every element over months of deliberate
> work.** **The question is always whether the reading is SUPPORTED — by the work, the
> tradition, the context** — **and support is arguable in both directions** (§1 → `art-claims-elements-colour-and-composition`, register 4).

## §21. How to Actually Look

**⚠️ A sequence that works, and most people skip straight to step 5:**
```
1. ⚠️ LOOK LONGER THAN IS COMFORTABLE. Museum studies repeatedly find median
   viewing times measured in SECONDS. Give it minutes
2. DESCRIBE literally. What is actually there? (§17 level 1)
3. ⚠️ SQUINT. What's the value structure? Where does your eye go first, and
   WHY did it go there? (§3, §5)
4. Formal analysis — composition, colour, edge, scale, medium (§2–§7)
5. Subject and iconography — what does it depict, conventionally? (§17)
6. CONTEXT — when, where, for whom, for what setting, who paid (§19)
7. ⚠️ INTERPRET, and check the reading against 2–6. Does it account for
   what's actually there?
8. ⚠️ Evaluate — and keep "is it good?" separate from "do I like it?" (§1)
```
**⚠️ Two things that improve looking more than any amount of theory**: **seeing work in
person** (⚠️ **scale, surface, edge quality and colour are substantially lost in
reproduction, and scale in particular is routinely misjudged from photographs**), **and
drawing** — **even badly.** ⚠️ **Attempting to render something forces the sustained
observation that looking alone rarely produces.**

## §22. The Art World and Market

**⚠️ Institutional structure shapes what gets made, kept and seen, and this is not
cynicism — it's how the field operates.**
```
PRIMARY MARKET     ⚠️ first sale, via gallery. Galleries control PLACEMENT, not
   just price — who is allowed to buy affects an artist's trajectory
SECONDARY MARKET   ⚠️ resale, auction. Public prices, and the artist usually
   receives nothing (droit de suite/resale royalty exists in some jurisdictions)
MUSEUMS            ⚠️ acquisition, deaccessioning, conservation, canon-formation
CRITICS/ACADEMY    legitimation and discourse
BIENNIALS/FAIRS    ⚠️ increasingly the primary discovery channel
```
**⚠️ Economic peculiarities worth knowing**: **art is a positional and Veblen good
(demand can rise with price)**; **quality is unverifiable, so REPUTATION and PROVENANCE
substitute for it**; ⚠️ **prices are highly concentrated in a few names**; **and
authenticity, attribution and forgery are perennial** — **the Knoedler case is the
instructive modern example, where an established gallery sold forgeries for years.**
**⚠️ Provenance research also carries the restitution question**: **Nazi-era looted art
and colonial-era acquisition are active legal and ethical issues for major collections,
not historical footnotes** (§13 → `art-aesthetics-and-philosophy-of-art`).
