*Part II opens here.* Everything in livestock is a system with feedback, and single-variable
optimization reliably backfires: maximize milk and lose fertility, maximize growth and increase
maintenance cost, maximize stocking rate and lose the pasture that fed the animals. The two engines
of livestock agriculture are **the soil and the rumen** — most of livestock management is managing
microbial communities you cannot see. (Soil as the other engine: §15–§16 → `homestead-soil-and-fertility`.)

## §8 Ruminant Digestion — Why Cattle Are Different

Ruminant digestion is the single most important mechanism in livestock science because it explains
what ruminants are **for**: converting grass and crop residue that humans cannot eat into food
humans can eat.

### The four stomach compartments

A ruminant has four stomach compartments.

| Compartment | What it does |
|---|---|
| Rumen | A large fermentation vat of bacteria, protozoa, fungi, and archaea. **The animal does not digest cellulose, the microbes do.** |
| Reticulum | Traps foreign objects — hardware disease from swallowed nails or wire is a real risk. |
| Omasum | Water and volatile fatty acid absorption. |
| Abomasum | The "true" acid stomach, equivalent to a monogastric's stomach. |

### Volatile fatty acids and microbial protein

The rumen microbes produce **volatile fatty acids (acetate, propionate, butyrate)**, which are the
ruminant's main energy source — **not glucose**.

They also produce **microbial protein**: the microbes themselves are digested downstream, so a
ruminant can build high-quality protein from low-quality nitrogen, including non-protein nitrogen
like urea. This is why cattle can thrive on grass alone.

> **YOU FEED THE RUMEN MICROBES, NOT THE COW**
>
> Abrupt diet changes cause problems because the microbial population needs **one to three weeks**
> to adapt. Rapid fermentation of starch (too much grain too fast) drops rumen pH, killing
> fiber-digesting microbes and cascading into **ruminal acidosis** — reduced intake, laminitis,
> liver abscesses, potentially death. Prevent by adequate effective fiber, gradual transitions
> (always), and consistent feeding. **Bloat** — stable foam trapping fermentation gas, especially on
> lush legume pastures — can kill within hours.

### Ruminant, monogastric, hindgut fermenter

The distinction decides what you must buy in and what the animal can find for itself.

| Digestive strategy | Animals | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Ruminant (foregut fermenter) | Cattle, sheep, goats | Microbes upgrade low-quality feed and low-quality nitrogen; can thrive on grass alone. |
| Monogastric | Pigs, poultry | Need **higher-quality diets and specific amino acids** because they lack the microbial upgrading step. They compete more directly with humans for feed but convert it more efficiently per unit of gain. |
| Hindgut fermenter | Horses, rabbits | Ferment **after** the small intestine, so they capture less microbial protein. Rabbits recover it by eating their own cecal pellets (**cecotrophy**). |

Acidosis, bloat, milk fever, ketosis and grass tetany are all nutrition-linked diseases and are all
preventable through nutrition management — see §11–§14 → `homestead-livestock-housing-health-and-grazing`.

## §9 Livestock Nutrition

| Nutrient | Key Points |
|---|---|
| Energy | Usually the first limiting nutrient on forage diets. Measured as TDN, ME, or NE. Forage quality falls as plants mature — lignin rises, energy and protein fall. Harvest or graze timing determines quality more than species choice. |
| Protein | For ruminants, the rumen-degradable protein (RDP) / rumen-undegradable protein (RUP) split matters more than total crude protein. RDP feeds the rumen microbes; RUP bypasses to the small intestine. |
| Fiber | NDF (neutral detergent fiber) predicts intake — high NDF means lower intake. ADF (acid detergent fiber) predicts digestibility — high ADF means lower digestibility. Effective fiber is needed for rumen health. |
| Minerals | Calcium:phosphorus ratio matters. Salt is essential. Trace minerals (selenium, copper) have strong regional deficiency patterns. Copper is easily toxic, and sheep are far more sensitive than cattle — **never give sheep cattle mineral mix.** |
| Vitamins | A, D, E are the critical ones for livestock. B vitamins are synthesized in the rumen of mature ruminants. |
| Water | The most important and most neglected nutrient. Clean, accessible, adequate quantity. **Animals will not eat if they cannot drink.** |

> **TEST YOUR FORAGE**
>
> Hay from the same field in different years can differ by a wide margin in energy and protein.
> Feeding "hay" as if it were a constant is how animals lose condition through winter. Get a forage
> analysis (**$20-40**) and formulate rations based on actual values, not assumptions.

### Body condition scoring

Body condition scoring is the practical, free management tool.

- Condition at calving or lambing predicts rebreeding success **better than almost anything else you
  can measure**.
- Thin females do not cycle, and the interval from calving to first estrus lengthens sharply with
  poor body condition.
- Score your animals regularly and adjust feeding accordingly.

Nutrition therefore drives reproduction, the largest economic lever in a livestock operation —
§11–§14 → `homestead-livestock-housing-health-and-grazing`. Forage quality is set by grazing and
harvest timing, which is a grazing-management decision in the same sibling skill. Definitions of
**Body condition score (BCS)** is the practical, free monitoring tool that turns all of the above
into a weekly decision: a subjective score of an animal's fat cover, **typically on a 1–5 or a 1–9
scale** depending on the system in use. Condition at calving or lambing predicts rebreeding success,
which makes it a forward indicator of next season's calf or lamb crop rather than a record of last
season's feeding.

BCS, NDF/ADF, VFAs and the rumen: §23–§24 → `homestead-reference`.

## §10 Species Selection

**Match the animal to the environment.** The highest-producing genetics in a resource-limited
environment underperform and break down — this is one of the most common expensive mistakes in
livestock.

| Species | Gestation | Estrous Cycle | Key Characteristics |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cattle (beef) | ~283 days | ~21 days | Ruminant. Convert grass to meat. Low labor per unit. Calving ease and temperament are heritable and economically important. |
| Cattle (dairy) | ~283 days | ~21 days | High-producing animals need high-quality feed and intensive management. Mastitis, metabolic disease (milk fever, ketosis) are major risks. |
| Sheep | ~150 days | ~17 days (seasonal) | Ruminant. Short-day seasonal breeders (fall breeding, spring lambing). Multiple births common. Parasite susceptibility (barber pole worm). Copper-sensitive. |
| Goats | ~150 days | ~21 days (seasonal) | Ruminant. Browser more than grazer. Hardy and opportunistic. Escape artists — fencing must be excellent. Copper-tolerant (unlike sheep). |
| Pigs | ~114 days | ~21 days | Monogastric. Efficient converters. Need higher-quality diet than ruminants. Rooting behavior destroys pasture if not managed. Strong, smart, need sturdy fencing. |
| Poultry (chickens) | ~21 days (incubation) | N/A (eggs) | Monogastric. Fast generation time. Layers vs broilers have different management. Needs secure housing against predators. |
| Horses | ~340 days | ~21 days (seasonal) | Hindgut fermenter. Needs consistent forage-based diet. Dental care, hoof care, and colic prevention are major management concerns. |

### Reading the table

- **Ruminants** (beef cattle, dairy cattle, sheep, goats) carry the microbial upgrading step of §8,
  so grass and crop residue are feed rather than waste.
- **Monogastrics** (pigs, chickens) do not, so they need a higher-quality diet, specific amino acids,
  and bought-in feed that competes with human food — in exchange for better conversion per unit of
  gain.
- **Hindgut fermenters** (horses) ferment after the small intestine and capture less microbial
  protein, so a consistent forage base is not optional.
- Copper splits the two small ruminants: **sheep are copper-sensitive, goats are copper-tolerant.**
  This is a feeding and mineral-mix decision, not a detail (§9).
- Fencing difficulty is a real selection criterion: goats require the best fencing, pigs the
  strongest. Housing and fencing specifics: §11–§14 → `homestead-livestock-housing-health-and-grazing`.
- Seasonality constrains your calendar: sheep and goats are seasonal breeders, horses seasonal;
  cattle and pigs cycle ~21 days year-round.

### Selection heuristics that follow

1. Decide what feed the site actually produces before deciding what species to buy. A ruminant fits a
   forage-producing site; a monogastric commits you to purchased feed.
2. Do not import the highest-producing genetics into a resource-limited environment.
3. Price the infrastructure the species forces on you — goat and pig fencing, poultry predator
   protection, dairy management intensity — alongside the animal.
4. Check the mineral program against the species before the first bag is bought (sheep and copper).
